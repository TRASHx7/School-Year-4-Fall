Part 1 · Travelling waves (Lecture 2a)

01Two kinds of wave

A wave moves a shape (and energy) along. The material it travels through stays put on average: each little piece just wiggles about its own resting spot. What sorts waves into two kinds is the direction of that wiggle compared with the direction the wave travels.

  • Transverse. Shake one end of a string up and down. The wave runs along the string in x, toward the wall, but each particle of string moves up and down in y, at right angles to the wave. These are also called travelling waves.
  • Longitudinal. Push a piston back and forth in a cylinder of air. Bunches of squeezed air (compressions) travel down the tube, and each air particle moves back and forth parallel to the wave, horizontally.
transverse: particle motion ⟂ v⃗   ·   longitudinal: particle motion ∥ v⃗
Analogy · a stadium wave and a line of commuters

In a stadium wave, each fan just stands up and sits down, yet the "wave" races around the stadium. That's transverse. Now picture a queue where someone at the back gets bumped: each person stumbles forward and steps back, and the bump travels to the front. That's longitudinal. Nobody in either crowd leaves their seat or spot in the queue.

Try it: watch one particle (the dark dot)
Tricky bits
  • The wave moves; the medium doesn't. A piece of string near your hand never reaches the wall. If a question asks how far a particle travels in a period, it's 4ym of up-and-down motion, not one wavelength along x.
  • "Perpendicular" or "parallel" compares two directions: the particle's motion and the wave's motion. Don't compare with the string or the tube itself.
  • The rest of this lecture uses the string (transverse) picture, but the maths in §2 to §5 works the same for sound: y just becomes a sideways displacement along x.

02The harmonic wave function

One function tells you the displacement of every piece of string at every moment. Plug in a position x and a time t, and out comes how far that piece sits above or below the resting line.

y(x, t) = ym sin(kx − ωt + φ) k = 2πλ angular wave number, rad/m ω = 2πT = 2πf angular frequency, rad/s; T = 1/f
SymbolNameWhat it tells you
ymamplitudeThe biggest displacement. The string swings between +ym and −ym.
kangular wave numberHow fast the phase changes as you walk along x. One wavelength λ is 2π radians of phase.
ωangular frequencyHow fast the phase changes as time passes at a fixed spot. One period T is 2π radians.
φphase angle (phase constant)Where the wave starts at x = 0, t = 0 (§3).

Freeze time and you get a snapshot: a sine curve in x that repeats every λ. Stand at one spot and watch: that piece of string does simple harmonic motion in y, repeating every T, perpendicular to the wave's motion.

Analogy · k is ω for space

The lecture boxes k = 2π/λ and says it's "analogous to" ω = 2π/T. Think of a clock hand that does a full turn (2π) per repeat. ω counts how many radians it turns per second; k counts how many radians it turns per metre as you walk along the string.

Try it: the wave explorer
snapshot y(x, t)one particle at x = 0.25 m
Tricky bits
  • k is not a constant. The lecture underlines this. It's set by the wave (its λ), not a universal number like a spring constant. Don't confuse it with the k in Hooke's law.
  • Read k and ω straight off the brackets. In 2 sin(4x − 2t), k = 4 and ω = 2. Then λ = 2π/k and T = 2π/ω. A common slip is to treat the 4 as λ.
  • ω vs f. ω is in rad/s, f in Hz (cycles/s). They differ by 2π. If your answer is off by about 6.28, this is why.

03The phase constant φ

φ slides the whole wave sideways. It sets the displacement at the origin when the clock starts. Put t = 0 and x = 0 into the wave function and only φ is left inside the sine:

y(0, 0) = ym sin φ
Lecture example: φ = 0 and φ = π/6
Case I: set φ = 0
at t = 0: y = y_m sin(kx + 0) = y_m sin(kx)
∴ at x = 0, y = 0 (curve starts at the origin, going up)
 
Case II: φ = π/6
at t = 0: y = y_m sin(kx + π/6)
at x = 0: y = y_m sin(π/6) = y_m/2
The curve now crosses the y-axis at half its height: a phase shift.
Try it: slide the phase (loads with the lecture's φ = π/6)
φ = 0your φy(0, 0)
Analogy · starting a race late

Two runners on the same circular track at the same pace, one with a head start. They run identically; the only difference is where each was when the stopwatch started. φ is that head start, measured in radians.

Tricky bits
  • Radians, not degrees. sin(π/6) = 0.5, but your calculator in degree mode gives sin(0.5236°) ≈ 0.009. Check the mode.
  • φ changes nothing but the start. Amplitude, λ, T and speed are all unchanged.
  • Positive φ shifts the curve to the left in a snapshot (the wave is "ahead"). In the lecture's sketch the φ = π/6 curve reaches its peak before the φ = 0 curve does.
  • Many φ give the same y(0, 0): sin(π/6) = sin(5π/6) = 1/2. To pin φ down you also need to know whether the string at x = 0 is moving up or down.

04Wave speed and direction

Take two snapshots, at t1 and t2. The shape is the same, just shifted. Follow one point of the shape (say a particular height y): it sits at x1 in the first frame and x2 in the second. Same height means same phase, and that gives the speed.

Lecture derivation: v = ω/k
Same displacement y₁ = y₂, and let φ = 0:
y_m sin(kx₁ − ωt₁) = y_m sin(kx₂ − ωt₂)
kx₁ − ωt₁ = kx₂ − ωt₂
kx₁ − kx₂ = ωt₁ − ωt₂
k Δx = ω Δt
∴ Δx/Δt = ω/k = v
 
Another way to express it:
v = ω/k = (2π/T)/(2π/λ) = λ/T = λf  since T = 1/f
v = ωk = λT = λf y = ym sin(kx ∓ ωt + φ) (−) travels toward +x · (+) travels toward −x
Analogy · surfing one crest

Imagine riding one crest like a surfer. To stay on it, the stuff inside the sine has to stay constant. If time grows, ωt grows, so kx must grow too: you move toward +x. In one period you travel one wavelength, so your speed is λ/T.

Try it: the crest-rider

Worked example · Practice Problem 1 (2a): radio waves from a spacecraft

Radio waves from a spacecraft have wavelength λ = 0.15 m. What is the frequency? Hint: all electromagnetic waves travel at the speed of light.

Solution, as in the lecture
v = c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s,  λ = 0.15 m
since v = ω/k = λ/T = λf
∴ c = λf → f = c/λ
f = (3 × 10⁸ m/s)/(0.15 m) = 2 × 10⁹ Hz = 2000 MHz (2 GHz)
Calculator: v = λf (loads with Practice Problem 1)
Tricky bits
  • The sign is backwards from what you'd guess. kx minus ωt travels in the +x direction.
  • Speed only needs the numbers in front of x and t. For 2 sin(4x − 2t), v = 2/4 = 0.5. The amplitude 2 has nothing to do with it.
  • Frequency is set by the source; speed by the medium. Change the medium and λ changes, not f.
  • MHz is 10⁶ Hz. 2 × 10⁹ Hz is 2000 MHz, not 2 MHz.

05The wave equation

Differentiate y twice in space and twice in time. Both times the sine comes back (with a minus sign), so both second derivatives are just multiples of y itself. Comparing them gives a single equation that any travelling wave obeys.

Lecture derivation, step by step
y = y_m sin(kx − ωt + φ)
 
With respect to x:
∂y/∂x = k y_m cos(kx − ωt + φ)
∂²y/∂x² = −k² y_m sin(kx − ωt + φ) = −k² y
 
Repeat with t:
∂y/∂t = −ω y_m cos(kx − ωt + φ)
∂²y/∂t² = −ω² y_m sin(kx − ωt + φ) = −ω² y
 
Solve each for y:
for x: y = −(1/k²) ∂²y/∂x²   |   for t: y = −(1/ω²) ∂²y/∂t²
therefore ∂²y/∂t² = (ω²/k²) ∂²y/∂x²
and since v = ω/k:  ∂²y/∂t² = v² ∂²y/∂x²
∂²y∂t² = v² ∂²y∂x² the wave equation
Analogy · bendier string, faster acceleration

∂²y/∂x² is how sharply the string is curved at a spot. ∂²y/∂t² is how fast that spot is accelerating. The wave equation says: the more a spot is bent, the harder it gets pulled back, like a bent ruler snapping straight. v² is the exchange rate between the two.

Calculator: check the wave equation at any point (loads with y₁ = 2 sin(4x − 2t))
Tricky bits
  • Partial derivatives. ∂/∂x holds t fixed; ∂/∂t holds x fixed. The chain rule pulls out a factor of k for x and −ω for t.
  • Direction drops out. Waves going either way satisfy it, because ±ω gets squared.
  • It's v², not v. If an equation reads ∂²y/∂t² = 9 ∂²y/∂x², the speed is 3, not 9.
  • Any wave with ω/k = v solves it, whatever its amplitude or phase.

Worked example · Practice Problem 2 (2a)

y₁ = 2 sin(4x − 2t),  y₂ = sin(3x − 4t),  y₃ = 2 sin(3x − 3t). (a) Rank the wave speeds u. (b) Rank the maximum speeds perpendicular to the wave's motion, v.

Solution, as in the lecture
(a) u = ω/k
u₁ = 2/4 = 0.5  ;  u₂ = 4/3 = 1.333  ;  u₃ = 3/3 = 1
∴ u₁ < u₃ < u₂

v₁ = 2 d/dt[sin(4x − 2t)] = 2(−2) cos(4x − 2t) = −4 cos(4x − 2t)
v₂ = d/dt[sin(3x − 4t)] = −4 cos(3x − 4t)
v₃ = d/dt[2 sin(3x − 3t)] = −6 cos(3x − 3t)
∴ velocity amplitudes: |v₁,m| = 4 ; |v₂,m| = 4 ; |v₃,m| = 6
∴ v₁,m = v₂,m < v₃,m

Analogy · the stadium again

How fast the stadium wave goes round (wave speed) is a different question from how fast each fan jumps up (particle speed). A lazy crowd can make a fast wave if each person stands up just after their neighbour, and an energetic crowd can make a slow one.

Tricky bits
  • Amplitude affects particle speed, not wave speed. y₁ and y₃ both have amplitude 2, but different u. y₁ and y₂ have different amplitudes but the same |vm| = 4, because ω makes up for it.
  • Particle speed is fastest at y = 0 (crossing the middle) and zero at the crests, just like a mass on a spring.
  • Particle speed can be bigger than the wave speed. Wave 3: |vm| = 6 but u = 1. Nothing breaks.
  • Watch the symbols: in this problem u is the wave speed and v the particle speed, the opposite of §4's v = ω/k. Read which one the question means.

Part 2 · Phasors (Lecture 2b)

07What a phasor is

A phasor is a complex (or analytic) way to write a wave. It turns a wave into an arrow (a vector) with a length and an angle. That makes adding waves (§8) a lot easier than juggling trig identities.

Start from the wave equation. One solution is a sine. Another, the "analytic" one, pairs a cosine with an i·sine:

Lecture build-up: from sin and cos to eiθ
y(x,t) = y₁ + i y₂,  where
y₁ = y_m cos(kx + ωt + φ),  y₂ = y_m sin(kx + ωt + φ)
y = y_m [cos(kx + ωt + φ) + i sin(kx + ωt + φ)]
Euler's identity: e^{iθ} = cos θ + i sin θ
y(x,t) = y_m e^{i(kx + ωt + φ)}
       = y_m e^{ikx} · e^{i(ωt + φ)}
       = x_m e^{i(ωt + φ)} = x̃(t)  ← the phasor (time dependent); x_m = y_m e^{ikx}
 
Re(x̃(t)) = x_m cos(ωt + φ)  ← the real displacement
x̃(t) = x_m e^{iφ} e^{iωt} = x̃_m e^{iωt},  where x̃_m = x_m e^{iφ}
eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ x̃(t) = x̃m eiωt,   x̃m = xm eiφ complex amplitude: length xm, angle φ x(t) = Re[x̃(t)] = xm cos(ωt + φ)
Analogy · a shadow of a spinning arrow

Picture an arrow of length xm spinning counter-clockwise at ω rad/s, starting at angle φ. Shine a light from above: its shadow on the horizontal (real) axis slides back and forth as xm cos(ωt + φ). The wave is the shadow; the phasor is the arrow. Since every arrow in a problem spins at the same ω, you can freeze them all at t = 0 and just work with x̃m.

Try it: the spinning phasor and its shadow
phasor x̃(t)Re x̃ = xm cos(ωt + φ)
Tricky bits
  • The physical wave is the real part. The imaginary part is a bookkeeping helper. When you're done, take Re[ ] to get back a cosine.
  • Phasors are cosine-based. Re gives cos(ωt + φ). A sine has to be rewritten as a cosine first (§9).
  • Two names, two things. x̃(t) spins (it has eiωt). x̃m is the frozen arrow (just length and angle). Most problems ask for x̃m.
  • Lecture 2b writes kx + ωt while 2a used kx − ωt. Both solve the wave equation; they just travel in opposite directions.

08Adding waves with phasors

Two waves with the same ω on the same string add up to another wave with that same ω. Its amplitude and phase come from adding the two arrows head to tail.

Lecture setup
wave 1: x₁ = x_m1 cos(ωt + φ₁)  →  x̃₁ = x̃_m1 e^{iωt},  x̃_m1 = x_m1 e^{iφ₁}
wave 2: x₂ = x_m2 cos(ωt + φ₂)  →  x̃₂ = x̃_m2 e^{iωt},  x̃_m2 = x_m2 e^{iφ₂}
superposition by summation:
x̃_R = x̃₁ + x̃₂ = (x̃_m1 + x̃_m2) e^{iωt} = x̃_m e^{iωt}
x̃_m = |x̃_m| e^{iφ}  |x̃_m| = amplitude of the sum, φ = its phase constant
horizontal: X = xm1 cos φ1 + xm2 cos φ2  ·  vertical: Y = xm1 sin φ1 + xm2 sin φ2 amplitude = √(X² + Y²)  ·  φR = tan−1(Y/X)

Worked example · Practice Problem 1 (2b)

Two waves on the same string: y₁ = 5.6 m sin(2πx − 400πt) and y₂ = 6.6 m sin(2πx − 400πt + 0.8π rad). Find (i) the amplitude of the resultant and (ii) its phase angle relative to wave 1.

Solution, as in the lecture
Draw the phasors: y_m1 along the axis (φ₁ = 0), y_m2 at φ₂ = 0.8π ≈ 144°,
which is 36° above the negative axis (180° − 144° = 36°).
 
x-axis: y_mx = (y_m1)_x + (y_m2)_x = [5.6 − 6.6 cos 36°] m = 0.261 m
y-axis: y_my = (y_m1)_y + (y_m2)_y = 0 + [6.6 sin 36°] m = 3.88 m
 
y_m = √(y_mx² + y_my²) = √(0.261² + 3.88²) = 3.89 m
φ_R = tan⁻¹(3.88 m / 0.261 m) ≈ 86.15°
Follow-up: a third wave of amplitude 5 m. What φ₃ maximises the resultant?
To maximise the resultant, wave 3 should be in phase with the resultant of the first two:
φ₃ = φ_R  (≈ 86.15°; the arrows then line up, giving 3.89 + 5 = 8.89 m)

Analogy · two people pulling a sled

Two people pull a sled with ropes at different angles. The sled feels one combined pull: add the ropes as vectors. If they pull nearly opposite (like 0° and 144° here), the big forces mostly cancel and the sled gets a small sideways tug. A third person adds the most by pulling exactly along that combined direction.

Tricky bits
  • Amplitudes don't simply add. 5.6 + 6.6 = 12.2 m only if the waves are in phase. Here they're 144° apart and the answer is just 3.89 m.
  • Mind the 36°. The lecture uses the 36° angle to the negative axis, so the x-part is minus 6.6 cos 36°. If you use 144° directly, cos 144° is already negative: 6.6 cos 144° = −5.34. Don't subtract twice.
  • tan⁻¹ quadrant. Your calculator gives an angle between −90° and 90°. If X is negative, add 180°. Here X = +0.261 is positive, so 86.15° is fine.
  • Rounding. With unrounded values the angle is 86.16°; the lecture's 86.15° comes from using 0.261 and 3.88. Either is fine on an exam.
  • Only waves with the same ω (and same k) can be added this way. All here share 2πx − 400πt.

09Writing a signal as a phasor

To turn a real signal into a phasor, get it into the standard form (positive amplitude) × cos(ωt + phase), then read off the length and angle. Two fixes come up all the time:

  • A minus sign out front. −1 = e−iπ, so a negative amplitude is the same as a positive one with π taken off the phase.
  • A sine instead of a cosine. sin θ = cos(θ − π/2), so take π/2 off the phase.
−A cos(ωt + θ) = A cos(ωt + θ − π) e−iπ = −1 A sin(ωt + θ) = A cos(ωt + θ − π/2) cos(θ − π/2) = sin θ

Worked example · Practice Problem 2 (2b)

Find the phasor representation of the current i(t) = −4 cos(ωt + 3π/4).

Solution, as in the lecture
i(t) = −4 cos(ωt + 3π/4)
ĩ(t) = −4 e^{i(ωt + 3π/4)}
since e^{−iπ} = −1, we can simplify further:
ĩ(t) = 4 e^{−iπ} e^{i(ωt + 3π/4)}
     = 4 e^{i(ωt − π/4)}
     = 4 e^{−iπ/4} e^{iωt}
∴ ĩ(t) = ĩ_m e^{iωt}, where ĩ_m = 4 e^{−iπ/4}
Calculator: signal → phasor (loads with Practice Problem 2)
Analogy · turning around instead of walking backwards

"Walk 4 m backwards while facing 135°" puts you in the same place as "walk 4 m forwards facing −45°". A negative amplitude is walking backwards; the phasor convention says always walk forwards, so turn around by 180° (π) instead.

Tricky bits
  • Amplitude stays positive. The answer is 4 e−iπ/4, not −4 ei3π/4. They're equal numbers, but the standard phasor has a positive length.
  • ±π both work. 3π/4 + π = 7π/4 is the same angle as −π/4. Pick the one between −π and π.
  • Sine shift goes the right way: sin is cos minus π/2 (sine lags cosine). Adding π/2 instead is the classic mistake.
  • If both fixes are needed (−A sin), do both: subtract π/2 and add or subtract π.

10Adding currents with phasors

Put it all together: convert each signal to a cosine phasor (§9), add the complex amplitudes (§8), then read the answer back as a cosine.

ĩ(t) = ĩ1(t) + ĩ2(t) = (ĩm1 + ĩm2) eiωt  ⇒  i(t) = |ĩm| cos(ωt + arg ĩm)

Worked example · Practice Problem 3 (2b)

Find the superposition of i₁(t) = 5 sin(ωt + π/4) and i₂(t) = 7 cos(ωt − π/4) using phasors.

Solution, as in the lecture
Convert i₁(t) to a cosine via a phase shift, cos(θ − π/2) = sin θ:
i₁(t) = 5 cos(ωt + π/4 − π/2) = 5 cos(ωt − π/4)
ĩ₁(t) = 5 e^{−iπ/4} e^{iωt}
ĩ₂(t) = 7 e^{−iπ/4} e^{iωt}
ĩ(t) = ĩ₁(t) + ĩ₂(t)
ĩ(t) = 12 e^{−iπ/4} e^{iωt},  ĩ_m = 12 e^{−iπ/4}
Back to a real current: i(t) = 12 cos(ωt − π/4)

Analogy · converting currencies before adding

You can't add 5 dollars and 7 euros as "12". First convert to one currency. Sines and cosines are different currencies; converting i₁ to a cosine is the exchange. After the conversion, both arrows point at −π/4, so here the lengths really do just add: 5 + 7 = 12.

Tricky bits
  • Convert first, add second. Adding 5 eiπ/4 + 7 e−iπ/4 (forgetting the sine shift) gives 8.60, not 12.
  • 12 only works because the phases match. In general you must add components (§8), not lengths.
  • Same ω required. Both currents have the same ω, so eiωt factors out. Different frequencies can't be added as fixed phasors.
  • Give the final answer in the form asked: phasor (12 e−iπ/4) or time signal (12 cos(ωt − π/4)).
