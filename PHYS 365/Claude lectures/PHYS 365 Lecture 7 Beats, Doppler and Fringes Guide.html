PHYS 365 · Lecture 7 · Study guide

When waves meet: standing waves, beats, Doppler shifts and Young's fringes

Every topic in this lecture is the same move: put two waves in the same place and add them. What changes is what is different between the two waves. Opposite directions give standing waves. Slightly different frequencies give beats. A fixed phase difference gives bright and dark fringes. The Doppler effect explains how motion changes the frequency you receive in the first place.

Opposite directionsincident + reflected§1 to §3
→
Different frequenciesf1 ≠ f2 gives beats§4, §5

Part 1 · Reflection and resonance

01A wave hits a boundary

Send a continuous traveling wave toward a boundary (the end of a string, a change of medium, the end of a pipe). Part of it continues through as the transmitted wave. Part of it bounces back as the reflected wave.

Because the incident wave keeps arriving, the reflected wave has to travel back through it. On the incident side there are now two waves in the same place, going in opposite directions, and they add together. That adding is interference, and it's the whole reason the rest of this lecture exists.

incident side: y = yincident + yreflected two waves, interfering far side: y = ytransmitted one wave, no interference
Analogy · a rope tied to a wall

Tie a rope to a wall and keep shaking your end. Each wiggle runs to the wall and bounces back toward you, right through the wiggles you're still sending. Some spots on the rope end up shaking hard, others barely move. That stationary pattern of "shakes a lot" and "barely moves" is what the reflected wave creates.

Tricky bits
  • The lecture's sketch is one frozen moment ("single frame/instance"). In reality all three waves move. What you can measure is the envelope: the biggest swing at each point over time. That's what §2 is built on.
  • Interference only happens on the incident side. The transmitted wave is alone on its side, so it is an ordinary traveling wave.
  • If nothing reflects (r = 0), the envelope is flat: every point swings the same amount. Slide r to 0 above and the dashed lines go straight.

02Standing wave ratio (SWR)

Where the incident and reflected waves arrive in step, their amplitudes add: the swing is ym,i + ym,r (maximum amplitude). Where they arrive opposite, they subtract: ym,i − ym,r (minimum amplitude). The SWR is simply the ratio of the two:

SWR = ym,i + ym,rym,i − ym,r max amplitude over min amplitude SWR = 1 + r1 − r,   r = ym,rym,i amplitude reflection coefficient SWR = 1 + √R1 − √R,   R = r2 power reflection coefficient

Limits. Total reflection: as r → 1, SWR → ∞ (the minimum amplitude is zero, so there are true nodes). No reflection: as r → 0, SWR → 1 (max equals min, no pattern at all). So 1 ≤ SWR < ∞, and a smaller SWR means less reflection.

Worked derivation: from amplitudes to r and R (lecture pages 2 to 4)
1
SWR = ym,i + ym,rym,i − ym,rStart from the definition: max amplitude over min amplitude.
2
SWR = 1 + ym,rym,i1 − ym,rym,iDivide the top and the bottom by ym,i. The value doesn't change.
3
SWR = 1 + r1 − rName the ratio of reflected to incident amplitude r.
4
R = r2  →  r = √R  →  SWR = 1 + √R1 − √RPower goes as amplitude squared, so the power coefficient R is r squared. Substitute r = √R.
5
r → 1: SWR → ∞  ·  r → 0: SWR → 1The two limits from page 3.
Analogy · loud and quiet spots in an echoey room

Play a steady tone toward a wall and walk around. If the wall absorbs everything (no echo), it sounds equally loud everywhere: ratio 1. If the wall is a perfect mirror, some spots go completely silent: the ratio of loudest to quietest is infinite. The SWR is that loudest-to-quietest ratio, so it tells you how strong the echo is without measuring the echo directly.

Tricky bits
  • r or R? r compares amplitudes; R compares powers. If a problem says "25% of the power reflects", that's R = 0.25, so r = 0.5, not 0.25. Using R where r belongs is the most common mistake here.
  • Going backwards (you measure SWR, they ask for r): rearrange SWR = 1 + r1 − r to get r = SWR − 1SWR + 1.
  • SWR has no units and can never be below 1. If you get 0.6, you've flipped the fraction.
  • The lecture's picture draws max and min as full top-to-bottom heights. Using peak-to-peak or peak values gives the same ratio, because the factor of 2 cancels.

03Resonance in an open pipe

A speaker plays a sound at frequency f into a pipe of length L that is open at both ends. The sound reflects off the open ends and travels back and forth. At most frequencies the reflections arrive out of step and mostly cancel. At special frequencies, the normal mode frequencies of the pipe, every reflection lines up with the next push and the amplitude grows to a maximum. That's resonance.

fn = n v2L = n f1,   n = 1, 2, 3, … open pipe harmonics f1 = v2L fundamental; v is the speed of sound

Where the formula comes from. An open end is a place where the air moves freely (a displacement antinode). With an antinode at each end, the pipe must hold a whole number of half wavelengths: L = n λ2, so λ = 2Ln and f = vλ = n v2L.

Analogy · pushing a swing

Push a swing at random moments and it hardly moves; some pushes fight the motion. Push once per swing, in rhythm, and every push adds to the last. The pipe is the swing, the speaker is your hand, and f1, 2f1, 3f1… are the rhythms that work.

Try it: sweep the speaker frequency
Loads with L = 0.50 m and v = 343 m/s (example values), with the speaker on f1. The top plot is the amplitude ym versus f, like the lecture's sketch on page 5.
Jump to:
Tricky bits
  • Notation clash. The lecture writes fn = nf, where that f means the fundamental f1, not the speaker's frequency. Write f1 in your own work to avoid mixing them up.
  • Open at both ends means every n works (1, 2, 3, …). On an exam, check the setup: a pipe closed at one end is a different case (only odd harmonics), and this formula doesn't apply to it.
  • Length up, frequency down. f1 is proportional to 1L. Doubling L halves every resonant frequency.

Part 2 · Beats

04Adding two waves with different frequencies

Two sources play at slightly different frequencies f1 and f2, with the same amplitude. At your ear they add. Sometimes they're in step (loud), sometimes opposite (quiet), and the sound rises and falls in intensity. Those swells are beats.

The lecture looks at one fixed position, so each wave only depends on time:

s1 = sm cos(ω1t),   s2 = sm cos(ω2t) sR = [2sm cos(ω′t)] cos(ωt) ω′ = 12(ω1 − ω2),   ω = 12(ω1 + ω2)

Read the result as a fast wave, cos(ωt), at the average frequency, whose amplitude 2smcos(ω′t) slowly swells and shrinks. The lecture calls that slow part s′R,m, the time-dependent amplitude.

Worked derivation: the resultant wave (lecture page 7)
1
sR = s1 + s2 = sm[cos(ω1t) + cos(ω2t)]Superposition: add the two waves. Same amplitude, so sm factors out.
2
cos α + cos β = 2 cos(α − β2) cos(α + β2)The sum-to-product identity (boxed on page 7). Use α = ω1t and β = ω2t.
3
sR = 2sm cos[12(ω1 − ω2)t] cos[12(ω1 + ω2)t]Substitute. The t factors out of each bracket.
4
sR = [2sm cos(ω′t)] cos(ωt)Name the half-difference ω′ and the half-sum ω. The bracket is s′R,m, the time-dependent amplitude.
Analogy · two people walking side by side

Two friends walk together, one taking slightly quicker steps. Their footsteps fall together, drift apart until they alternate, then drift back together. You hear "in step, out of step, in step" at a slow, steady rhythm. The steps themselves are the fast wave; the drifting in and out of step is the beat.

Try it: two waves and their sum (slowed down so you can see it)
Loads with f1 = 22 Hz and f2 = 18 Hz over a 1 s window, so you should count 4 beats.
s1s2sR = s1 + s2±2smcos(ω′t)
Tricky bits
  • Which cosine is the "amplitude"? The slow one, cos(ω′t), because ω′ is much smaller than ω. It changes so slowly that over a few fast wiggles it looks like a size, not a wave.
  • The identity's order doesn't matter. cos is even, so cos(α − β2) = cos(β − α2). You can subtract either way.
  • Equal amplitudes matter. With equal amplitudes the sound drops to exactly zero between beats. With unequal amplitudes the beats still happen but never reach full silence.

05Beat frequency and beat period

The tone you hear has the average frequency. The loudness swells at the beat frequency.

f = f1 + f22 the pitch you hear f′ = f1 − f22,   T′ = 1f′ frequency and period of cos(ω′t) Tbeat = T′2,   fbeat = 2f′ = f1 − f2 number of beats ÷ time taken

Why the factor of 2? The ear hears loudness, which depends on the size of the swing, not its sign. The envelope 2smcos(ω′t) reaches its biggest size twice per cycle: once at +2sm and once at −2sm. So one full envelope period T′ contains two beats. The lecture's page 8 figure shows this: T′ spans two loud bulges, and the boxed "one beat" is a single bulge.

Worked derivation: beat period and frequency (lecture pages 8 and 9)
1
sR,m(t) = 2sm cos(ω′t)The time-dependent amplitude from §4.
2
ω′ = ω1 − ω22  →  f′ = ω′2π = f1 − f22Divide by 2π to turn angular frequency into ordinary frequency.
3
Tbeat = T′2Two loudness peaks per envelope cycle.
4
fbeat = 1Tbeat = 2f′ = f1 − f2Half the period means double the frequency.
5
f = ω2π = f1 + f22The fast wave inside oscillates at the average frequency (page 8).
Analogy · two clocks ticking

One clock ticks 442 times a minute, another 440. Every so often their ticks land together. How often? As often as the faster clock "gains a tick" on the slower one: 442 − 440 = 2 times a minute. The beat frequency is just the difference in rates.

Calculator: beats from two frequencies
Loads with 442 Hz and 440 Hz (example values: a slightly sharp tuning fork against A440). Press play to hear 2 beats per second.
Tricky bits
  • A slip in the notes. Pages 8 and 9 write T′ = T1 − T22. Periods don't combine that way. The correct statement is in frequencies: f′ = f1 − f22, so T′ = 2f1 − f2. (Try 442 Hz and 440 Hz: the period formula gives about 5 microseconds, but you can clearly hear beats every 0.5 s.) The boxed results, Tbeat = T′2 and fbeat = f1 − f2, are correct. Worth confirming with your instructor.
  • Beat frequency is never negative. Use fbeat = |f1 − f2|.
  • Beats can't tell you which is higher. 4 beats per second against 440 Hz means 436 Hz or 444 Hz. To decide, change one frequency slightly and see whether the beats speed up or slow down.

Part 3 · Doppler effect

06Doppler effect: a detector moving toward a source

The Doppler effect links motion to a change in frequency. The lecture builds it up with the simplest case: the source S sits still (vS = 0) and sends out wavefronts spaced one wavelength λ apart. The detector D runs toward it at speed vD. The wavefronts don't change, but the detector meets them more often, so it measures a higher frequency.

f′ = f [v + vDv] detector moving toward a stationary source v = wave speed · vS = source speed · vD = detector speed (all in m/s)
Worked derivation: counting wavefronts (lecture pages 10 to 12)
1
vtDistance the wavefront travels in time t.
2
vDtDistance the detector moves toward the source in time t.
3
vt + vDtDistance the wavefront travels relative to the detector in time t. They're closing in on each other, so the distances add.
4
vt + vDtλNumber of wavefronts the detector intercepts. Each one takes up a length λ.
5
f′ = vt + vDtλt = v + vDλFrequency is wavefronts per unit time, so divide by t. The t cancels.
6
λ = vf  ∴  f′ = f [v + vDv]The stationary source sets λ. Substitute it in.
Analogy · walking into the rain

Rain falls in a steady sheet blowing toward you. Stand still and a certain number of drops hit you each second. Walk into the rain and you hit more drops per second. The spacing between drops hasn't changed at all; you're just meeting them faster. That's exactly the moving-detector case.

Calculator: the wavefront count, step by step
Loads with f = 500 Hz, v = 343 m/s, vD = 34.3 m/s toward the source and t = 1 s (example values). Change t and watch it cancel.
Positive vD means toward the source. Make it negative to move away.
Tricky bits
  • A moving detector doesn't change λ. Only the source sets the wavelength. The detector changes how fast it meets the crests.
  • Moving away flips the sign: the detector now runs from the wavefronts, so the relative distance is vt − vDt and f′ = f v − vDv.
  • Speeds are measured relative to the air (the medium), not relative to each other.

07The general Doppler equation

Here f is the frequency the detector would hear if both detector and source were stationary (the emitted frequency). Then, with either or both moving along the line joining them:

f′ = f [v ± vDv ± vS]

Sign convention: choose each sign so that f′ goes up for motion toward and down for motion away (between source and detector). Detector toward: + on top. Source toward: − on the bottom (a smaller denominator makes f′ bigger).

Lecture page 14 caseFormula
Source moves toward a still detectorf′ = f vv − vS
Detector moves toward a still sourcef′ = f v + vDv
Both move toward each otherf′ = f v + vDv − vS
Analogy · a duck paddling across a pond

A duck bobbing in place makes evenly spaced circular ripples. When it paddles forward, each new ripple starts a little further ahead, so the ripples bunch up in front of it (short wavelength, high frequency) and spread out behind (long wavelength, low frequency). A moving source squeezes the waves themselves. A moving detector, like the rain walker in §6, just meets unchanged waves faster.

Try it: move the source and the detector
Loads with f = 1000 Hz, v = 343 m/s and the source moving toward a still detector at 34.3 m/s (example values). Wavefronts are drawn slowed down; the presets match the three boxed cases on page 14.
Page 14 cases:
Positive means moving toward the other one; negative means moving away.
Tricky bits
  • Don't memorize sign tables; use the rule. Ask "does this motion bring them closer?" If yes, pick the sign that makes f′ bigger: + on top for the detector, − on the bottom for the source.
  • Source motion and detector motion are not interchangeable. At the same speed, a moving source gives a bigger shift than a moving detector (try both presets at 34.3 m/s: 1111 Hz versus 1100 Hz), because only source motion changes the wavelength.
  • "Chasing" problems: if the source follows a detector that's running away, the source moves toward (− on the bottom) and the detector moves away (− on top): f′ = f v − vDv − vS.
  • The formula breaks as vS → v. The denominator goes to zero: the source keeps up with its own wavefronts (a shock wave or sonic boom).

Part 4 · Coherence and interference

08Coherent waves

Two waves are coherent if (a) they are monochromatic (one single frequency) and (b) there is a constant phase relation between them: in phase, out of phase, or something in between, but it doesn't change over time. Consider two coherent waves:

y1(x,t) = ym,1 cos(kx − ωt + φ1) y2(x,t) = ym,2 cos(kx − ωt + φ2) In phase: φ1 − φ2 = 2nπ  ·  Out of phase: φ1 − φ2 = (2n + 1)π
Worked derivation: the in-phase and out-of-phase conditions (lecture pages 16 and 17)
1
kx − ωt + φ1 = kx − ωt + φ2Case I: in phase means the two phases are equal at every x and t.
2
φ1 − φ2 = 0 + 2nπkx and ωt cancel. Phases that differ by a whole number of turns (2π each) are the same.
3
φ1 − φ2 = 2nπBoxed result: crests line up with crests.
4
φ1 − φ2 = (2n + 1)πCase II: out of phase means half a turn off (an odd multiple of π), so crests line up with troughs.

Why it matters for light (page 18). To get an interference pattern on a screen, the light waves must be coherent: the phase difference must be time-independent. Laser light is coherent, so it makes a steady pattern. Ordinary light from a bulb is a jumble of waves whose phases change randomly and very fast, so no pattern forms.

Analogy · a marching band versus a crowd

A marching band steps in a fixed rhythm, so you can predict which feet land together: that's coherent. A crowd leaving a stadium also takes steps, but everyone's timing is random and keeps changing, so on average nothing lines up: that's incoherent. Interference needs the band, not the crowd.

Try it: set the phase difference
ym,1 = 1. Loads in phase with unequal amplitudes, like the lecture's page 16 sketch.
y1y2y1 + y2
Cases:
Tricky bits
  • "Coherent" doesn't mean "in phase". Out of phase by a fixed amount is still coherent. Coherence is about the phase difference staying constant.
  • n can be any integer, including negatives. 4π and −2π both mean in phase; 3π and −π both mean out of phase.
  • Out of phase only gives complete cancellation if the amplitudes are equal. With ym,1 = 1 and ym,2 = 0.5, the out-of-phase sum is 0.5, not 0.
  • Why monochromatic? Two different frequencies drift in and out of step (that's beats, §4). Their phase difference changes with time, so they can't be coherent.

09Young's double-slit experiment

Coherent light hits a barrier with two narrow slits S1 and S2, a distance d apart. Each slit acts as a new source, and the two waves overlap on a screen a distance D away. To reach a point P at angle θ, one wave travels a little further than the other. When D is much bigger than d, the two paths are nearly parallel and the extra distance is:

ΔL = d sin θ path length difference Bright (constructive, max): d sin θ = mλ Dark (destructive, min): d sin θ = (m + 12)λ m = 0, ±1, ±2, … m = 0 is the central bright fringe

The slits start the waves in phase, so the only thing that can put them out of step at P is the path difference. A whole number of wavelengths extra: they still arrive in step (bright). An extra half wavelength: they arrive opposite (dark).

Worked geometry: why ΔL = d sin θ (lecture Young's page 1)
1
Rays r1 and r2 to a far screen are nearly parallel.D is huge compared with d, so both rays leave the slits at the same angle θ.
2
Drop a perpendicular from one slit onto the other ray.This marks where the two paths become equal in length from there on.
3
The leftover piece (the blue segment) is the extra distance ΔL.It sits in a right triangle with hypotenuse d, and the angle at the slit is also θ.
4
ΔL = d sin θOpposite side = hypotenuse × sin θ.
Analogy · two runners, one finish line

Two runners leave at the same moment from start lines a bit apart and stride at exactly the same rate. Whether their footsteps land together at the finish depends only on how much further one ran. A whole number of strides extra: in step. Half a stride extra: perfectly out of step.

Try it: pick a path difference and see which fringe you land on
The screen labels match the lecture's page 3 drawing: m for bright fringes on the left, (m + ½) for dark fringes on the right.
Jump to:
Tricky bits
  • Dark fringe numbering. The first dark fringe above the center has m = 0 (m + ½ = ½). The second has m = 1 (½ + 1 = 32). So "the 2nd dark fringe" uses m = 1, not m = 2. Below the center, −½ is m = −1 and −32 is m = −2.
  • The center is always bright, whatever λ, d or D: at θ = 0 both paths are equal, ΔL = 0.
  • d sin θ can't exceed d, so m can never be bigger than dλ. That caps how many fringes exist.

10Where the fringes land on the screen

Exams usually give distances on the screen, not angles. Let ym be the distance between the central bright fringe and the mth bright fringe. Geometry gives tan θ = ymD, and for very small θ we can use tan θ ≈ sin θ:

Bright: ym = m D λd  ·  Dark: ym = (m + 12)D λd Δy = ym+1 − ym = D λd spacing between neighbours (bright or dark)
Worked derivation: fringe position and spacing (Young's pages 4 to 6)
①
tan θ = ymDRight triangle from the slits to the screen: opposite ym, adjacent D.
2
d sin θ = mλ,   sin θ ≈ tan θBright-fringe condition, plus the small-angle approximation.
②
d tan θ = mλ  →  tan θ = mλdSwap sin for tan and divide by d.
3
① = ②:  ymD = mλdBoth equal tan θ, so set them equal.
4
ym = mλDdMultiply both sides by D. Boxed result.
5
ym+1 − ym = (m + 1)Dλd − mDλd = DλdNeighbouring bright fringes. The m terms cancel. Same answer for dark fringes.
Analogy · a slide projector

The fringe pattern behaves like a picture from a projector. Move the screen further away (bigger D) and the picture gets bigger. Use a longer wavelength (bigger λ) and the pattern spreads. Move the slits further apart (bigger d) and the pattern squeezes together. That's all Dλd says.

Tricky bits
  • "Δy at m = 0" in the notes doesn't restrict anything: the m cancels, so the spacing Dλd is the same between every neighbouring pair, as long as the angles stay small.
  • Units! λ is usually in nm (×10−9 m) and d in mm (×10−3 m). Convert everything to metres before you divide. A factor-of-1000 error is the most common way to lose marks here.
  • "Distance between the 3rd bright fringes on either side" is 2y3 = 6Δy, not 3Δy.
  • Small angle check: the shortcut is fine when ym is much smaller than D. If a problem has fringes at large angles, go back to d sin θ = mλ and tan θ = ymD separately.

11How bright is each point? Intensity versus phase difference

Between the brightest and darkest points the brightness changes smoothly. The lecture gives the intensity in terms of the phase difference Δφ between the two waves arriving at a point:

I = 4I0 cos2(Δφ2) I0 = intensity on the screen when one slit is covered (no interference) Linking to path difference: Δφ = 2πλΔL one wavelength of path = 2π of phase
Worked check: bright and dark fringes (Young's page 8)
1
Bright: Δφ = 2mπ  →  I = 4I0 cos2(mπ) = 4I0cos(mπ) is ±1, and squaring gives 1.
2
Dark: Δφ = (2m + 1)π  →  I = 4I0 cos2[(m + 12)π] = 0The cosine of an odd multiple of 90° is zero.
3
One source: I = I0 everywhereThe flat purple line on the lecture's graph. No second wave, no interference.
Analogy · sweeping sand into piles

Spread sand evenly over a floor: that's one slit, I0 everywhere. Add a second slit's worth (2I0 total on average) and then sweep it into rows: piles twice as high as the average (4I0), bare floor in between (0). Interference doesn't create or destroy light. It moves it from the dark fringes into the bright ones.

Try it: intensity at a chosen phase difference
Loads at Δφ = 0, which should give the lecture's 4I0. Set I0 to get numbers in real units.
2 sources: 4I0cos²(½Δφ)one source: I0average 2I0
Jump to:
Tricky bits
  • Why 4I0 and not 2I0? Amplitudes add, and intensity goes as amplitude squared. Double the amplitude gives four times the intensity. The average over the pattern is still 2I0, so energy is conserved.
  • Halve the angle. The formula uses Δφ2 inside the cosine. Forgetting the ½ is the classic slip: at Δφ = π you'd get 4I0 instead of 0.
  • Calculator mode. Δφ is in radians. In degrees, Δφ = π becomes 180°, so the argument is 90°.
