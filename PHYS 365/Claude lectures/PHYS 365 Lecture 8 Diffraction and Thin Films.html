Diffraction and Thin Films Guide

Light that squeezes through a narrow slit spreads out and paints bright and dark bands on a screen. Light that bounces off the top and bottom of a soap film splits into two rays that rejoin and make colors. Both effects come from the same rule you already know from sound: add waves that took different paths, and the path difference decides whether they reinforce or cancel. This guide covers Dr. Andrew's Lecture 8a (Huygens' principle and single-slit diffraction) and Lecture 8b (thin film interference plus two revision problems on beats and two-speaker interference).

StartBig picture

In Lecture 7 two separate sources (two slits, two speakers) sent waves to the same point, and the path difference between them decided whether you got a bright or a dark spot. Lecture 8 asks two new versions of the same question.

Lecture 8a: what if the "sources" are every point across a single opening? Huygens' principle says each point of a wavefront acts as its own little source, so one slit already contains infinitely many sources. Adding all of them gives the single-slit diffraction pattern: a wide bright center, dark fringes at \(a\sin\theta = m\lambda\), and dimmer side bands described by \(I(\theta) = I_m\left[\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\right]^2\).

Lecture 8b: what if the two waves are made by one beam splitting at the top and bottom of a thin film? The extra trip through the film gives an optical path difference \(2n_2 d\cos\theta_2\), and a possible half-cycle flip on reflection decides which thicknesses look bright and which look dark. The lecture ends with two revision problems from earlier material: a beat frequency and a two-speaker interference minimum.

Part 1 · Diffraction (Lecture 8a)

Section 1Huygens' principle

To predict where a wave will be a moment from now, you do not need to know where it came from. Huygens' principle (also called the Huygens-Fresnel principle) gives a recipe that works from the current wavefront alone:

  • Every point on a wavefront acts as the source of a small spherical wavelet.
  • The wavelets spread out at the wave speed and interfere with each other.
  • Where they add up, they form the new wavefront: the surface that touches the front edge of all the wavelets (their common tangent, or envelope).

Dr. Andrew draws two cases. For a plane wave, the sources sit on a straight line, all the wavelets have the same radius, and their common front edge is another straight line, parallel to the first and further along. For a spherical wave, the sources sit on a circle, and the envelope of the wavelets is a bigger circle with the same center.

Why this matters for the rest of the lecture: when a wavefront hits a slit, only the points inside the opening keep sending wavelets. Those wavelets spread into the region behind the barrier and interfere, which is exactly what diffraction is.

\[ r = v\,\Delta t \]
radius of each wavelet after a time \(\Delta t\)
\(r\) = wavelet radius, in metres (m) · \(v\) = wave speed in the medium, in metres per second (m/s) · \(\Delta t\) = elapsed time, in seconds (s)
\[ \text{plane: front moves forward by } v\,\Delta t \qquad \text{spherical: } R = R_0 + v\,\Delta t \]
where the new wavefront ends up
\(R_0\) = radius of the initial spherical wavefront, in metres (m) · \(R\) = radius of the new wavefront, in metres (m)
Filled in

The lecture states the principle in words and pictures only. The two equations above are added so you can put numbers to the pictures; they follow directly from "each wavelet moves outward at the wave speed".

Example 1 (lecture): building the new wavefront, plane and spherical

Plane wave (Lecture 8a page 1). Put point sources along the initial flat wavefront. Each one sends out a semicircular wavelet toward the direction of travel. Because all wavelets started at the same time and travel at the same speed, every wavelet has the same radius \(r = v\,\Delta t\). The line that touches the top of all of them is flat and sits a distance \(r\) above the initial wavefront: that is the wavefront at the later time.

Spherical wave (Lecture 8a page 2). Put point sources around the initial circular wavefront. Each wavelet again has radius \(r\). The outer envelope of all of them is a circle of radius

\[ R = R_0 + r = R_0 + v\,\Delta t \]

with the same center as the original circle. The wave keeps its spherical shape and simply grows.

New wavefront = envelope of wavelets of radius \(v\,\Delta t\)
Analogy

Picture a line of marching band members, each told "take one step forward in any direction you like, but exactly one step". If everyone steps straight ahead, the new front row is a straight line one step further on. The front row is set by the furthest anyone can reach, which is what the envelope of wavelets is. Where it breaks down: real wavelets are not people choosing a direction; they spread in every direction at once, and the backward parts cancel out (that is the "Fresnel" refinement).

Tricky bits
  • The new wavefront is the envelope (outer common tangent) of the wavelets, not the circles themselves and not their centers.
  • The wavelet radius is \(v\,\Delta t\), not the diameter. When a problem says "how far has the wavefront moved", the answer is one radius.
  • Convert time units before multiplying: \(343\ \text{m/s} \times 2.0\ \text{ms} = 343 \times 0.0020 = 0.686\ \text{m}\), not 686 m.
  • Huygens' principle assumes a uniform (homogeneous) medium so all wavelets have the same speed. If the speed changes, the wavelets have different radii and the wavefront bends (that is refraction, Section 4).

Section 2Single-slit dark fringes

Shine light through a single narrow slit onto a screen. Geometric optics (light as straight rays) predicts one bright band on the screen, the same shape as the slit, with sharp shadows around it. What actually appears, measured empirically, is a set of bright and dark fringes: a wide bright band in the middle with dimmer bands on both sides, separated by dark gaps. This spreading and banding is diffraction.

The reason is Huygens' principle. Every point across the slit opening of width \(a\) is a source of wavelets. To reach a point on a far screen at angle \(\theta\) from the center line, wavelets from different parts of the slit travel slightly different distances. At some angles those differences make them cancel in pairs, and you get a dark fringe.

The pairing argument (Lecture 8a page 4)

Split the slit into two halves. Pair each ray in the top half with the ray exactly \(\frac{a}{2}\) below it in the bottom half. Toward angle \(\theta\), the lower ray of each pair travels further by

\[ \Delta L_2 = \frac{a}{2}\sin\theta \]

If that extra distance is half a wavelength, every pair arrives exactly out of phase and cancels, so the whole slit gives zero light at that angle. Splitting into four quarters (pairs \(\frac{a}{4}\) apart) gives the next dark angle, and so on.

\[ a\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad m = \pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \ldots \]
angles of the dark fringes (minima) in single-slit diffraction
\(a\) = slit width, in metres (m) · \(\theta\) = angle from the center line to the point on the screen, in degrees or radians · \(m\) = order of the dark fringe (integer, no unit): \(+\) above the central bright band, \(-\) below it · \(\lambda\) = wavelength of the light, in metres (m)
Example 2 (lecture): first and second minima from path differences
Slit width \(a\), wavelength \(\lambda\), rays toward a far screen at angle \(\theta\) (Lecture 8a page 4).

First minimum. Pair rays \(\frac{a}{2}\) apart (top of slit with middle, and so on). Destructive interference needs a half-wavelength path difference:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta L_2 &= \frac{a}{2}\sin\theta = \frac{\lambda}{2}\\ a\sin\theta &= \lambda \end{aligned}\]

Second minimum. Split the slit into quarters and pair rays \(\frac{a}{4}\) apart:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta L_1 &= \frac{a}{4}\sin\theta = \frac{\lambda}{2}\\ a\sin\theta &= 2\lambda \end{aligned}\]

All minima. Continuing the pattern (sixths, eighths, ...) gives every dark fringe:

\[ a\sin\theta = m\lambda \quad \text{[dark fringes]} \]

On the screen (Lecture 8a page 5) the dark fringes are labelled \(m = +1, +2, +3\) going up from the central bright band and \(m = -1, -2, -3\) going down, so \(m = \pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \ldots\)

\(a\sin\theta = m\lambda\), \(m = \pm 1, \pm 2, \ldots\)
Filled in

Two assumptions the lecture uses without stating: (1) the screen is far away compared with \(a\), so rays heading to the same point are nearly parallel and the extra distance is exactly \(\frac{a}{2}\sin\theta\); (2) \(m = 0\) is left out because at \(\theta = 0\) every ray travels the same distance and they all add. That is the center of the bright central band, not a dark fringe.

Analogy

Imagine a choir standing in one row, all singing the same note, and you stand off to one side. If the row is long enough that the singer at one end is exactly half a wavelength further from you than the singer in the middle, then every singer in the left half has a partner in the right half who is half a wavelength further away, and the partners cancel. The row goes quiet for you even though everyone is singing. Where it breaks down: real singers are not perfectly in step, while the points across a slit are, because one wavefront feeds them all.

Tricky bits
  • Single slit vs double slit, opposite meanings. In Young's double slit (Lecture 7), \(d\sin\theta = m\lambda\) gives bright fringes. In a single slit, \(a\sin\theta = m\lambda\) gives dark fringes. Same shape of equation, opposite result.
  • \(m = 0\) is not allowed for single-slit minima. The center is the brightest point.
  • A narrower slit gives a wider pattern: \(\sin\theta = \frac{m\lambda}{a}\) grows when \(a\) shrinks.
  • Number of dark fringes on each side is the largest whole \(m\) with \(\frac{m\lambda}{a} \le 1\), since \(\sin\theta\) cannot exceed 1.
  • Keep \(a\) and \(\lambda\) in the same unit (µm with µm, or m with m) and set your calculator to degrees if you want \(\theta\) in degrees.

Section 3Single-slit intensity

The dark-fringe rule tells you where the light is zero. To know how bright every other point is, you add up the wavelets from the whole slit with their phases. Dr. Andrew states the result. The brightest point is the center (\(\theta = 0\)), with intensity \(I_m\). Moving away from the center, the intensity drops to zero at the first dark fringe, rises to a small side peak, drops to zero again, and so on, with each side peak dimmer than the last.

Everything is controlled by one number, \(\alpha\), which is half the phase difference between the rays from the top and bottom edges of the slit. Those two edge rays differ in path by \(a\sin\theta\), and the phase-path rule turns that into a phase.

\[ I(\theta) = I_m\left[\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\right]^2 \]
intensity of the diffraction pattern at any angle \(\theta\)
\(I(\theta)\) = intensity at angle \(\theta\), in watts per square metre (W/m²) · \(I_m\) = maximum intensity, at the center of the pattern, in W/m² · \(\alpha\) = half the phase difference between the top and bottom rays, in radians (rad)
\[ \alpha = \frac{\Delta\phi}{2} = \frac{\pi a}{\lambda}\sin\theta \]
what \(\alpha\) is
\(\Delta\phi\) = phase difference between the rays from the top and bottom of the slit, in radians (rad) · \(a\), \(\lambda\), \(\theta\) as in Section 2
\[ [\text{phase difference}] = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,[\text{path length difference}] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,a\sin\theta \]
the note on Lecture 8a page 7, applied to the two edge rays
Example 3 (lecture): intensity at the minima and at the maxima
Use \(I(\theta) = I_m\left[\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\right]^2\) with \(\alpha = \frac{\pi a}{\lambda}\sin\theta\) (Lecture 8a pages 6 and 7).

Minima (dark fringes). Put in \(a\sin\theta = m\lambda\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha &= \frac{\pi}{\lambda}(m\lambda) = m\pi\\ I(\theta) &= I_m\left[\frac{\sin m\pi}{m\pi}\right]^2 = 0 \end{aligned}\]

since \(\sin m\pi = 0\) for every whole number \(m\). This confirms the dark-fringe rule from Section 2.

Maxima (bright fringes). The side peaks sit roughly halfway between dark fringes:

\[ \begin{aligned} a\sin\theta &= \left[m+\tfrac{1}{2}\right]\lambda\\ \alpha &= \left(m+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\pi\\ I(\theta) &= I_m\left[\frac{\sin\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi}{\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi}\right]^2 = \frac{I_m}{\left[\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi\right]^2} \end{aligned}\]

because \(\sin\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi = \pm 1\). Putting in numbers (Filled in):

\[ \begin{aligned} m = 1:&\quad I = \frac{I_m}{(1.5\pi)^2} = 0.0450\,I_m\\ m = 2:&\quad I = \frac{I_m}{(2.5\pi)^2} = 0.0162\,I_m\\ m = 3:&\quad I = \frac{I_m}{(3.5\pi)^2} = 0.00827\,I_m \end{aligned}\]
Side peaks: about 4.5%, 1.6% and 0.83% of \(I_m\)
Filled in

Three details the lecture leaves out. (1) At the center, \(\alpha = 0\) and \(\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha} \to 1\), so \(I(0) = I_m\): the central band is the main maximum, and the bright-fringe rule \(\left[m+\frac{1}{2}\right]\lambda\) is for the side peaks, \(m = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\) (use the mirror-image angles below the center). Plugging \(m = 0\) would give \(a\sin\theta = \frac{\lambda}{2}\), which is partway down the central band, not a peak. (2) The rule \(\alpha = \left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi\) is an approximation: the exact side peaks are at \(\alpha = 1.430\pi\) and \(2.459\pi\), with intensities \(0.0472\,I_m\) and \(0.0165\,I_m\), within a few percent of the lecture's values. (3) On the handwritten page the expression for \(\alpha\) has a correction typed over it; the intended form is \(\alpha = \frac{\pi a}{\lambda}\sin\theta\), which is what the guide uses.

Analogy

Think of a crowd doing a stadium wave, each person slightly out of step with the next. Viewed from straight ahead (\(\theta = 0\)), everyone is in step and the effect is huge. Viewed from an angle, the steps spread around a full cycle and mostly cancel; if they spread over exactly one full cycle (\(\Delta\phi = 2\pi\)), they cancel completely. The \(\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\) factor measures how much survives. Where it breaks down: in a crowd you see people, not a sum of waves, so there is nothing to cancel.

Tricky bits
  • \(\alpha\) is half the phase difference: \(\alpha = \frac{\Delta\phi}{2}\). At the first dark fringe \(\Delta\phi = 2\pi\) but \(\alpha = \pi\).
  • \(\alpha\) is in radians. When you compute \(\sin\alpha\), put the calculator in radian mode, even if \(\theta\) was in degrees.
  • Do not forget the square. \(\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\) is the amplitude ratio; the intensity ratio is its square.
  • The side peaks are weak: the first one is only about 4.5% of the center, which is why most of the light lands in the central band.
Practice 3.1 (not from the lecture): intensity at a given angle
\(a = 100\ \text{µm}\), \(\lambda = 600\ \text{nm}\), \(\theta = 0.20^\circ\). Find \(\frac{I}{I_m}\).
\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha &= \frac{\pi a}{\lambda}\sin\theta = \frac{\pi(100\times10^{-6}\ \text{m})}{600\times10^{-9}\ \text{m}}\sin(0.20^\circ)\\ &= (523.6)(0.003491) = 1.828\ \text{rad}\\ \frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha} &= \frac{\sin(1.828\ \text{rad})}{1.828} = \frac{0.9671}{1.828} = 0.529\\ \frac{I}{I_m} &= (0.529)^2 = 0.280 \end{aligned}\]
\(I \approx 0.280\,I_m\) (28% of the center)
Practice 3.2 (not from the lecture): brightness of the second side peak
The center of a single-slit pattern has \(I_m = 5.0\ \text{W/m}^2\). Estimate the intensity of the \(m = 2\) side peak using the lecture's maxima rule.
\[ \begin{aligned} I &= \frac{I_m}{\left[\left(2+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi\right]^2} = \frac{5.0\ \text{W/m}^2}{(2.5\pi)^2}\\ &= \frac{5.0\ \text{W/m}^2}{61.69} = 0.081\ \text{W/m}^2 \end{aligned}\]
\(I \approx 0.081\ \text{W/m}^2\)
Practice 3.3 (not from the lecture): phase across the slit at the first dark fringe
At the first dark fringe, what is the phase difference \(\Delta\phi\) between the top and bottom rays, and what is \(\alpha\)?
\[ \begin{aligned} a\sin\theta &= \lambda \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\lambda) = 2\pi\ \text{rad}\\ \alpha &= \frac{\Delta\phi}{2} = \pi\ \text{rad} \end{aligned}\]
\(\Delta\phi = 2\pi\ \text{rad}\), \(\alpha = \pi\ \text{rad}\)

Part 2 · Thin film interference (Lecture 8b)

Section 4Thin films and Snell's law

The bright colors on certain surfaces come from thin film interference. Dr. Andrew's examples are the sunlit surface of a soap bubble and the colorful patterns when light reflects off a compact disk. A thin layer (soap, oil on water) reflects light twice: once at its upper boundary and once at its lower boundary. The two reflected waves overlap and interfere:

  • Constructive interference enhances the reflected light (that color looks bright).
  • Destructive interference reduces the reflected light (that color is missing).

The lecture's setup (Lecture 8b pages 2 and 4) is a layer of oil, thickness \(d\) and refractive index \(n_2\), with air (\(n_1\)) above and water below. Incident light hits the top surface at point A:

  • Ray 1 reflects straight off the top surface at A.
  • Ray 2 refracts into the oil at A, reflects off the bottom surface at B, and refracts back out into the air at C, leaving parallel to ray 1.

Point D is on ray 1, level with C along the outgoing wavefront (CD is perpendicular to the rays). From the line CD onward the two rays travel side by side, so all of their path difference is built up before that line. The lecture also assumes each layer is homogeneous (same index everywhere inside it).

To follow ray 2 into the film you need Snell's law, which links the angles on either side of a boundary (Lecture 8b page 3). The angles are measured from the normal line (the line perpendicular to the surface).

\[ \frac{n_1}{n_2} = \frac{\sin\theta_2}{\sin\theta_1} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2 \]
Snell's law, refractive index relation
\(n_1\), \(n_2\) = refractive indices of the first and second medium (ratio, no unit) · \(\theta_1\) = angle of incidence from the normal, in degrees · \(\theta_2\) = angle of refraction from the normal, in degrees
\[ \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \frac{\sin\theta_1}{\sin\theta_2} \]
Snell's law, velocity relation
\(v_1\), \(v_2\) = speed of light in the first and second medium, in metres per second (m/s)
\[ n = \frac{c}{v} \qquad\qquad \lambda_n = \frac{\lambda}{n} \]
from the course formula sheet; this is why the two forms of Snell's law agree
\(c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}\) = speed of light in vacuum · \(v\) = speed in the medium, in m/s · \(\lambda\) = wavelength in vacuum (or air), in metres (m) · \(\lambda_n\) = wavelength inside a medium of index \(n\), in metres (m)
Filled in

The lecture gives the index form and the velocity form of Snell's law side by side without linking them. They are the same statement because \(n = \frac{c}{v}\): \(\frac{n_1}{n_2} = \frac{v_2}{v_1}\), so \(\frac{v_1}{v_2} = \frac{n_2}{n_1} = \frac{\sin\theta_1}{\sin\theta_2}\). The relation \(\lambda_n = \frac{\lambda}{n}\) is on the course formula sheet and is added here because it explains why a film behaves as if it were thicker than it is (Section 6).

Possible lecture error

The lecture lists the colors on a compact disk as a thin film example. The rainbow on a CD comes mainly from diffraction: its data tracks are about 1.6 µm apart and act like a reflection grating. A soap bubble or an oil slick on water is the clean thin-film example. If an exam asks for thin-film examples, soap bubbles and oil films are the safe choices; check with your instructor how they want the CD example treated.

Example 4 (lecture): why the film must be thin

Lecture 8b page 4: interference effects are most significant when light interacts with something whose size is similar to (or smaller than) its wavelength, written as

\[ [\,d \lt \lambda\,] \]

Ray 1 and ray 2 started as the same wave at A, so they are coherent. When they meet again after CD, they may be in phase or out of phase, depending on the extra distance ray 2 travelled inside the film. With \(d\) around a wavelength, that extra distance is a small, well-defined fraction of a wavelength, so each color either clearly adds or clearly cancels.

Thin film (\(d\) about \(\lambda\) or less): rays 1 and 2 may be in or out of phase
Snell's law ray tracer
Defaults: Practice 4.1 (air into oil; the lecture gives no numbers).
Analogy

A shopping cart rolling at an angle from smooth tile onto carpet: the wheel that hits the carpet first slows down first, so the cart swings toward the normal. Light entering a higher-index (slower) medium bends toward the normal in the same way. Where it breaks down: a cart loses energy on carpet and eventually stops; light just changes speed and keeps going at the new speed.

Tricky bits
  • Angles in Snell's law are measured from the normal, not from the surface.
  • Watch which index goes where: \(\frac{n_1}{n_2} = \frac{\sin\theta_2}{\sin\theta_1}\) has the subscripts crossed. The safe form is \(n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2\).
  • Snell's law relates sines, not angles: \(\theta_2 \ne \frac{\theta_1}{n_2}\).
  • Going into a higher index, the ray bends toward the normal (\(\theta_2 \lt \theta_1\)), and the speed and wavelength drop by the factor \(n\). The frequency does not change.
  • The lecture's rule of thumb is \(d \lt \lambda\). Films a few wavelengths thick still show some color; very thick layers (a window pane) show none, because the many overlapping orders wash out.

Section 5Phase change on reflection

Path difference is only half of the thin film story. The other half is what happens at the moment of reflection. Lecture 8b page 6: light waves undergo a phase change of \(\pi\) rad upon reflection at an interface if \(n_2 \gt n_1\), that is, when light travelling in medium 1 reflects off a medium with a higher index. Reflecting off a lower index gives no phase change.

A phase change of \(\pi\) rad (180°) flips the wave upside down, which has the same effect as an extra half wavelength of path. So in every thin film problem you count the flips:

  • One flip (only one of the two reflections is off a higher index): the reflections alone already put the rays half a cycle apart.
  • Zero or two flips: the reflections alone leave the rays in step (two flips cancel each other).
\[ \Delta\phi_{\text{refl}} = \begin{cases} \pi\ \text{rad} \;\left(\text{same as } \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\right) & \text{if the far side has the higher index } (n_2 \gt n_1)\\ 0 & \text{if the far side has the lower index } (n_2 \lt n_1) \end{cases} \]
phase change on reflection
\(\Delta\phi_{\text{refl}}\) = phase change of the reflected wave, in radians (rad) · \(n_1\) = index of the medium the light is travelling in · \(n_2\) = index of the medium it reflects off (both ratios, no unit)
Example 5 (lecture): a very thin film looks dark
Lecture 8b pages 5 and 6: a film of very small thickness \(d\), so the difference between the path lengths of the two rays is negligible. Does a phase change occur?

Take an oil film in air (Lecture 8b page 7): \(n_{\text{air}} = 1\) and \(n_{\text{oil}} \gt 1\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{upper boundary (air to oil)}:&\quad n_{\text{air}} \lt n_{\text{oil}} \;\Rightarrow\; \pi\ \text{rad shift in ray 1}\\ \text{lower boundary (oil to air)}:&\quad n_{\text{oil}} \gt n_{\text{air}} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{no shift in ray 2} \end{aligned}\]

With negligible path difference, the only difference between the rays is the single \(\pi\) flip:

\[ \Delta\phi_{\text{total}} \approx 0 + \pi = \pi\ \text{rad} \]

The two rays are exactly out of phase, so destructive interference happens at every wavelength of the incident light.

Yes, one \(\pi\) flip: the very thin film has a dark appearance
Filled in

The lecture's diagrams switch between oil on water (pages 2 and 4) and oil in air (pages 7 to 9). For typical values (oil about 1.45, water 1.33) the bottom reflection is still off a lower index, so both setups have exactly one flip and the same equations apply. If the layer below the film had a higher index than the film (for example a coating of index 1.38 on glass of index 1.50), both reflections would flip and the bright and dark conditions in Section 6 would swap.

Analogy

Flick a rope tied to a heavy wall: the pulse comes back upside down. Flick a rope tied to a light, loose string: it comes back right side up. Light reflecting off a higher index is the heavy wall; off a lower index is the loose string. Where it breaks down: a rope pulse is one bump you can watch, while light is a continuous wave, so the "upside down" shows up only as a half-cycle shift.

Tricky bits
  • The flip depends on the index of the medium being reflected off compared with the one the light is in, not on which layer is "on top".
  • Count flips for both reflections. One flip shifts the conditions by half a wavelength; two flips cancel.
  • Transmitted light never gets the flip; only reflected light can.
  • A very thin soap film looks black just before it pops: one flip and negligible path difference, so all colors cancel.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{top (air to soap)}:&\quad 1.00 \lt 1.33 \Rightarrow \pi\\ \text{bottom (soap to air)}:&\quad 1.33 \gt 1.00 \Rightarrow 0\\ \text{relative shift} &= \pi\ \text{rad} \end{aligned}\]

Section 6Optical path difference

Ray 2 spends part of its trip inside the film, where light is slower and its wavelength is shorter by the factor \(n\). So a distance \(s\) in the film holds as many wavelengths as a distance \(ns\) in vacuum. That is the optical path length: the product of the geometric length \(s\) of the light's path and the refractive index \(n\) of a homogeneous medium. Comparing optical path lengths, not raw distances, tells you how far out of step two rays are.

\[ \text{OPL} = n\,s \]
optical path length
OPL = optical path length, in metres (m) · \(n\) = refractive index of the medium (ratio, no unit) · \(s\) = geometric length of the path in that medium, in metres (m)
\[ \text{OPD} = n_2\left(\overline{AB} + \overline{BC}\right) - n_1\left(\overline{AD}\right) = 2n_2 d\cos\theta_2 \]
optical path difference between ray 2 (in the oil) and ray 1 (in the air)
OPD = optical path difference, in metres (m) · \(\overline{AB}\), \(\overline{BC}\), \(\overline{AD}\) = segment lengths in the diagram of Section 4, in metres (m) · \(d\) = film thickness, in metres (m) · \(\theta_2\) = refraction angle inside the film, in degrees
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{constructive:}&\quad 2n_{\text{oil}}\,d\cos(\theta_2) = \left(m - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\lambda, \quad m = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\\ \text{destructive:}&\quad 2n_{\text{oil}}\,d\cos(\theta_2) = m\lambda, \quad m = 0, 1, 2, \ldots \end{aligned}\]
reflected light from a film with exactly one \(\pi\) flip (oil in air, oil on water, soap film in air)
\(n_{\text{oil}}\) = film index (the lecture's \(n_2\)), no unit · \(\lambda\) = wavelength in air or vacuum, in metres (m) · \(m\) = order (integer, no unit)
Example 6 (lecture): deriving \(\text{OPD} = 2n_2 d\cos\theta_2\)
Film of thickness \(d\) and index \(n_2\); air above with \(n_1 = n_{\text{air}} = 1\) (Lecture 8b pages 8 and 9).

Ray 2 crosses the film twice at angle \(\theta_2\) from the normal, so each leg is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with height \(d\):

\[ \overline{AB} = \overline{BC} = \frac{d}{\cos\theta_2} \]

The horizontal distance from A to C is \(\overline{AC} = 2d\tan\theta_2\). Ray 1 leaves A at angle \(\theta_1\) from the normal, so the part of ray 1 up to the wavefront CD is

\[ \overline{AD} = 2d\tan\theta_2 \times \sin\theta_1 \]

Snell's law, \(\frac{n_1}{n_2} = \frac{\sin\theta_2}{\sin\theta_1}\), lets us replace \(n_1\sin\theta_1\) with \(n_2\sin\theta_2\). Then:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{OPD} &= n_2\left[\frac{2d}{\cos\theta_2}\right] - 2d\tan\theta_2\,\left(n_2\sin\theta_2\right)\\ &= 2n_2 d\left[\frac{1}{\cos\theta_2} - \frac{\sin^2\theta_2}{\cos\theta_2}\right]\\ &= 2n_2 d\left[\frac{1-\sin^2\theta_2}{\cos\theta_2}\right]\\ &= 2n_2 d\left[\frac{\cos^2\theta_2}{\cos\theta_2}\right]\\ &= 2n_2 d\cos\theta_2 \end{aligned}\]

Because the top reflection adds a \(\pi\) flip and the bottom one does not, the rays cancel when the OPD is a whole number of wavelengths:

\[ \text{Destructive interference} \;\Rightarrow\; 2n_2 d\cos\theta_2 = m\lambda \]

and they reinforce when it is a half-odd number of wavelengths, \(2n_{\text{oil}}\,d\cos(\theta_2) = \left(m - \frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda\) (Lecture 8b page 10).

\(\text{OPD} = 2n_2 d\cos\theta_2\)
Filled in

Steps the lecture skips: (1) why \(\overline{AD} = \overline{AC}\sin\theta_1\): ray 1 makes angle \(90^\circ - \theta_1\) with the surface, and D is the foot of the perpendicular from C, so \(\overline{AD} = \overline{AC}\cos(90^\circ - \theta_1) = \overline{AC}\sin\theta_1\). (2) The \(\cos^2\theta_2\) line, using \(1 - \sin^2\theta_2 = \cos^2\theta_2\). (3) Why \(m\lambda\) means dark: the total phase difference is \(\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\text{OPD} + \pi\), which is an odd multiple of \(\pi\) exactly when \(\text{OPD} = m\lambda\). (4) The ranges of \(m\), which the lecture does not state: \(m = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\) for dark (\(m = 0\) is the very thin film of Section 5) and \(m = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\) for bright. At normal incidence (\(\theta_2 = 0\)) these become the course formula sheet's \(2t = m\frac{\lambda}{n}\) and \(2t = \left(m - \frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{\lambda}{n}\), with \(t\) for the thickness.

What the pattern depends on (Lecture 8b page 10): (a) the wavelength of light, \(\lambda\); (b) the film thickness, \(d\); (c) the refractive indices on both sides of the film; (d) the refractive index of the film. Items (b) and (d) set the OPD, item (c) sets how many flips there are, and item (a) decides which colors satisfy each condition. Because white light contains many wavelengths, a film of varying thickness shows bands of different colors.

Analogy

Two runners start together. One runs straight along the track; the other takes a detour through a sandpit, where every step is shorter. What matters at the finish is not the distance but how many steps each took, and the sandpit multiplies the step count by \(n\). The OPD is the extra step count. Where it breaks down: runners do not get flipped upside down at the edge of the sandpit, but one of the light rays does (Section 5).

Tricky bits
  • Use the angle inside the film, \(\theta_2\), in \(\cos\theta_2\), not the incidence angle \(\theta_1\). Find \(\theta_2\) with Snell's law first.
  • \(\lambda\) in these equations is the wavelength in air; the film index \(n\) on the left already accounts for the shorter wavelength inside.
  • The "\(m\lambda\) is dark" rule holds only with exactly one flip. With zero or two flips, swap: \(m\lambda\) is bright and \(\left(m - \frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda\) is dark.
  • The thinnest film that reflects a color strongly (one flip, normal incidence) is \(d = \frac{\lambda}{4n}\), a quarter of the wavelength inside the film, not \(\frac{\lambda}{2n}\).
  • When asked "which visible color", list a few values of \(m\) and keep only the answers between about 380 nm and 750 nm.

Section 7Beat frequency

Lecture 8b ends with two revision problems on sound from earlier lectures. The first is about beats (Lecture 7). When two sound waves of slightly different frequencies play together, they drift in and out of step. When in step they add (loud); half a beat later they cancel (quiet). The loudness rises and falls at the beat frequency, defined as the absolute difference of the frequencies of the two sound waves.

\[ f_{\text{beat}} = \left|f_1 - f_2\right| \]
beat frequency
\(f_{\text{beat}}\) = beat frequency, in hertz (Hz) · \(f_1\), \(f_2\) = frequencies of the two sound waves, in hertz (Hz)
\[ T_{\text{beat}} = \frac{1}{f_{\text{beat}}} \]
time from one loud moment to the next
\(T_{\text{beat}}\) = beat period, in seconds (s)
Example 7 (lecture): two tuning forks, 200 Hz and 400 Hz
Two tuning forks are struck simultaneously: \(f_1 = 200\ \text{Hz}\), \(f_2 = 400\ \text{Hz}\). What beat frequency is generated? (Lecture 8b pages 11 and 12)
\[ \begin{aligned} f_{\text{beat}} &= \left|f_1 - f_2\right|\\ &= \left|200 - 400\right|\ \text{Hz}\\ &= 200\ \text{Hz} \end{aligned}\]
\(f_{\text{beat}} = 200\ \text{Hz}\)
Filled in

The lecture's answer, 200 Hz, is what the definition gives, and it is the expected answer on a test. Two practical notes: the beat period \(T_{\text{beat}} = \frac{1}{f_{\text{beat}}}\) is added from Lecture 7; and your ear hears beats as a slow pulsing only when the two frequencies are close (a difference of up to roughly 10 to 20 Hz). Forks at 200 Hz and 400 Hz are an octave apart, so in practice you would hear two separate notes, not a 200 Hz pulsing. The formula still gives the rate at which the combined waveform repeats its pattern.

Two tones and their sum
Defaults: Example 7 from the lecture (200 Hz and 400 Hz). Try 200 Hz and 210 Hz to see a slow envelope.
wave 1wave 2sum
Analogy

Two people walking side by side with slightly different step rates: every so often their feet land together, then drift apart, then land together again. How often they line up is the difference of their step rates. Where it breaks down: footsteps are separate events, while sound waves are continuous, so between "in step" and "out of step" the loudness changes smoothly.

Tricky bits
  • The beat frequency is the difference, not the sum and not the average. The average \(\frac{f_1 + f_2}{2}\) is the pitch you hear.
  • The envelope in \(\cos\left(2\pi\,\frac{f_1 - f_2}{2}\,t\right)\) has half the difference, but loud moments happen twice per envelope cycle, so beats come at the full difference \(\left|f_1 - f_2\right|\).
  • Beats tell you the size of the difference, not which fork is higher. To decide, change one fork slightly (add wax to lower it) and see whether the beats speed up or slow down.

Section 8Two-speaker interference

The second revision problem uses the same path difference logic as the rest of this lecture, now with sound. Two speakers driven by the same signal generator send out the same signal, in phase. At a detector, the wave from the farther speaker lags behind by the path difference \(\Delta r\). If that lag is half a wavelength, the waves cancel and the detector reads a minimum.

\[ \Delta r = \left|r_2 - r_1\right| = \left(m + \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\lambda, \quad m = 0, 1, 2, \ldots \]
minimum (destructive interference) for two in-phase sources; the first minimum is \(m = 0\), \(\Delta r = \frac{\lambda}{2}\)
\(r_1\), \(r_2\) = distances from each speaker to the detector, in metres (m) · \(\Delta r\) = path length difference, in metres (m) · \(\lambda\) = wavelength of the sound, in metres (m)
\[ \Delta r = m\lambda \;\;\text{(maximum)}, \qquad f = \frac{v}{\lambda}, \qquad r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \]
maxima, wave speed relation, and Pythagoras for each path
\(f\) = frequency, in hertz (Hz) · \(v\) = speed of sound, in metres per second (m/s) · \(x\), \(y\) = horizontal and vertical legs of each path, in metres (m)
Example 8 (lecture): finding the speakers' frequency from the first minimum
Two speakers, same signal, separated by 8 m. A sound level detector is 12 m in front of the speakers and 2 m below the upper speaker (so 6 m above the lower one), where the 1st minimum intensity is found. Speed of sound \(v = 343\ \text{m/s}\). Find the frequency. (Lecture 8b pages 13 to 16)

Strategy. (1) Calculate the path length from each speaker to the detector. (2) Use the path length difference to determine the wavelength (destructive interference). (3) Calculate the frequency with \(f = \frac{v}{\lambda}\).

Path lengths to the minimum point.

\[ \begin{aligned} r_1 &= \sqrt{12^2 + 2^2}\ \text{m} = 12.17\ \text{m}\\ r_2 &= \sqrt{6^2 + 12^2}\ \text{m} = 13.42\ \text{m} \end{aligned}\]

Path length difference to find \(\lambda\).

\[ \Delta r = \left|r_2 - r_1\right| = 1.25\ \text{m} \]

The 1st minimum intensity is the 1st destructive interference. The lecture writes \(\Delta r = \left|r_2 - r_1\right| = n\frac{\lambda}{2}\) and sets \(n = 1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta r &= \frac{\lambda}{2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2\Delta r = \lambda\\ \lambda &= 2\Delta r = 2(1.25\ \text{m}) = 2.5\ \text{m} \end{aligned}\]

Frequency.

\[ f = \frac{v}{\lambda} = \frac{343\ \text{m/s}}{2.5\ \text{m}} = 137.2\ \text{Hz} \]
\(f = 137.2\ \text{Hz}\)
Filled in

(1) The lecture's general form \(\Delta r = n\frac{\lambda}{2}\) is a minimum only for odd \(n\) (1, 3, 5, ...). With \(n = 2\) it would give \(\Delta r = \lambda\), which is a maximum. The guide writes the general rule as \(\Delta r = \left(m + \frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda\), the same as the course formula sheet's \(\frac{\Delta L}{\lambda} = 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, \ldots\) for destructive interference. The lecture's answer is unaffected because it uses \(n = 1\). (2) Both speakers are assumed to be in phase, since they share one signal generator. (3) With unrounded distances (\(r_1 = 12.166\ \text{m}\), \(r_2 = 13.416\ \text{m}\), \(\Delta r = 1.2509\ \text{m}\)) the answer is 137.1 Hz; the lecture's 137.2 Hz comes from rounding \(\Delta r\) to 1.25 m. Both are fine.

Two-speaker minimum finder

Analogy

Two friends clap in perfect sync from different spots in a hall. If you stand where one clap reaches you exactly halfway between the other's claps, the claps arrive alternately, and for a smooth tone that alternation means cancellation. Where it breaks down: claps are short pulses, so you would hear a double clap rather than silence; cancellation needs continuous waves.

Tricky bits
  • Measure each leg from its own speaker. The detector is 2 m from the top speaker vertically, so it is \(8 - 2 = 6\) m from the bottom one.

  • Round only at the end if you want to match an answer key closely; small rounding of \(\Delta r\) moves the frequency by about 0.1 Hz here.
