Simple harmonic motion: one cosine, two machines

Anything that gets pulled back toward its middle, with a pull proportional to how far it strayed, swings back and forth in a perfect cosine. Lecture 1a describes that motion (position, velocity, acceleration). Lecture 1b shows that a mass on a spring and a pendulum both obey it, and gives you their periods.

Part 1 · Describing simple harmonic motion (Lecture 1a)

01Oscillation, period and frequency

Picture a particle sliding left and right along the x-axis, never going past −xm on the left or +xm on the right. One full cycle is the trip from +xm over to −xm and back again.

  • Period T: the time for one full cycle (seconds).
  • Frequency f: how many full cycles it completes per second (hertz, Hz).
T = 1f    and    f = 1T

The lecture's frame-by-frame breakdown shows how it moves: it starts at +xm at rest (t = 0), speeds up heading left, is fastest as it crosses 0, slows down, stops at −xm at t = T/2, then repeats the trip to the right and is back at +xm at t = T.

Try it: the frame-by-frame breakdown, animated
Analogy · a playground swing

Stand beside a swing and count how many times per second the child comes back to your side: that's f. The wait between two visits is T. A fast swing has a big f and a short T; they are just two ways of saying the same thing.

Tricky bits
  • Count full cycles, not crossings. The particle passes x = 0 twice per cycle (once going each way). A cycle only ends when it is back at the same place moving the same way.
  • Fastest in the middle, stopped at the ends. Speed is zero at ±xm (it has to stop to turn around) and largest at 0. Students often flip this.
  • Units travel together. T in ms gives f in kHz; T in s gives f in Hz.

02The equation of motion

Take the frame-by-frame snapshots, turn the x-axis vertical, and lay the frames side by side in time (the lecture's "rotated view"). The dots trace out a cosine. That's the whole mathematical description of simple harmonic motion:

x(t) = xm cos(ωt + φ) displacement of the particle versus time xm = amplitude · ω = angular frequency (rad/s) · t = time · φ = phase constant

The angular frequency is "angle per time": ω = θ/T. One full wave sweeps the cosine's angle through θ = 2π rad, and that takes one period, so:

ω = 2πT = 2πf
Try it: build x(t) = xm cos(ωt + φ)
Analogy · the shadow of a clock hand

Imagine a clock hand of length xm spinning steadily. Its shadow on a horizontal wall slides back and forth exactly like our particle. The hand's angle is ωt + φ: ω is how fast it spins (radians per second) and φ is the angle it started at. One full turn of the hand (2π) is one full cycle of the shadow.

Tricky bits
  • ω is not f. ω is in rad/s; f is in Hz. They differ by 2π. In x(t) = 0.04 cos(10πt), ω = 10π rad/s but f = 5 Hz.
  • Radians mode. ωt + φ is an angle in radians. A calculator in degree mode gives garbage for cos(ωt + φ).
  • ωt + φ is an angle, not a position. The position is xm times the cosine of that angle, so it never leaves the range −xm to +xm.

03What exactly is φ?

The phase constant φ (also called the phase angle) sets where the particle is when the clock starts, at t = 0. Put t = 0 into the equation of motion and only φ is left inside the cosine:

x(0) = xm cos(0 + φ) = xm cos φ

The lecture marks four starting points on the axis:

①−xmat rest (about to go right)π
②0to the right3π/2

Worked example: test each position at t = 0

The lecture works positions ① and ③ and leaves ② and ④ as an exercise. All four:

① φ = π: x(0) = xm cos(0 + π) = xm cos(π) = −xm
③ φ = π/2: x(0) = xm cos(0 + π/2) = 0
② φ = 3π/2: x(0) = xm cos(3π/2) = 0 (same as ③!)
④ φ = 0: x(0) = xm cos(0) = +xm

② and ③ both start at 0. What separates them is the direction. Using the velocity from Section 5, v(0) = −xmω sin φ:

③ φ = π/2: v(0) = −xmω sin(π/2) = −xmω → moving left
② φ = 3π/2: v(0) = −xmω sin(3π/2) = +xmω → moving right

What the analysis says (lecture slide 7): the starting position at t = 0 depends on φ. At any later time, the full phase ωt + φ tells you where the particle is in its cycle.

Try it: pick a starting point
Analogy · starting a song partway through

Every oscillator with the same xm and ω plays the same tune. φ is just the timestamp where you hit play. φ = 0 starts at the top of the wave; φ = π starts at the bottom; φ = π/2 starts on the way down through the middle.

Tricky bits
  • x = 0 has two φ values. π/2 (moving left) and 3π/2 (moving right). If a problem says "starts at equilibrium", look for the direction before picking φ.
  • Adding π flips the sign. cos(θ + π) = −cos θ, so φ = π gives the mirror image of φ = 0.
  • φ and φ + 2π are the same start. A full extra turn changes nothing.

04Comparing two oscillators

Slide 8 compares two pairs of oscillators to separate what amplitude and frequency each control:

  • A1 ≠ A2 but f1 = f2: one curve is taller (xm > x′m), but the peaks and zero crossings line up in time. Same period.
  • A1 = A2 but f1 ≠ f2: both reach the same height, but one cycles more slowly. The slow (blue) one has the longer period: Tblue > Tpurple.

Analogy · two swings

Push one child higher than the other on identical swings: they still go back and forth in step (same f, different amplitude). Put them on swings with different chain lengths: now they drift out of step no matter how high they go (different f).

Tricky bits
  • Amplitude does not change the period. A bigger swing moves faster and covers more ground in the same time.
  • Longer T means lower f. On a graph, the "stretched out" curve is the low-frequency one.
  • Compare like with like. If one oscillator is given by T and the other by f, convert one before comparing.

Part 2 · Velocity and acceleration (Lecture 1a)

05Velocity: differentiate with the chain rule

Velocity is the rate of change of position, v = dx/dt. The cosine has something inside it (ωt + φ), so we need the chain rule.

Derivation from the lecture (slides 9 and 10)
Start: x(t) = xm cos(ωt + φ)
Let Z = cos(ωt + φ), then x = xmZ
Chain rule: dx/dt = (dZ/dt) · (dx/dZ)
Inner piece: dZ/dt = −ω sin(ωt + φ)
Outer piece: dx/dZ = xm
Multiply: v(t) = −xmω sin(ωt + φ)
Rewrite using cos(x − π/2) = sin x: v(t) = −xmω cos(ωt + φ − π/2)
v(t) = −xmω sin(ωt + φ) velocity amplitude (top speed) = ωxm

The second form says velocity is the same kind of wave as x, with the same ω, but shifted by a quarter cycle (π/2). That's why speed peaks where x = 0 and is zero where x = ±xm.

Try it: velocity is the slope of x(t)
x(t)tangent (slope = v)v(t)
Analogy · reading a hill's steepness

Walk along the x(t) curve. Velocity is how steep the path is under your feet. At the top of a hill (x = +xm) the ground is flat: v = 0. Halfway down, where the curve crosses zero, it's steepest: top speed.

Tricky bits
  • Don't drop the ω. The inner derivative of (ωt + φ) is ω. Top speed is ωxm, not xm.
  • Mind the minus sign. With φ = 0 the particle starts at +xm and heads left, so v must go negative first. The minus sign does that.
  • Two correct forms. −xmω sin(ωt + φ) and −xmω cos(ωt + φ − π/2) are the same function. Use whichever the question asks for.

06Acceleration and the three graphs

Differentiate once more (slide 11). xmω is a constant, and the derivative of sin(ωt + φ) is ω cos(ωt + φ):

a(t) = dv/dt = d/dt[−xmω sin(ωt + φ)]
     = −xmω · d/dt[sin(ωt + φ)]
     = −xmω² cos(ωt + φ) → acceleration amplitude xmω²
a(t) = −xmω² cos(ωt + φ) = −ω² x(t) same cosine as x, times −ω²

Spot the pattern: each derivative multiplies the amplitude by ω. Amplitudes go xm → ωxm → ω²xm. And because a(t) contains the same cosine as x(t), acceleration is always −ω² times the displacement. That relation is the fingerprint of SHM; Lecture 1b uses it directly.

Analogy · a bungee cord to the middle

Imagine the particle tied to x = 0 by a stretchy cord. The farther out it goes, the harder the cord yanks it back (big |a| at the ends). As it passes through the middle the cord is slack (a = 0), but by then it's going fastest, so it overshoots and the cycle continues.

Tricky bits
  • Biggest acceleration where it's stopped. At ±xm, v = 0 but |a| is maximum. Zero velocity does not mean zero acceleration.
  • a and x always have opposite signs because of a = −ω²x. On the graphs, the acceleration curve is the displacement curve flipped upside down.
  • Unit check. xm in m, ωxm in m/s, ω²xm in m/s². If your units don't match, you've lost or gained an ω.

07Worked example: the tire tread

A car tire has a tread pattern with 5 cm spacing between the crevices. As the tire moves, each crevice makes a single vibration. Determine the frequency of these vibrations if the car moves at a velocity of 50 m/s.

Lecture solution (slide 14)
Given: Δx = 5 cm = 0.05 m, v = 50 m/s
Speed: v = Δx/Δt (Δt = time for one spacing to pass)
One vibration per spacing: f = 1/Δt
Combine: v = Δx · f  →  f = v/Δx
Plug in: f = (50 m/s)/(0.05 m) = 1 kHz
Calculator: tread vibration frequency (loaded with the lecture's numbers)
Analogy · a stick along a picket fence

Run past a picket fence dragging a stick: it clacks once per picket. Clacks per second = your speed ÷ the picket spacing. Run twice as fast, twice the clacks. The tire's crevices are the pickets.

Tricky bits
  • Convert cm to m first. 50 ÷ 5 = 10 Hz is the classic wrong answer. 50 m/s ÷ 0.05 m = 1000 Hz.
  • The period is the time per crevice. T = 1/f = Δx/v = 1 ms here.

Part 3 · The mass-spring system (Lecture 1b)

08Hooke's law

A block of mass m sits on a frictionless floor, attached to a wall by a spring. At x = 0 the spring is relaxed. Pull the block out and let go: it oscillates between −xm and +xm, a real example of SHM.

The spring's force follows Hooke's law, where k is the spring stiffness (spring constant, N/m):

F = −kx x measured from the relaxed position

The minus sign makes it a restoring force: stretch the spring (x > 0) and it pulls back left; squash it (x < 0) and it pushes back right.

Try it: drag the block, watch the force
Analogy · a rubber band

Stretch a rubber band a little and it tugs gently; stretch it twice as far and it tugs twice as hard, always back toward its rest length. k is how "tough" the band is: a stiff band (big k) tugs hard per centimetre.

Tricky bits
  • x is the stretch, not the spring's length. Measure from the relaxed position x = 0.
  • The sign is the direction. F = −10 N means 10 N pointing toward −x.
  • Why frictionless? With friction the amplitude would shrink every cycle. Setting friction to 0 keeps the only horizontal force −kx, so the motion is pure SHM.

09Period and frequency of a mass-spring system

Combine Hooke's law with Newton's second law, then insert what we already know about SHM.

Derivation from the lecture (slides 2 and 3)
Newton: F = ma
Spring: −kx = ma = m d²x/dt² (1)
SHM: x(t) = xm cos(ωt + φ),  a(t) = d²x/dt² = −xmω² cos(ωt + φ)
Substitute into (1): −k(xm cos(ωt + φ)) = −m xmω² cos(ωt + φ)
Cancel xm cos(ωt + φ): −k = −mω²
Solve: ω = √(k/m)
Since ω = 2π/T = 2πf: T = 2π/ω = 2π√(m/k)
and f = 1/T = (1/2π)√(k/m)
ω = √km    T = 2π√mk    f = 12π√km
Try it: change k and m, watch the rhythm change
Analogy · trucks and sports cars

A stiff spring with a light load (big k, small m) buzzes quickly, like a sports car's tight suspension. A soft spring with a heavy load (small k, big m) bounces slowly, like a loaded truck. ω = √(k/m) is "stiffness per unit of laziness".

Tricky bits
  • T has m/k; f has k/m. They are reciprocals, so the fraction flips. Mixing them up is the most common mistake.
  • Square root. 4× the mass gives 2× the period, not 4×.
  • No amplitude. xm cancelled, so how far you pull the block does not change T.
  • Why cancel the cosine? The equation must hold at every instant t, so the coefficients in front of cos(ωt + φ) must match.

Part 4 · The simple pendulum (Lecture 1b)

10Forces on a pendulum and the small-angle approximation

Definition: a simple pendulum is an object with small mass m hanging from a light wire or string of length L. When it's pulled aside by an angle θ, two forces act on it: the string's tension (along the string) and its weight W = −mg (straight down).

Split the weight into two parts. The part along the string just pulls on the string. The part along the arc, W sin θ, is what pulls the bob back toward the bottom. Newton's second law along the arc:

W sin θ = ma (a = tangential acceleration)
With W = −mg: −mg sin θ = ma
Cancel m: a = −g sin θ
If θ is very small: sin θ ≈ θ  →  a = −gθ
a = −g sin θ  ≈  −gθ θ in radians, small angles only

Analogy · a ball on a ramp

The bob always sits on a little "ramp" (the arc). Near the bottom the ramp is almost flat, so only a sliver of gravity pushes along it. Swing it higher and the ramp gets steeper, so more of gravity pushes it back. For small angles the "steepness" is just θ itself.

Tricky bits
  • Radians only. sin θ ≈ θ works because sin 0.1745 ≈ 0.1745. In degrees you'd be comparing 0.17 with 10.
  • Tension does no restoring. It points along the string, at right angles to the motion.
  • Mass cancels. m appears in both the weight and ma, so a = −g sin θ has no m in it.
  • Two T's. The lecture uses T for tension here and T for period later. Read the context.

11The pendulum equation and its period

The bob moves along an arc of radius L, so its tangential acceleration is L times its angular acceleration α = d²θ/dt². Combine that with a = −gθ:

Arc motion: a = Lα = L d²θ/dt²
Combine: −gθ = L d²θ/dt²
Rearrange: L d²θ/dt² + gθ = 0

Now compare with the mass-spring equation from Lecture 1b, whose solution we already know:

Try it: a swinging pendulum (starts at the lecture's practice-problem values)
Analogy · the same song in a different key

The spring and the pendulum sing the same melody (the same equation); only the letters change. Wherever the spring says m, the pendulum says L. Wherever it says k, the pendulum says g. Learn one and you get the other for free.

Tricky bits
  • No mass in T. A heavy bob and a light bob on the same string swing with the same period. (The spring's T does depend on m; the pendulum's doesn't.)
  • √L, again. 4× the length gives 2× the period.
  • ω is not the swinging speed. Here ω = 2π/T is a fixed angular frequency. The bob's actual angular speed dθ/dt changes all the time (zero at the ends, largest at the bottom).
  • Small angles only. This T comes from sin θ ≈ θ. At large swings the real period is a bit longer.

Part 5 · Lecture 1b practice problems

12Practice problem 1: measuring g with a pendulum

Determine the gravitational acceleration in a region if a simple pendulum has a period of 1.8 s and length of 76 cm. Compare this region's gravitational acceleration with Earth's gravitational acceleration.

Lecture solution (slide 9)
Start from: T = 2π√(L/g)
Square both sides: T² = 4π²L/g
Solve for g: g = 4π²L/T²
Plug in (L = 76 cm = 0.76 m): g = 4π²(0.76)/(1.8 s)²
            = 9.26 m/s²
Earth: g₀ = 9.81 m/s²
Compare: δ = [(9.81 − 9.26)/9.81] × 100%
          ≈ 5.607% weaker
Calculator: g from a pendulum (loaded with the lecture's numbers)
Analogy · a gravity meter you can build

A pendulum is a clock whose ticking speed is set by gravity. Measure its length with a ruler and its period with a stopwatch, and it tells you g. Geologists have used exactly this to map tiny changes in gravity.

Tricky bits
  • Square before you rearrange. T = 2π√(L/g) → T² = 4π²L/g → g = 4π²L/T². Forgetting to square T is common.
  • 76 cm = 0.76 m. Using 76 gives g = 926 m/s².
  • δ is relative to Earth's value. Divide the difference by 9.81, not by 9.26.
  • Rounding. The lecture rounds g to 9.26 first, giving 5.607%. Carrying the unrounded g (9.2604) gives 5.603%. Either is fine; say which you did.

13Practice problem 2: a bouncing car

Bad shock absorbers cause cars to easily oscillate when going over bumps or after stopping. Determine the period and frequency of such an oscillation if an occupied car's total mass is 1 tonne and the force constant on the car's suspension system is 85 000 N/m.

Lecture solution (slide 11)
Model: suspension = simple harmonic oscillator (analogous to a mass-spring system)
Given: k = 85 000 N/m, m = 1 tonne = 1000 kg
Frequency: f = (1/2π)√(k/m) = (1/2π)√(85 000/1000)
           ≈ 1.467 Hz
Period: T = 1/f = 0.6815 s
Calculator: car suspension (loaded with the lecture's numbers)
Analogy · a mattress with no dampers

Sit down hard on a springy mattress and you bob up and down a few times. Shock absorbers are like friction that kills that bobbing after one bounce. Worn-out shocks leave only the springs, so the car bobs like the frictionless mass-spring from Section 9, about one and a half bounces per second.

Tricky bits
  • 1 tonne = 1000 kg. Not 1 kg, not 2000 lb.
  • √85 = 9.22 is ω, not f. You still have to divide by 2π to get 1.467 Hz.
  • The model ignores damping. A real car with good shocks would barely complete one cycle. The lecture's numbers describe the "bad shocks" case.
