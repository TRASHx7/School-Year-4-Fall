Blackbody, Photoelectric and Compton Guide

Three experiments that classical wave physics could not explain: the glow of hot objects, electrons knocked out of metal by light, and X-rays that come back with a longer wavelength. Each one is fixed by the same idea. Light carries energy in packets (photons) of size hf, and each photon also carries momentum h over λ.

Start hereBig picture

Up to Lecture 8, light was a wave: it diffracts, it interferes, it has a wavelength \(\lambda\) and a frequency \(f\) with \(\lambda f = c\). Lecture 9 shows three experiments where that picture gives the wrong answer. In each case the fix is the same: light's energy comes in lumps called quanta (later photons), each with energy \(hf\). Once you accept that, the same lumps also carry momentum \(\frac{h}{\lambda}\), and you can treat a photon hitting an electron like a collision between two particles.

Part 1 · Blackbody radiation (Lecture 9a)

Section 1Blackbodies and the radiation curve

A blackbody is an object that absorbs the energy that falls on it and re-emits large amounts of it. A perfect blackbody absorbs everything that hits it. When it sits at a steady temperature, it must send out exactly as much energy as it takes in, otherwise it would keep heating up. The light it sends out is called blackbody radiation.

\[ E_1 = E_2 \]
steady state of a perfect blackbody (lecture page 1)
\(E_1\) = energy absorbed, in joules (J) · \(E_2\) = energy emitted, in joules (J). Over a time interval the same holds for power: absorbed power = emitted power, in watts (W).

The important experimental fact is the shape of the emitted spectrum. Plot radiation intensity \(I\) against wavelength \(\lambda\) and you get a hump (lecture page 2):

  • At about 3000 K the object is "red hot". Its hump peaks in the infrared, and only the red tail reaches the visible range.
  • At about 4000 K the hump is taller and its peak has moved toward the visible range.
  • At about 5000 K the object is "white hot". The peak sits inside the visible range (about 0.4 to 0.7 µm), so all colours are emitted strongly.
  • The ultraviolet range is to the left of the visible band (shorter \(\lambda\)), the infrared to the right.

Hotter means more radiation at every wavelength and a peak at a shorter wavelength. Every real curve drops back to zero at very short wavelengths. The grey curve the lecture labels "classical physics (about 5000 K)" does not: it keeps rising as \(\lambda\) gets shorter. That disagreement is the problem Section 3 solves.

Example (lecture page 1): a black shirt on a hot day
Given: sunlight falls on a black shirt and on a white shirt.

A black surface is close to a blackbody: it absorbs almost all the visible sunlight that hits it. A white surface reflects most of it. So the black shirt takes in much more energy per second (\(E_1\) is larger).

The shirt warms up until it re-emits as much as it absorbs (\(E_1 = E_2\)). Because it absorbs more, it has to reach a higher temperature before its emission catches up, so it feels hotter on your skin.

The black shirt is the better absorber, so it settles at a higher temperature
Analogy

Think of a bathtub with the tap running and the drain open. The water level rises until the outflow matches the inflow. A blackbody's temperature works the same way: it climbs until the energy leaving (emission) matches the energy arriving (absorption). Turn the tap up (more sunlight) and the level settles higher (hotter object). The analogy breaks in one place: a hotter blackbody does not just emit more, it also changes colour, which a drain cannot do.

Tricky bits
  • "Black" body does not mean it looks black. A perfect absorber at 5000 K glows white. The name describes how it absorbs, not its colour.
  • The x-axis on the lecture graph is in micrometres (µm): 1 µm = 1000 nm = \(10^{-6}\) m. The visible band is roughly 0.4 to 0.7 µm, that is 400 to 700 nm.
  • A higher-temperature curve lies above a lower one at every wavelength. The curves never cross.
  • Absorbed equals emitted only at steady state. While an object is warming up, it absorbs more than it emits.

Section 2Wien's and Stefan's laws

Two experimental laws summarise the blackbody curves (lecture pages 4 and 5). Wien's displacement law says where the peak is: the hotter the body, the shorter the peak wavelength. Stefan's law says how much power comes out in total, adding up all wavelengths: it grows as the fourth power of temperature.

\[ \lambda_{\max}\,T = 2.898\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}\cdot\text{K} \]
Wien's displacement law (law 1)
\(\lambda_{\max}\) = wavelength at which the intensity curve peaks, in metres (m) · \(T\) = absolute temperature of the body, in kelvin (K)
\[ P(T) = \sigma A T^{4} \]
Stefan's law (law 2): total power radiated over the whole spectrum
\(P(T)\) = total radiated power, in watts (W) · \(\sigma\) = Stefan-Boltzmann constant, \(5.67\times10^{-8}\ \text{W/(m}^2\cdot\text{K}^4)\) · \(A\) = surface area of the blackbody, in square metres (m²) · \(T\) = temperature, in kelvin (K)

On the lecture's page 4 graph, a thick line joins the peaks of the 5000 K, 4000 K and 3000 K curves. It slopes down and to the right: as the temperature falls, the peak drops and moves to longer \(\lambda\). That line is Wien's law.

Example (applied to the lecture page 2 curves): where do the three peaks sit?
Temperatures from the lecture graph: \(T = 3000\ \text{K}\) (red hot), \(4000\ \text{K}\), \(5000\ \text{K}\) (white hot). Wien constant \(2.898\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}\cdot\text{K}\).

Rearrange Wien's law for the peak wavelength and substitute each temperature:

\[ \begin{aligned} \lambda_{\max} &= \frac{2.898\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}\cdot\text{K}}{T}\\ T = 3000\ \text{K}:\quad \lambda_{\max} &= \frac{2.898\times10^{-3}}{3000}\ \text{m} = 9.66\times10^{-7}\ \text{m} = \mathbf{966\ \text{nm}}\\ T = 4000\ \text{K}:\quad \lambda_{\max} &= \frac{2.898\times10^{-3}}{4000}\ \text{m} = \mathbf{724.5\ \text{nm}}\\ T = 5000\ \text{K}:\quad \lambda_{\max} &= \frac{2.898\times10^{-3}}{5000}\ \text{m} = \mathbf{579.6\ \text{nm}} \end{aligned} \]

Only the 5000 K peak lies inside the visible band (400 to 700 nm). That matches the "white hot" label. The 3000 K peak is in the infrared, which is why a red-hot object looks dull red: you only see the edge of its hump.

Stefan's law compares the total power from the same surface at 5000 K and 3000 K. The area and \(\sigma\) cancel:

\[ \frac{P(5000\ \text{K})}{P(3000\ \text{K})} = \left(\frac{5000}{3000}\right)^{4} = \mathbf{7.72}\ \text{(ratio, no unit)} \]
Peaks at 966 nm, 724.5 nm and 579.6 nm; the 5000 K body radiates 7.72 times as much power as at 3000 K

Analogy

Picture a stove element as you turn up the dial. First it glows dull red, then orange, then yellow-white. That colour shift is Wien's law. At the same time the heat you feel on your face climbs much faster than the dial setting, because doubling the temperature multiplies the power by 16. That is Stefan's law. Tricky bits
  • Kelvin only. Both laws need absolute temperature. Convert first: \(T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15\). Using Celsius in \(T^4\) gives nonsense.
  • The Wien constant is \(2.898\times10^{-3}\) m·K, not 2.898 m·K. Dropping the \(10^{-3}\) gives a peak wavelength in millimetres.
  • Stefan's law is a fourth power. Doubling \(T\) gives \(2^4 = 16\) times the power, not 2 or 4 times.
  • \(A\) is the radiating surface area. For a sphere, \(A = 4\pi r^2\), not \(\pi r^2\).
  • Wien gives the peak only; Stefan gives the total under the whole curve. Don't use one in place of the other.

Section 3The UV catastrophe and Planck's quanta

The classical picture (lecture page 6). Model the blackbody as a cavity: electromagnetic waves bounce around inside, in equilibrium with the walls. Radiation is treated purely as waves, each wave mode gets an equal share of thermal energy, and the result is the Rayleigh-Jeans law:

\[ I(T) = \frac{2\,c\,k_B\,T}{\lambda^{4}} \]
Rayleigh-Jeans law (lecture page 3, classical)
\(I\) = radiation intensity per unit wavelength · \(c\) = speed of light, \(3.00\times10^{8}\) m/s · \(k_B\) = Boltzmann's constant, \(1.381\times10^{-23}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\) · \(\lambda\) = wavelength, in metres (m) · \(T\) = temperature, in kelvin (K)

It matches the measured curves at long wavelengths, but the \(\lambda^4\) in the denominator means that as \(\lambda \to 0\) the intensity grows without limit. Adding up all wavelengths would give infinite power. This failure in the ultraviolet end is the ultraviolet catastrophe: a divergence between theory and experiment.

Planck's hypothesis (lecture pages 7 to 9). Cavity radiation comes from atoms oscillating in the walls, and those oscillators can only hold discrete (quantized) amounts of energy, called quanta:

\[ E_n = n\,h\,f,\qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \]
allowed energies of a Planck oscillator
\(E_n\) = energy of the oscillator in quantum state \(n\), in electron-volts (eV) or joules (J) · \(n\) = quantum number (whole number, no unit), describing the quantum state of the oscillator · \(h\) = Planck's constant, \(4.136\times10^{-15}\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{s} = 6.63\times10^{-34}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{s}\) · \(f\) = oscillation frequency, in hertz (Hz)
\[ \Delta E = h f \]
energy jump between two consecutive quantum states: the ladder is \(hf, 2hf, 3hf, \ldots, nhf\)
\(\Delta E\) = energy of one quantum, in eV or J

With this one assumption Planck derived a formula that fits the measured curves at every wavelength:

\[ I(\lambda, T) = \frac{2\pi h c^{2}}{\lambda^{5}}\cdot\frac{1}{\exp\!\left[\dfrac{hc}{\lambda k_B T}\right] - 1} \]
Planck's blackbody radiation law (lecture page 9)
\(I(\lambda,T)\) = power intensity per unit wavelength, in W/m² per metre of wavelength (W·m⁻³) · all other symbols as above. Wien's law and Stefan's law can both be derived from this formula.
Worked derivation: why Planck's law has no catastrophe

Call the exponent \(x = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda k_B T}\) (no unit). It compares one quantum's energy \(\frac{hc}{\lambda}\) with the typical thermal energy \(k_B T\).

Long wavelengths (\(x \ll 1\)): use \(e^{x} \approx 1 + x\), so \(e^{x} - 1 \approx x\):

\[ \begin{aligned} I &\approx \frac{2\pi h c^{2}}{\lambda^{5}}\cdot\frac{\lambda k_B T}{hc}\\ &= \frac{2\pi c\,k_B T}{\lambda^{4}} \end{aligned} \]

This is the Rayleigh-Jeans shape: the \(\lambda^{-4}\) law reappears, so the classical result is correct for long wavelengths.

Short wavelengths (\(x \gg 1\)): \(e^{x}\) grows much faster than \(\lambda^{-5}\), so

\[ I \approx \frac{2\pi h c^{2}}{\lambda^{5}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{hc}{\lambda k_B T}\right) \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \quad \text{as } \lambda \to 0 \]

Physically: a short-wavelength quantum \(hf\) is much bigger than \(k_B T\), so the walls almost never have enough thermal energy to emit one. Those modes stay nearly empty, and the curve turns over instead of blowing up.

Planck matches Rayleigh-Jeans at long λ and goes to zero at short λ
Filled in

The lecture states that Planck's law fixes the catastrophe but does not show the two limits. They were added here.

Possible lecture error

Lecture page 3 writes Rayleigh-Jeans as \(I(T) = \frac{2ck_BT}{\lambda^4}\), while page 9 writes Planck's law with a leading \(2\pi hc^2\). The long-wavelength limit of the page 9 formula is \(\frac{2\pi c k_B T}{\lambda^4}\), a factor of \(\pi\) larger. Both forms appear in textbooks (one counts power per unit solid angle, the other the total power leaving a surface), but the two pages mix them. For comparing the two laws on one graph, use \(\frac{2\pi c k_B T}{\lambda^4}\) with the page 9 Planck formula. Any ratio of Rayleigh-Jeans values (for example, halving \(\lambda\)) is unaffected.

Filled in

The lecture lists \(n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\). In Planck's model \(n = 0\) (an oscillator holding no quanta) is also allowed. It does not change the spacing \(\Delta E = hf\) or any result in this lecture.

Analogy

A vending machine that only takes whole coins. The machine's walls (the oscillators) have a pocketful of small change (thermal energy about \(k_BT\)). Low-frequency modes are cheap snacks, priced in small coins, so they sell all the time. High-frequency modes are priced at one big coin \(hf\) each, which the walls rarely have, so they almost never sell. Classical physics assumed you could pay any fraction of a price, so every snack sold equally often, and the infinitely many expensive ones added up to infinity. (The analogy breaks in that real thermal energy is shared statistically, not held as a fixed amount.)

Tricky bits
  • Planck quantized the energy of the oscillators in the walls. It was Einstein (Part 3) who said light itself travels as quanta.
  • Watch the units of \(h\): \(4.136\times10^{-15}\) eV·s gives energies in eV; \(6.63\times10^{-34}\) J·s gives joules. Never mix them in one line.
  • Rayleigh-Jeans has \(\lambda^4\); Planck has \(\lambda^5\) times an exponential. Halving \(\lambda\) in Rayleigh-Jeans multiplies \(I\) by \(2^4 = 16\).
  • The catastrophe is at short wavelength (ultraviolet), not long. At long wavelength classical theory works.

Part 2 · The photoelectric effect (Lecture 9a)

Section 4The experiment and the stopping potential

Shine monochromatic (single-wavelength) light of short enough wavelength on a metal surface. The metal absorbs the light and its surface emits electrons. This is the photoelectric effect, and the emitted electrons are photoelectrons (lecture page 10).

The apparatus (lecture page 11). Inside an evacuated glass tube sit two electrodes. Light falls on the cathode (the target material). Electrons fly across to the anode, and the ammeter in the outer circuit reads the resulting photocurrent. A battery and a rheostat set the potential difference between the electrodes, read by the voltmeter. If you reverse the battery so the anode is negative, it pushes electrons back. Make that reverse potential large enough and even the fastest electrons cannot reach the anode: the photocurrent drops to zero. That reverse potential is the stopping potential \(\Delta V_s\).

\[ K_{\max} = e\,\Delta V_s \]
largest kinetic energy of the photoelectrons (lecture page 14)
\(K_{\max}\) = maximum kinetic energy of an emitted electron, in joules (J) or electron-volts (eV) · \(e\) = magnitude of the electron charge, \(1.602\times10^{-19}\) C · \(\Delta V_s\) = stopping potential, in volts (V)
Worked derivation (lecture page 14): energy balance at the stopping potential

A charge \(q\) moving through a potential difference \(\Delta V\) changes its kinetic energy by \(q\Delta V\). For the electron the lecture writes the charge as \(e\) and the balance as:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta K &= q\,\Delta V\\ \Delta K - e\,\Delta V &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

Apply the stopping potential, which is a reverse potential, so \(\Delta V = -\Delta V_s\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta K - e(-\Delta V_s) &= 0\\ (0 - K_i) + e\,\Delta V_s &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

The fastest electron starts with kinetic energy \(K_i\) and just comes to rest (\(K_f = 0\)) at the anode. So

\[ K_i = e\,\Delta V_s \quad\Longrightarrow\quad K_{\max} = e\,\Delta V_s \]
\(K_{\max} = e\,\Delta V_s\)
Filled in

The lecture writes the charge simply as \(e\) and lets the minus sign on \(-\Delta V_s\) carry the direction. Expanding: \(\Delta K + e\Delta V_s = 0\), so \(\Delta K = -e\Delta V_s\). The electron loses \(e\Delta V_s\) of kinetic energy crossing the reverse potential, and the fastest one has exactly that much to lose.

The convenient unit. An electron-volt is the energy one electron gains or loses crossing 1 V, so \(1\ \text{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\). In eV, \(K_{\max}\) has the same number as \(\Delta V_s\) in volts: a stopping potential of 1.5 V means \(K_{\max} = 1.5\ \text{eV}\).

Example (built on lecture page 14): a 1.5 V stopping potential
Measured stopping potential \(\Delta V_s = 1.5\ \text{V}\). Electron mass \(m_0 = 9.109\times10^{-31}\ \text{kg}\).
\[ \begin{aligned} K_{\max} &= e\,\Delta V_s = (1.602\times10^{-19}\ \text{C})(1.5\ \text{V})\\ &= 2.403\times10^{-19}\ \text{J} = 1.5\ \text{eV}\\ v_{\max} &= \sqrt{\frac{2K_{\max}}{m_0}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(2.403\times10^{-19}\ \text{J})}{9.109\times10^{-31}\ \text{kg}}}\\ &= \mathbf{7.26\times10^{5}\ \text{m/s}} \end{aligned} \]

That is about 0.24% of \(c\), so the non-relativistic \(K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2\) is fine here.

K_max = 2.403 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 1.5 eV; v_max = 7.26 × 10⁵ m/s
Analogy

Electrons leaving the cathode are like balls kicked up a hill toward the anode. The reverse potential is the height of the hill. Raise it until not even the hardest-kicked ball makes it over the top; that height tells you the kinetic energy of the hardest kick. (It breaks down in that the hill here is electrical, so it only slows charged particles.)

Tricky bits
  • \(e\Delta V_s\) gives the maximum kinetic energy, not the typical one. Electrons from deeper in the metal lose extra energy on the way out and are stopped earlier.
  • Converting: multiply volts by \(1.602\times10^{-19}\) to get joules. Dividing by it instead gives a huge, wrong number.
  • \(K_{\max}\) in eV equals \(\Delta V_s\) in volts. Don't multiply by \(1.602\times10^{-19}\) if the answer is wanted in eV.
  • The stopping potential is a reverse potential (anode negative). That is why it appears as \(-\Delta V_s\) on the left of the current graph.

Section 5Three results classical physics cannot explain

Lecture pages 12 to 16 list three key characteristics of the photoelectric effect. Classical wave theory predicts something different for each one.

ObservationWhat is measuredWhat classical (wave) physics predicts
1. Absence of lag timeElectrons come out instantly, even in very dim light.A dim wave spreads energy thinly, so each electron needs time to build up enough energy: there should be a lag.
2. Intensity and kinetic energyBrighter light gives more photocurrent, but the stopping potential \(-\Delta V_s\) is the same for any intensity, so \(K_{\max}\) does not change.A stronger wave shakes electrons harder: \(K\) should be proportional to intensity, and depend only on intensity.
3. Cut-off frequencyBelow a minimum frequency \(f_c\) no photocurrent is generated, however bright the light. Above it, \(K_{\max}\) rises linearly with \(f\).\(K\) should not depend on frequency at all, so there should be no cut-off: any frequency works if you wait or turn up the brightness.

The \(K_{\max}\) versus \(f\) graph (lecture page 15). Each metal gives a straight line that starts at its own cut-off frequency (\(f_{c,1}\) for metal 1, \(f_{c,2}\) for metal 2). Extend the lines backwards (dashed) and they hit the vertical axis at \(-\varphi_1\) and \(-\varphi_2\), where \(\varphi\) is the metal's work function (Section 6). The two lines are parallel: the slope is the same for every metal. Part 3 shows the slope is Planck's constant.

\[ K_{\max} = 0 \quad \text{for } f < f_c \qquad(\text{no photocurrent}) \]
the cut-off: no electrons below \(f_c\), whatever the intensity

Part 3 · Einstein's photons (Lecture 9b)

Section 6Photon energy and the work function

Einstein asked: if Planck was right that energy comes in quanta in the cavity walls, could the same idea explain how the surface of the photoelectrode absorbs energy from light? His approach (lecture 9b pages 1 to 3):

  • A beam of monochromatic light of frequency \(f\) is made of photons.
  • A photon is a particle of light and moves at the speed of light.
  • Each photon carries one energy quantum \(E_f\) that depends only on its frequency.
  • Energy transfer is "all or nothing": one photon gives all its energy to one electron, and the photon ceases to exist after the transfer.
\[ E_f = h f \]
energy of one photon
\(E_f\) = photon energy, in eV or J · \(h\) = Planck's constant, \(4.136\times10^{-15}\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{s}\) · \(f\) = frequency of the light, in Hz
\[ E_f = K_{\max} + \varphi \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad K_{\max} = E_f - \varphi = hf - \varphi \]
Einstein's photoelectric equation: photon energy = kinetic energy + energy to escape
\(K_{\max}\) = maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectron, in eV · \(\varphi\) = work function: the detachment energy needed to pull an electron free of the surface, in eV
Metal (lecture 9b page 4)Work function \(\varphi\) (eV)
Ag (silver)4.73
Al (aluminium)4.08
Pt (platinum)6.35

Einstein's interpretation of the puzzles from Section 5 (lecture page 5):

  1. Absence of lag time. The interaction is between one individual photon and one individual electron, so it is instantaneous. Lowering the intensity cannot lengthen it.
  2. Intensity and kinetic energy. Intensity is the number of photons arriving at the metal surface per unit time. More photons free more electrons (more current), but each photon still has \(E_f = hf\), so \(K_{\max}\) does not change.
  3. Cut-off frequency. If \(hf < \varphi\), a single photon cannot free an electron, and no number of photons helps. This is Section 7.

Comparing \(K_{\max} = hf - \varphi\) with the straight lines of Section 5: slope \(h\) (the same for every metal) and intercept \(-\varphi\).

Paying to leave a car park. Each photon is one payment of \(hf\). The exit fee is the work function \(\varphi\). Whatever is left over after the fee is the change you drive away with (\(K_{\max}\)). Section 7Cut-off frequency and cut-off wavelength

At the threshold, the photon has just enough energy to free the electron and nothing is left over: \(K_{\max} = 0\). Setting that in Einstein's equation gives the cut-off frequency (lecture 9b page 6). Then \(\lambda f = c\) turns it into a cut-off wavelength (page 7).

\[ f_c = \frac{\varphi}{h} \]
cut-off (threshold) frequency: depends only on the metal's work function
\(f_c\) = cut-off frequency, in Hz · \(\varphi\) = work function, in eV · \(h = 4.136\times10^{-15}\) eV·s
\[ \lambda_c = \frac{c}{f_c} = \frac{hc}{\varphi} \]
cut-off wavelength: the photoelectric effect does not occur for \(\lambda > \lambda_c\)
\(\lambda_c\) = cut-off wavelength, in m (or nm with \(hc = 1240.8\) eV·nm) · \(c = 3.00\times10^{8}\) m/s

A bigger work function means a higher cut-off frequency, which means a photon of higher energy is needed (the lecture's arrows: \(\uparrow\varphi \to \uparrow f \to \uparrow E\)). Photons with energy below \(E_c = hf_c\) do not produce the photoelectric effect.

Section 8Photon momentum

Photons are massless energy particles, and like all propagation of pure energy (EM waves) they travel at only one speed, \(c\). So how can something with no mass have momentum? Classical \(p = mv\) gives zero. The answer comes from special relativity, which links energy, momentum and rest mass for anything moving close to \(c\) (lecture 9b pages 8 to 11):

\[ E^{2} = p^{2}c^{2} + m_0^{2}c^{4} \]
relativistic energy relation
\(E\) = total energy, in J · \(p\) = linear momentum, in kg·m/s · \(m_0\) = rest mass, in kg · \(c = 3\times10^{8}\) m/s
\[ p_f = \frac{h}{\lambda} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \vec{p}_f = \hbar\,\vec{k} \]
photon momentum, as a magnitude and as a vector
\(p_f\) = photon momentum, in kg·m/s · \(h = 6.63\times10^{-34}\) J·s · \(\lambda\) = wavelength, in m · \(\hbar = \frac{h}{2\pi}\) = reduced Planck's constant, \(1.055\times10^{-34}\) J·s · \(\vec{k}\) = wave vector, pointing along the direction of travel, with magnitude \(k = |\vec{k}| = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\) (wave number), in rad/m
Worked derivation (lecture 9b pages 10 and 11): p_f = h over λ, then ħk

A photon has no rest mass, \(m_0 = 0\), so the relativistic relation loses its second term:

\[ E_f^{2} = p_f^{2}c^{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; E_f = p_f\,c \qquad (1) \]

Planck's energy quantum with \(f = \frac{c}{\lambda}\):

\[ E_f = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \qquad (2) \]

Set (1) equal to (2); the \(c\) cancels:

\[ p_f\,c = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \;\Longrightarrow\; p_f = \frac{h}{\lambda} \]

Now recall the wave number \(k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\), so \(\lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} p_f &= \frac{h}{\;\dfrac{2\pi}{k}\;} = \frac{h}{2\pi}\,k = \hbar k\\ \vec{p}_f &= \hbar\,\vec{k} \end{aligned} \]
\(p_f = \frac{h}{\lambda} = \hbar k\), pointing along the photon's direction
Example (built on lecture 9b pages 10 and 11): green photon
\(\lambda = 500\ \text{nm} = 500\times10^{-9}\ \text{m}\), \(h = 6.63\times10^{-34}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{s}\)
\[ \begin{aligned} p_f &= \frac{h}{\lambda} = \frac{6.63\times10^{-34}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{s}}{500\times10^{-9}\ \text{m}} = \mathbf{1.326\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}}\\ k &= \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} = \frac{2\pi}{500\times10^{-9}\ \text{m}} = 1.257\times10^{7}\ \text{rad/m}\\ \hbar k &= (1.055\times10^{-34}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{s})(1.257\times10^{7}\ \text{rad/m}) = 1.326\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}\ \checkmark\\ E_f &= \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{1240.8\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{500\ \text{nm}} = 2.482\ \text{eV} \end{aligned} \]
p_f = 1.326 × 10⁻²⁷ kg·m/s (tiny, but measurable as radiation pressure)
Analogy

Wind has no "weight" you can hold, yet it pushes a sail. Light pushes too: solar sails use exactly this photon momentum. The analogy breaks because wind is made of massive air molecules; a photon's momentum comes from its energy alone, \(p = \frac{E}{c}\).

Tricky bits
  • Never use \(p = mv\) for a photon. Use \(p_f = \frac{h}{\lambda}\) or \(p_f = \frac{E_f}{c}\).
  • \(h\) versus \(\hbar\): \(p = \frac{h}{\lambda}\) uses \(h\); \(p = \hbar k\) uses \(\hbar = \frac{h}{2\pi}\). Mixing them is off by \(2\pi\).
  • For momentum in kg·m/s, use \(h\) in J·s and \(\lambda\) in metres. An eV-based \(h\) gives eV·s/m.
  • Shorter wavelength means larger momentum. X-ray photons carry far more momentum than visible ones.

Section 9X-ray scattering and the Compton shift

Classical expectation (lecture 9b page 12). When an EM wave scatters off the atoms of a material, it is re-radiated at the same frequency, so the incident and scattered wavelengths should be equal, \(\lambda_1 = \lambda_2\).

The experiment (pages 13 and 14). X-rays of wavelength \(\lambda\) from a source pass through collimating slits and hit a graphite target. A detector swings around the target and measures the scattered X-rays at scattering angle \(\theta\) (measured from the original direction). The scattered spectrum shows:

  • At \(\theta = 0^\circ\): a single peak at the original \(\lambda\).
  • At \(\theta = 45^\circ\): two peaks. One at \(\lambda\) and one shifted to a longer wavelength \(\lambda'\), with \(\Delta\lambda \approx 6\times10^{-4}\ \text{nm}\) on the lecture sketch.
  • At \(\theta = 90^\circ\): the shifted peak is further out and taller, \(\Delta\lambda \approx 2.2\times10^{-3}\ \text{nm}\) on the sketch.

The shift in wavelength is called the Compton shift. Compton explained it with Einstein's photons (page 15): the incoming photon collides with a valence electron in the graphite, hands over some of its energy and momentum, and leaves as a scattered photon with less energy, so \(\lambda' > \lambda\). Because the photon loses energy, this is inelastic scattering. The result (derived in Section 10) is:

\[ \Delta\lambda = \lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_0 c}\,(1 - \cos\theta) = \lambda_c\,(1-\cos\theta) \]
Compton shift
\(\lambda\) = incident X-ray wavelength, in m or nm · \(\lambda'\) = scattered wavelength, in m or nm · \(\theta\) = scattering angle of the photon, in degrees · \(m_0\) = electron rest mass, \(9.109\times10^{-31}\) kg · \(\lambda_c = \frac{h}{m_0c} \approx 0.00243\ \text{nm}\) = Compton wavelength of the electron
Example (lecture 9b page 14): the shifts at 0°, 45° and 90°
\(\lambda_c = 0.00243\ \text{nm}\). Angles from the lecture's three spectra.
\[ \begin{aligned} \theta = 0^\circ:\quad \Delta\lambda &= 0.00243\,(1 - \cos 0^\circ)\ \text{nm} = \mathbf{0}\\ \theta = 45^\circ:\quad \Delta\lambda &= 0.00243\,(1 - 0.7071)\ \text{nm} = \mathbf{7.12\times10^{-4}\ \text{nm}}\\ \theta = 90^\circ:\quad \Delta\lambda &= 0.00243\,(1 - 0)\ \text{nm} = \mathbf{2.43\times10^{-3}\ \text{nm}} \end{aligned} \]

So at 0° there is no shift (one peak), and the shift grows with angle, as in the sketches.

Δλ = 0, 7.12 × 10⁻⁴ nm and 2.43 × 10⁻³ nm
Filled in

The lecture's sketches label the shifts as \(6\times10^{-4}\) nm (45°) and \(2.2\times10^{-3}\) nm (90°). These read as approximate values from measured spectra; the Compton formula from page 21 gives \(7.12\times10^{-4}\) nm and \(2.43\times10^{-3}\) nm. Use the formula values in calculations.

Analogy

A cue ball (photon) hits a stationary ball (electron). If it barely grazes it (small \(\theta\)), the cue ball keeps almost all its energy. If it bounces off at a large angle, it hands over much more energy and continues slower. For a photon "slower" is impossible, so losing energy shows up as a longer wavelength instead. The analogy breaks because the photon always moves at \(c\).

Tricky bits
  • \(\theta\) is the angle of the scattered photon, measured from the incoming direction, not the electron's recoil angle.
  • The shift \(\Delta\lambda\) does not depend on the incident wavelength. The same 0.00243 nm at 90° is huge for X-rays and invisible for light, which is why Compton used X-rays.
  • Maximum shift is at \(\theta = 180^\circ\) (photon bounces straight back): \(\Delta\lambda = 2\lambda_c = 0.00486\ \text{nm}\).
  • Calculator in degrees mode for \(\cos\theta\).
  • Same symbol, different thing: \(\lambda_c\) here is the Compton wavelength, not the photoelectric cut-off wavelength of Section 7.

Section 10Deriving the Compton formula

Treat the scattering as a collision between two particles and apply two conservation laws (lecture 9b pages 16 to 21). Before: a photon with energy \(E_f\), momentum \(\vec{p}_f\), frequency \(f\), and a relativistic electron at rest with rest energy \(m_0c^2\). After: the photon has \(\tilde{E}_f\), \(\tilde{\vec{p}}_f\), \(f'\) and travels at angle \(\theta\); the electron has total energy \(E\) and momentum \(\vec{p}\).

\[ E_f + m_0c^{2} = \tilde{E}_f + E \]
photon-electron energy balance
\(E_f,\ \tilde{E}_f\) = photon energy before and after, in J · \(m_0c^2\) = electron rest energy, in J · \(E\) = electron's total energy after the collision, in J

Worked derivation (lecture 9b pages 16 to 21): the Compton shift

Step 1: energy. Move the photon terms together and square both sides (page 17):

\[ \begin{aligned} E &= (E_f - \tilde{E}_f) + m_0c^{2}\\ \left[(E_f - \tilde{E}_f) + m_0c^{2}\right]^{2} &= E^{2} \end{aligned} \]

The electron obeys \(E^{2} = p^{2}c^{2} + m_0^{2}c^{4}\). Expand the left side:

\[ \begin{aligned} (E_f - \tilde{E}_f)^{2} + m_0^{2}c^{4} + 2m_0c^{2}(E_f - \tilde{E}_f) &= p^{2}c^{2} + m_0^{2}c^{4}\\ E_f^{2} - 2\tilde{E}_fE_f + \tilde{E}_f^{2} + 2m_0c^{2}E_f - 2m_0c^{2}\tilde{E}_f &= p^{2}c^{2} \end{aligned} \]

(the \(m_0^{2}c^{4}\) terms cancel, page 18). Divide by \(c^{2}\) and regroup:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{E_f^{2}}{c^{2}} - \frac{2\tilde{E}_fE_f}{c^{2}} + \frac{\tilde{E}_f^{2}}{c^{2}} + 2m_0E_f - 2m_0\tilde{E}_f &= p^{2}\\ \left[\frac{E_f}{c} - \frac{\tilde{E}_f}{c}\right]^{2} + 2m_0c\left[\frac{E_f}{c} - \frac{\tilde{E}_f}{c}\right] &= p^{2} \end{aligned} \]

Since a photon has \(p_f = \frac{E_f}{c}\) (page 19):

\[ (p_f - \tilde{p}_f)^{2} + 2m_0c\,(p_f - \tilde{p}_f) = p^{2} \qquad (\text{A}) \]

Step 2: momentum. From \(\vec{p}_f = \tilde{\vec{p}}_f + \vec{p}\), the electron's momentum is \(\vec{p} = \vec{p}_f - \tilde{\vec{p}}_f\). Square it using the dot product:

\[ \begin{aligned} p^{2} = |\vec{p}|^{2} = \left[\vec{p}_f - \tilde{\vec{p}}_f\right]^{2} &= p_f^{2} + \tilde{p}_f^{2} - 2\,\vec{p}_f\cdot\tilde{\vec{p}}_f\\ &= p_f^{2} + \tilde{p}_f^{2} - 2\,p_f\tilde{p}_f\cos\theta \qquad (\text{B}) \end{aligned} \]

Step 3: eliminate \(p^{2}\). Set (A) equal to (B) and expand (page 20):

\[ \begin{aligned} p_f^{2} + \tilde{p}_f^{2} - 2\tilde{p}_fp_f + 2m_0c\,p_f - 2m_0c\,\tilde{p}_f &= p_f^{2} + \tilde{p}_f^{2} - 2p_f\tilde{p}_f\cos\theta\\ -\tilde{p}_fp_f + m_0c\left[p_f - \tilde{p}_f\right] &= -p_f\tilde{p}_f\cos\theta \end{aligned} \]

(the squares cancel and everything was divided by 2). Divide by \(\tilde{p}_fp_f\) and by \(m_0c\):

\[ \begin{aligned} -\frac{1}{m_0c} + \frac{p_f - \tilde{p}_f}{\tilde{p}_f\,p_f} &= -\frac{\cos\theta}{m_0c}\\ \frac{1}{\tilde{p}_f} - \frac{1}{p_f} &= \frac{1}{m_0c}\left[1 - \cos\theta\right] \end{aligned} \]

Step 4: wavelengths. With \(p_f = \frac{h}{\lambda}\) and \(\tilde{p}_f = \frac{h}{\lambda'}\) (page 21), \(\frac{1}{\tilde{p}_f} = \frac{\lambda'}{h}\) and \(\frac{1}{p_f} = \frac{\lambda}{h}\). Multiply through by \(h\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \lambda' - \lambda &= \frac{h}{m_0c}\left[1 - \cos\theta\right]\\ \lambda_c &= \frac{h}{m_0c} = \frac{6.63\times10^{-34}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{s}}{(9.109\times10^{-31}\ \text{kg})(3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s})} = 2.43\times10^{-12}\ \text{m} = 0.00243\ \text{nm} \end{aligned} \]
\(\Delta\lambda = \lambda' - \lambda = \lambda_c(1 - \cos\theta)\), with \(\lambda_c \approx 0.00243\) nm

Analogy

The derivation is the same bookkeeping as a snooker collision: total energy in equals total energy out, and the momentum arrows before add up to the momentum arrows after. The only special rules are relativity's \(E^{2} = p^{2}c^{2} + m_0^{2}c^{4}\) for the electron and \(p = \frac{E}{c}\) for the massless photon. The analogy fails at the end: a snooker ball slows down, a photon's wavelength stretches.

Tricky bits
  • The electron must be treated relativistically: use \(E^{2} = p^{2}c^{2} + m_0^{2}c^{4}\), not \(\frac{p^2}{2m}\).
  • \(E\) in the energy balance is the electron's total energy (rest + kinetic), not its kinetic energy.
  • The cross term comes from the dot product: \(\vec{p}_f\cdot\tilde{\vec{p}}_f = p_f\tilde{p}_f\cos\theta\). Forgetting the factor 2 in \(-2p_f\tilde{p}_f\cos\theta\) breaks the cancellation.
  • The result is \(\frac{1}{\tilde{p}_f} - \frac{1}{p_f}\) (after minus before), which turns into \(\lambda' - \lambda\), a positive shift.

Section 11Single-slit diffraction minima

The Lecture 9a practice problem goes back to wave optics from the previous lecture. Light of wavelength \(\lambda\) passing through one slit of width \(a\) spreads out, and the pattern on a screen has dark points (minima) where waves from different parts of the slit cancel. Minima sit at angles that satisfy:

\[ a\sin\theta_m = m\lambda, \qquad m = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \]
single-slit minima (dark points)
\(a\) = slit width, in m · \(\theta_m\) = angle of the \(m\)-th minimum from the central axis, in degrees · \(m\) = order of the minimum (whole number, no unit; \(m = 0\) is the bright centre, not a minimum) · \(\lambda\) = wavelength, in m

Because \(\sin\theta \le 1\), the highest order that can appear is the largest whole number \(m\) with \(m \le \frac{a}{\lambda}\). Minima come in pairs, one on each side of the centre, so the total count on the screen is twice that.

Analogy

Water waves going through a gap in a harbour wall. A narrow gap makes the waves fan out widely; a wide gap lets them through in a tighter beam. The dark spots are directions where ripples from one half of the gap arrive exactly out of step with ripples from the other half. The analogy is close; the main difference is scale, since light needs a gap only a few wavelengths wide.

Tricky bits
  • For the count, round \(\frac{a}{\lambda}\) down (4.243 gives 4), then double for both sides. Rounding up would put a minimum beyond 90°, which does not exist.
  • \(m = 0\) is the central bright maximum, not a minimum. Single-slit minima start at \(m = 1\).
  • Don't confuse with the double-slit bright-fringe formula \(d\sin\theta = m\lambda\): same shape, different meaning (\(d\) is slit separation and those are maxima).
  • A narrower slit gives a wider pattern (bigger angles) and fewer minima.
  • Convert nm and µm to metres (or at least to the same unit) before dividing.
