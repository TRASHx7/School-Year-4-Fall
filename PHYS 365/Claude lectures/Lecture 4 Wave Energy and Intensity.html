Wave energy, intensity, decibels and impedance

A wave carries energy, not stuff. Lecture 4a works out how fast a wave on a string (or in air) delivers that energy. Lecture 4b follows the energy as it spreads out and gets absorbed, measures it in decibels, and asks what happens when it hits a new medium.

About the numbers: these lectures are derivations, with one data point (busy street traffic, 70 dB ↔ 10−5 W/m²). Every lecture derivation is here step by step. Where a calculator or worked example needs numbers the lecture doesn't give, they are marked practice numbers.

Part 1 · Energy on a string (Lecture 4a)

01Where the energy lives

A travelling wave moves kinetic and potential energy along the string. Each little piece of string, of mass dm, bobs up and down in simple harmonic motion while the wave shape slides along x.

  • Kinetic energy depends on how fast the piece moves vertically, uy. At a crest (point ② in the lecture) it stops for an instant: uy = 0, KE = 0. Crossing y = 0 (point ①) it moves fastest: KE max.
  • Potential energy here is elastic: it comes from the string being stretched. At a crest the string is flat, so it is barely stretched: PE = 0 (minimum stretch). Crossing y = 0 the string is steepest, so it is stretched most: PE max.

So KE and PE peak at the same places (the zero crossings) and vanish together (the crests and troughs).

y = ym sin(kx − ωt) KE ∝ uy² ∝ cos²(kx − ωt),   PE ∝ (∂y/∂x)² ∝ cos²(kx − ωt)
Try it: watch the energy ride along the wave

Arrows show each element's vertical velocity uy. Notice the arrows are biggest, and the string steepest, at the same spots.
Analogy · a stadium wave

In a stadium "wave", nobody runs around the stadium. Each person just stands up and sits down. The pattern travels, and so does the effort. A person in the middle of standing up is moving fastest (most KE) and is also the one tugging hardest on their neighbours' arms (most stretch, most PE). The person at the very top is momentarily still and their arms hang level with the neighbours who are also near the top.

Tricky bits
  • This is not a mass on a spring. In a spring oscillator, KE is max where PE is zero. On a travelling wave both are max at y = 0 and both are zero at the crests. The energy of one element is not constant: it is handed along to the next element.
  • PE is not gravitational. The crest is highest but has zero PE. The PE is the stretch energy stored by the tension τ.
  • Transverse, not longitudinal. The notes write "(longitudinal)" next to the y-axis motion, but a string element moving along y while the wave moves along x is a transverse wave. Sound (§5) is the longitudinal case.
  • uy (element speed, up/down) is not v (wave speed, along x).

02The rate of kinetic energy transfer

We want energy per second carried past a point. Start with the KE of one element, write its mass and speed using the wave function, then divide by dt. The trick: dx/dt is the wave speed v, because in time dt the wave pattern sweeps across a length dx = v dt of string.

Analogy · a flashing conveyor belt

Picture energy riding past you on a conveyor belt in clumps (the cos² humps). Instantaneously the delivery rate flickers between zero and a maximum. Over a full cycle, though, the cos² humps fill exactly half of the box between 0 and 1, so the steady average is half the peak.

Tricky bits
  • Where did v come from? From dx/dt. The element itself doesn't move along x; it's the wave front sweeping new string into motion at speed v.
  • Peak vs average. The peak rate is ½μvω²ym². The average KE rate is half of that, ¼μvω²ym².
  • Squares everywhere. Double ym or ω and the rate goes up 4×.
  • The minus sign in uy disappears when you square it.

03Elastic potential energy

A piece of string that was length dx gets tilted into length ds. The tension τ does work stretching it by dℓ = ds − dx, and that work is stored as elastic PE, dV. For small slopes we use √(1 + x) ≈ 1 + x/2.

ds = √(dx² + dy²) ≈ dx [1 + ½(dy/dx)²] dV = τ dℓ = τ2(dy/dx)² dx dVdtave = ¼ μω²vym² same as the KE average

Analogy · a rubber band on a ramp

Lay a rubber band flat and it's relaxed. Pin its ends to the bottom and top of a ramp of the same horizontal length and it must stretch to cover the hypotenuse. The steeper the ramp, the more it stretches and the more energy it stores. The tension is what makes stretching "cost" energy, which is why the lecture writes "thanks to τ!!".

Tricky bits
  • Stretch depends on slope², not on height. That's why the crest (slope 0) has zero PE.
  • The approximation needs small slopes. √(1 + x) ≈ 1 + x/2 only when x = (dy/dx)² ≪ 1. Slide the demo to slope 1 to see it break.
  • The two identities to memorise: τ = μv² and k = ω/v. They are how ¼τvk²ym² turns into ¼μvω²ym².
  • The lecture heading says "average wave acceleration", but what is being averaged is the rate of PE transfer, dV/dt.

04Total power of a travelling wave on a string

Add the two equal averages. That's the average power, the energy per second the wave carries past any point.

Pave = (dK/dt)ave + (dV/dt)ave = 2(dK/dt)ave = ½ μvω²ym² v = √(τ/μ)  ⇒  Pave = ½ √(τμ) ω²ym²
Lecture derivation: eliminating v
Pave = 2[dK/dt]ave = ½ μvω²ym²
v = √(τ/μ) = √(tension / linear density)
Pave = ½ μ √(τ/μ) ω² ym² = ½ √(τμ) ω² ym²
Worked example practice numbers

A string has μ = 0.05 kg/m and τ = 20 N. A wave with ω = 100 rad/s and ym = 1.0 cm travels on it. Find the average power.

  • v = √(20/0.05) = √400 = 20 m/s.
  • (dK/dt)ave = ¼(0.05)(20)(100)²(0.010)² = ¼(1.0)(10 000)(10−4) = 0.25 W.
  • (dV/dt)ave = the same, 0.25 W.
  • Pave = 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.50 W. Check with ½√(τμ)ω²ym² = ½(1.0)(10 000)(10−4) = 0.50 W. ✓

Analogy · a two-lane highway

Energy travels down the string in two lanes, KE and PE, carrying equal traffic on average. Total power is just both lanes added: twice one lane.

Tricky bits
  • ω, not f. If you're given frequency in Hz, use ω = 2πf before squaring. Forgetting this makes you (2π)² ≈ 39 times too small.
  • Units: ym in metres and μ in kg/m (525 g/m = 0.525 kg/m).
  • Scaling: P ∝ ω², ∝ ym², but only ∝ √τ and ∝ √μ (once v is eliminated).
  • Look ahead: √(τμ) is the string's impedance Z (§12). So Pave = ½Zω²ym².

05Power of a sound wave

Same idea in air. Take a thin slice of air, thickness dx and area A, oscillating back and forth. Its mass is dm = ρ dV = ρA dx. Compare with the string's dm = μ dx: the product ρA plays the role of μ. Swap it in and the power formula is done.

dm = ρ dV = ρA · dx,   ρA = μ unit check: kg/m³ × m² = kg/m Pave = ½ ρAvω²ym²
Worked example practice numbers

A 1000 Hz sound in air (ρ = 1.21 kg/m³, v = 343 m/s) has displacement amplitude ym = 1.0 × 10−8 m. How much power crosses an area A = 2.0 m²?

  • ω = 2π(1000) = 6283 rad/s, so ω² = 3.948 × 107 s−2.
  • ρAv = (1.21)(2.0)(343) = 830.1 kg/s.
  • Pave = ½(830.1)(3.948 × 107)(1.0 × 10−16) = 1.64 × 10−6 W (1.64 μW).
Calculator: power of sound through area A
Analogy · a string made of air

Think of the column of air in front of a speaker as a very fat "string" whose mass per metre is ρA. A thicker column (bigger A) is like a heavier string: shaking it at the same amplitude and frequency takes more power.

Tricky bits
  • ym is the air's displacement amplitude, along the direction of travel (longitudinal), and it is tiny: nanometres to micrometres.
  • Divide by A and you get intensity: P/A = ½ρvω²ym². That's the bridge to Part 2.
  • Remember to square both ω and ym; the powers of ten add up fast.

Part 2 · Spreading out (Lecture 4b)

06Circular, spherical and plane waves

A wavefront is a surface joining points in the same phase (say, all the crests). Neighbouring wavefronts are one wavelength λ apart, and the wave velocity v points perpendicular to them.

  • From a point source on a surface (a pebble in a pond): circular wavefronts.
  • From a point source in 3D (a small speaker): spherical wavefronts.
  • Far from the source, a small patch of a huge sphere looks flat: plane waves with straight-line or planar wavefronts.
Try it: walk away from the source
Analogy · the flat Earth illusion

Earth is a sphere, but your backyard looks flat because it's tiny compared with Earth's radius. A microphone far from a speaker sees the same thing: its little patch of a giant spherical wavefront is effectively a plane.

Tricky bits
  • The spacing between wavefronts stays λ everywhere. What changes with distance is the curvature, not the spacing.
  • "Plane wave" is an approximation that depends on the size of your detector compared with the distance r.

07Intensity of spherical waves and the inverse square law

Intensity is power per unit area, in W/m². A point source emitting power P equally in all directions spreads it over a sphere of area 4πr². With no losses, every sphere gets the same total P, so intensity falls as 1/r².

I = sound wave powerarea of a sphere = P4πr²   [W/m²] IaIb = rb²ra² inverse square law
Lecture derivation: inverse square law
Ia = P/(4π ra²), Ib = P/(4π rb²) same P through both spheres
Ia/Ib = [P/(4π ra²)] / [P/(4π rb²)] = rb²/ra² P and 4π cancel
Calculator: point source
practice numbers P = 10 W, ra = 2 m, rb = 4 m.
Analogy · spray paint

Hold a spray can close to a wall and you get a small, thick spot. Step back to twice the distance and the same paint covers 4× the area, so the coat is ¼ as thick. Paint per second = power; thickness of the coat = intensity.

Tricky bits
  • The ratio is upside-down. Ia/Ib = rb²/ra². Closer point, bigger intensity. Sanity-check every answer this way.
  • Don't forget to square. 3× farther means 9× weaker, not 3×.
  • Power P doesn't change with distance (no losses yet). Only intensity does.

08Intensity of cylindrical waves (line source)

A long line source of length L (a busy road, a train track) sends its power out through the side of a cylinder, not through a sphere. The lateral area of a cylinder of radius r is 2πrL, which grows only like r. So intensity falls as 1/r, more slowly than a point source.

I = sound wave powerarea of lateral surface = P2πrL   [W/m²] IaIb = rbra
Lecture example: train tracks as a line source

A train's vibrating tracks act as a line source. Compare the intensity at point a (radius ra) with point b (radius rb), both on cylinders of the same length L.

Ia = P/(2π ra L), Ib = P/(2π rb L)
Ia/Ib = (rb · 2πL)/(ra · 2πL) 2πL cancels
= rb/ra
Try it: line source vs point source
line source, 1/r (cylindrical)point source, 1/r² (spherical)
Analogy · a soaker hose vs a sprinkler head

A sprinkler head (point) throws water over a growing circle in every direction. A soaker hose (line) only spreads water sideways, away from its length, so the wetting thins out more gently as you walk away.

Tricky bits
  • No square for a line source. 4× farther from the tracks is 4× weaker, not 16×.
  • The notes' cylinder slide repeats the words "point source", but the source there is a line. Only a line source gives cylindrical waves.
  • The ends of the cylinder are ignored; we assume L is long compared with r.

09Acoustic attenuation, and combining it with spreading

Spreading only redistributes energy. Real media also remove it: viscosity always turns some sound energy into heat. That loss is exponential in distance, set by the attenuation coefficient α (units 1/m).

I(r2) = I(r1) e−αΔr,   Δr = |r2 − r1| I2I1 = [r1r2]n−1 e−αΔr n = 2 cylindrical, n = 3 spherical

Read the combined formula as two factors multiplied: ① spreading, (r1/r2)n−1, and ② attenuation, e−αΔr. With n = 3 the spreading factor is (r1/r2)², the inverse square law from §7. With n = 2 it is r1/r2, the line source from §8.

Worked example practice numbers

A point source (spherical, n = 3) in a medium with α = 0.01 m−1. Compare the intensity at r2 = 20 m with r1 = 10 m.

  • Spreading: (10/20)3−1 = (0.5)² = 0.25.
  • Attenuation: Δr = 10 m, e−(0.01)(10) = e−0.1 = 0.905.
  • I2/I1 = 0.25 × 0.905 = 0.226. Spreading did most of the damage here.
Try it: spreading vs absorption
spreading onlyattenuation onlycombined
Analogy · a toll road

Spreading is like a crowd dispersing into a bigger plaza: nobody leaves, they just thin out. Attenuation is a toll booth every metre that takes the same percentage of whatever money you still have. A fixed percentage per metre is exactly what an exponential is.

Tricky bits
  • The exponent is n − 1, not n. Spherical (n = 3) uses a square; cylindrical (n = 2) uses power 1.
  • Δr is a distance difference, |r2 − r1|, not r2.
  • Check units: αΔr must be dimensionless (1/m × m).
  • Near the source spreading usually dominates; very far away, the exponential eventually wins.

Part 3 · Decibels

10Sound level β

Our ears handle intensities over a range of about 1012, so we use a logarithmic loudness scale. The reference is the hearing threshold, the faintest sound we can hear, I0 = 1 × 10−12 W/m². β is dimensionless and expressed in decibels (dB).

β = (10 dB) log10(I/I0),   I0 = 1 × 10−12 W/m² I = I0 10β/10 dB since log10(x) = y ⇒ 10y = x

Analogy · counting zeros

Every 10 dB is "one more zero" on the intensity. 70 dB means 107 times the threshold: a 1 followed by seven zeros. That's why the scale stays in friendly two-digit numbers even though the intensities span twelve powers of ten.

Tricky bits
  • Ratios become additions. ×10 in intensity = +10 dB. ×100 = +20 dB. ×2 ≈ +3 dB.
  • Decibels don't add. Two 70 dB sources give 73 dB, not 140 dB. Add the intensities, then convert.
  • 0 dB is not "no sound". It's I = I0, the threshold. Negative dB is possible (quieter than the threshold).
  • Use log base 10, not ln.

11Change in sound level between two points

Subtract two levels and the I0 cancels: a level difference only depends on the intensity ratio. Plug in the spreading + attenuation ratio from §9 and split the log of a product into a sum.

Δβ = β2 − β1 = (10 dB) log10(I2/I1) Δβ = (10 dB) log10[(r1/r2)n−1 e−αΔr] Δβ = (n − 1)(10 dB) log10(r1/r2) − 4.343 αΔr log10 e = 0.4343
Lecture derivation: splitting the log
β1 = 10 dB log10(I1/I0), β2 = 10 dB log10(I2/I0)
Δβ = β2 − β1 = 10 dB log10(I2/I1) log a − log b = log(a/b)
= 10 dB log10[(r1/r2)n−1 e−αΔr] ratio from §9
= (n−1)(10 dB) log10(r1/r2) + 10 dB · log10(e−αΔr)
log10(e−αΔr) = −αΔr log10e = −0.4343 αΔr
= (n−1)(10 dB) log10(r1/r2) − 4.343 αΔr
Worked example practice numbers, lecture's 70 dB

Traffic noise is 70 dB (the lecture's busy street) at r1 = 10 m. Treat it as a point source (n = 3) in air with α = 0.001 m−1. What is the level at r2 = 100 m?

  • Spreading: (3 − 1)(10 dB) log10(10/100) = 20 × (−1) = −20.00 dB.
  • Attenuation: Δr = 90 m, 4.343 × 0.001 × 90 = 0.39 dB lost.
  • Δβ = −20.00 − 0.39 = −20.39 dB, so β2 = 70 − 20.39 = 49.6 dB.
  • Check the long way: I2 = 10−5 × (0.1)² × e−0.09 = 9.14 × 10−8 W/m², and 10 log10(9.14 × 104) = 49.6 dB ✓.

Analogy · a bank statement in dB

Logs turn multiplying into adding. Instead of "your intensity was multiplied by 0.01 and then by 0.914", the decibel statement reads "−20 dB for spreading, −0.39 dB for absorption". Each loss is its own line item, and you just add them up.

Tricky bits
  • Watch the typo in the notes. The second slide on this topic writes (r2/r1)n−1 inside the log, but the expanded line, and the slide before it, use (r1/r2). The correct one is r1/r2: moving away (r2 > r1) must make Δβ negative.
  • Where 4.343 comes from: 10 × log10 e = 10 × 0.4343. It converts the natural exponent into decibels.
  • Rules of thumb (α = 0): spherical loses 6.02 dB per doubling of distance and 20 dB per ×10. Cylindrical loses 3.01 dB per doubling and 10 dB per ×10.
  • Δβ is a difference; add it to β1 to get β2.

Part 4 · Impedance

12Impedance Z

When a wave reaches the boundary between two media, part of it is reflected and part transmitted. How much of each depends on a property of each medium called its impedance Z. Impedance combines how hard the medium is to accelerate (inertial property) with how hard it springs back (elastic property).

Z = √(inertial property × elastic property) String: Z = √(μτ) linear density × tension Fluid: Z = √(ρB) density × bulk modulus Solid rod: Z = √(ρY) density × Young's modulus

Handy rewrite for the string: since v = √(τ/μ), √(μτ) = μv. (Likewise √(ρB) = ρv for a fluid.) And this is the same √(τμ) that appeared in the power formula of §4: Pave = ½Zω²ym².

practice numbers Material values (water, air, steel) are typical textbook values.
Analogy · how stubborn the medium is

Impedance is a medium's "stubbornness" to being shaken. A heavy, tight rope (big μ and τ) resists your hand far more than a light, slack thread. When a wave moves between two media of very different stubbornness, it can't hand its motion over cleanly, so much of it bounces back.

Tricky bits
  • Product under the root, not a ratio. Speed is √(elastic/inertial); impedance is √(elastic × inertial). Easy to swap on an exam.
  • Units differ by medium. String Z: kg/s. Fluid and rod Z: kg/(m²·s) (often called rayl).
  • What matters at a boundary is whether Z1 and Z2 are different, not how big either one is.

13Boundary conditions: two strings attached

The lecture's example joins string 1 (μ1, τ1, Z1 = √(μ1τ1)) to string 2 (μ2, τ2, Z2 = √(μ2τ2)). An incident pulse travels along string 1 toward the knot. At the knot it splits into a reflected pulse (back along string 1) and a transmitted pulse (on along string 2).

Boundary conditions are rules that must hold at the knot, applied to the displacement of the wave function there. The main one: the string doesn't break, so the displacement just left of the knot equals the displacement just right of it. These conditions are what relate the amplitude ym of the reflected and transmitted waves to the ym of the incident wave.

In the lecture's sketch the reflected pulse comes back upside down and the transmitted pulse continues upright on the green string.

Try it: send a pulse into a knot
string 1 (Z1)string 2 (Z2)
The amplitudes use the standard results r = (Z1 − Z2)/(Z1 + Z2) and t = 2Z1/(Z1 + Z2), which follow from the boundary conditions. Lecture 4b stops before deriving them, so treat them as a preview. Same tension assumed on both strings, so v ∝ 1/Z.
Analogy · bumping into someone

Run into someone much heavier than you (bigger Z) and you bounce back: reflection, inverted. Run into someone much lighter and you keep going while they fly off ahead: the reflection is upright. Run into your twin (equal Z) and all your motion passes on with nothing coming back.

Tricky bits
  • Inverted vs upright. Into a higher-Z medium (heavier string): reflected pulse inverted. Into a lower-Z medium: reflected pulse upright. Equal Z: no reflection.
  • The transmitted pulse is never inverted, because displacement must match on both sides of the knot.
  • Amplitude isn't energy. Going into a lighter string, the transmitted amplitude can be bigger than the incident one, yet it carries less energy, because power ∝ Z × amplitude² (§4).
  • Frequency stays the same across the knot; speed and wavelength change.
