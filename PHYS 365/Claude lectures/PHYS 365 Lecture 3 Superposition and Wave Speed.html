Superposition and wave speed

Two ideas run through these lectures. First, when waves overlap you just add their displacements, and the phase difference φ decides whether they build up or cancel. Second, every mechanical wave's speed follows one rule: v = √(restoring force ÷ inertia). Strings, fluids, gases and solids only differ in what plays those two roles.

Part 1 · Adding waves (Lecture 3a)

01Adding two waves: the superposition formula

Two travelling waves run along the same stretched string. They have the same amplitude ym, the same k and the same ω. The only difference is that the second one is shifted by a phase constant φ:

y1(x,t) = ym sin(kx − ωt) call the bracket α y2(x,t) = ym sin(kx − ωt + φ) call the bracket β

The principle of superposition says the string's actual displacement is simply the sum, point by point and instant by instant: yR = y1 + y2. To tidy the sum up, the lecture uses the sum-to-product identity:

sin α + sin β = 2 sinα + β2 cosα − β2

Here (α + β)/2 = kx − ωt + φ/2 and (α − β)/2 = −φ/2. Cosine doesn't care about the sign, so cos(−φ/2) = cos(φ/2). The result:

yR = [2ym cos(φ/2)] sin(kx − ωt + φ/2) Am = 2ym cos(φ/2) amplitude of the resultant

Read it in two pieces. The sine part is a normal travelling wave with the same k and ω as before, just with phase constant φ/2. The bracket in front is its amplitude, and it is set entirely by φ.

Analogy · two people pushing a swing

Two people push the same swing with equal strength and the same rhythm. If they push at the same moment (φ = 0), the swing goes twice as high. If one pushes exactly when the other pulls (φ = π), nothing happens. Anything in between gives something in between, and cos(φ/2) is the exact "how in sync are they" score.

Try it: two waves and their sum
y1y2yR = y1 + y2
Tricky bits
  • φ/2 shows up twice. Once inside the cosine (amplitude) and once inside the sine (phase constant). Students often write φ in one of them.
  • The amplitude is the size of 2ymcos(φ/2). If cos(φ/2) is negative (φ between π and 3π), the minus sign just flips the wave. Report a positive amplitude.
  • This tidy formula needs equal amplitudes and the same k and ω. With different k or ω the sum is not a single sinusoid any more.
  • Superposition doesn't change either wave. Each keeps travelling as if the other weren't there; only the string's total displacement is the sum.

02Fully in step and fully out of step

The lecture sketches the two extreme cases.

φ = 0: constructive

With y1 = ym1 sin(kx − ωt) and y2 = ym2 sin(kx − ωt), the crests line up exactly. Every point's displacements add, so the resultant has amplitude ym1 + ym2. (The amplitudes don't even have to be equal for this.)

φ = π: destructive

With y2 = ym sin(kx − ωt + π), wave 2 is wave 1 flipped upside down. Where wave 1 has +ym, wave 2 has −ym, and ym − ym = 0. The lecture's picture: a flat string. The waves cancel off.

φ = 0:  A = ym1 + ym2  (equal: 2ym) φ = π:  A = |ym1 − ym2|  (equal: 0)

Analogy · noise-cancelling headphones

Headphones play a copy of the outside noise shifted by π. Your eardrum receives the sum, which is (nearly) nothing. Both sound waves still exist in the air; they just add to zero where your ear is.

Try it: unequal amplitudes, φ = 0 or π
Starts at the lecture's page 3 case: equal amplitudes, φ = π, flat string. Slide the amplitudes apart to see that cancellation is only complete when they match.
Tricky bits
  • φ = 2π is the same as φ = 0. A shift of a whole cycle lines the crests up again. The formula agrees: |2ymcos(π)| = 2ym.
  • φ = π only gives a flat string if the amplitudes are equal. With 3 mm and 5 mm you're left with 2 mm.
  • "Flat" means flat at every instant, not just at one moment. That's because both waves move together at the same speed.

03Worked example: Exercise 1

The problem (lecture 3a, p. 4). Two sinusoidal waves travel on a string with A1 = A2 = 9 mm. They have the same λ and ω: λ1 = λ2 = 0.4 m. Their resultant has AR = 4 mm, has λR = λ1 = λ2, and travels in the negative x-direction by 56 cm in 8 ms. If y1 = ym sin(kx ± ωt + φ1) with φ1 = 0, find ym, k, ω, φ and the sign in front of ω for the resultant wave.

Full solution, step by step

a) Amplitude. Write both waves with the unknown sign:

y1 = ym sin(kx ± ωt),   y2 = ym sin(kx ± ωt + φ) A1 = A2 = ym = 9 × 10−3 m

b) Wave number. The resultant has the same wavelength, λR = 0.4 m, so it shares k:

k2 = 2πλ2 = 2π0.4 m = 15.7 ≈ 16 rad/m = kR

c) Speed, then angular frequency. The resultant moves 56 cm in 8 ms:

vR = ΔxΔt = 0.56 m8 × 10−3 s = 70 m/s vR = ωRkR → ω2 = ωR = (70)(16) = 1120 rad/s

d) Phase constant and sign. Use the superposition result with the + sign:

yR = 2ym cos(φ/2) sin(kRx + ωRt + φ/2) AR = 2(9 mm) cos(φ/2) = 4 mm cos(φ/2) = 4/18 = 0.222 → φ/2 = 1.347 rad → φ = 2.69 rad

The sign in front of ω is +, because the resultant travels in the (−) x-direction.

Putting it together: yR = (4 mm) sin(16x + 1120t + 1.35), with x in m and t in s.

Analogy · reading a race result backwards

You're told how far the runner went and how long it took (speed), and how long each stride is (λ). From those you rebuild the cadence (ω = vk). The amplitude clue works the same way: the final height of 4 mm tells you how badly out of sync the two waves were.

Tricky bits
  • Rounding k changes ω. 2π/0.4 = 15.7 rad/m. The lecture rounds to 16 and gets 1120 rad/s. Keeping 15.7 gives 1100 rad/s. Either is fine if you show the work; untick the box in the calculator to see both.
  • Convert units first. 56 cm → 0.56 m and 8 ms → 8 × 10−3 s, or you're off by a factor of 1000 or 100.
  • Sign rule: kx − ωt moves toward +x, kx + ωt moves toward −x. Ask: "to keep the phase constant as t grows, which way must x go?"
  • arccos gives φ/2, not φ. Double it. And put your calculator in radians.

Part 2 · Sound and strings (Lecture 3a)

04The travelling sound wave: displacement and pressure

Sound in a pipe is a longitudinal wave: the air molecules jiggle back and forth along the direction the wave travels. Where they bunch up you get a compression (higher pressure); where they spread out you get a rarefaction (lower pressure). The lecture describes the same wave in two ways:

s(x,t) = sm cos(kx − ωt) longitudinal displacement ΔP(x,t) = ΔPm sin(kx − ωt) pressure change ΔPm = (vρω) sm v = wave speed, ρ = fluid density

One is a cosine and the other a sine, so they are a quarter cycle (90°) apart. Where the molecules are displaced the most, they are momentarily at the edge of their swing and the pressure change is zero. The pressure change is largest where the displacement is zero, because that's where neighbours on either side are pushed toward (or pulled away from) the same spot.

Unit check on ΔPm = vρω sm: (m/s)(kg/m³)(1/s)(m) = kg/(m·s²) = N/m² = Pa. That's a pressure, as it should be.

Analogy · a crowd doing "the wave" sideways

Picture a queue of people who each shuffle forward and back. A person at the far end of their shuffle (biggest displacement) isn't squeezed: their neighbours shuffled the same way. The squeeze is worst at the person standing in their normal spot while the people behind are pushed forward and the people ahead are pushed back toward them.

Tricky bits
  • The displacement graph is drawn going up and down, but the motion is along x. The graph's height means "how far left or right."
  • ΔP is the change from normal air pressure, not the total pressure. ΔPm is usually a few pascals, against about 105 Pa ambient.
  • ΔPm uses ω = 2πf, not f. Plugging f in makes your answer 2π too small.
  • Because of the 90° shift: s maximum ⇔ ΔP = 0, and s = 0 ⇔ |ΔP| maximum.

05Wave speed on a stretched string

Two things set how fast a pulse runs along a string:

  • Linear density μ (mass per length). More mass per metre means more inertia: each bit of string responds more sluggishly, so the wave is slower.
  • Tension τ. Higher tension means a stronger pull back toward straight, so the wave is faster.
v = √(τ/μ),   μ = mL linear density (kg/m)

The lecture's sketch of a moving pulse shows why: at each point on the hump the tension pulls along the string on both sides. Those two pulls don't quite cancel, and the leftover τnet points toward the straight line. That net pull is the restoring force; μ is the inertia it fights.

Analogy · a tug-of-war rope vs a slinky

Flick a tight, light fishing line and the bump is gone in an instant. Flick a heavy, saggy rope and you can watch the bump crawl. Tight and light is fast; slack and heavy is slow.

Tricky bits
  • τ here is tension (newtons), not torque and not a period.
  • Grams to kilograms. μ must be in kg/m. Using g/m makes v about 31.6× too small (√1000).
  • Square root. Quadrupling τ only doubles v.
  • v belongs to the string, not the wave. Shaking faster changes f and λ, not v. When a wave enters a different string, f stays the same (set by the source) and λ = v/f changes.

06Deriving the wave equation for a string

This is where v = √(τ/μ) comes from. Zoom in on a tiny piece of string between x and x + Δx. Tension τ1 pulls its left end (down and left), and τ2 pulls its right end (up and right), each along the string's direction at that end.

The derivation, step by step (lecture pp. 10 to 12)

1. x-components. The string only moves up and down, and the slopes are small, so the horizontal pulls balance:

τ1x = τ2x = τ

2. y-components. The net vertical force is

τy = τ2y − τ1y   ①

3. Slopes. Each tension points along the string, so its components are in the same ratio as the slope:

At x:  (∂y/∂x)x = τ1y/τ1x = τ1y/τ   ② At x + Δx:  (∂y/∂x)x+Δx = τ2y/τ   ③

4. Put ② and ③ into ①:

τy = τ(∂y/∂x)x+Δx − τ(∂y/∂x)x

5. Newton's law. τy = m ay, and the segment's mass is m = μΔx (μ = m/Δx, the segment is nearly a straight line). Divide both sides by τΔx:

(∂y/∂x)x+Δx − (∂y/∂x)xΔx = μτ ay

6. Let Δx → 0. The left side is the change in slope per length, which is ∂²y/∂x². And ay = ∂²y/∂t²:

∂²y∂x² = μτ ∂²y∂t²

7. Compare with the general wave equation ∂²y/∂x² = (1/v²) ∂²y/∂t². Matching gives 1/v² = μ/τ, so v = √(τ/μ).

Try it: slope difference = net force
String shape y = (5 cm) sin(2πx/1 m), Δx = 0.08 m. Arrows are drawn steeper than real so you can see them.
Analogy · a hammock

A straight, tight rope feels no sideways push: both ends pull in exactly opposite directions. Bend it (curvature) and the two end pulls now point slightly the same way, giving a net push back toward straight. More bend, more push. That's exactly "acceleration ∝ ∂²y/∂x²."

Tricky bits
  • Slope alone gives no force. A tilted but straight segment has equal slopes at both ends, so τy = 0. You need a change in slope.
  • Coefficient is 1/v², not v². If an equation reads ∂²y/∂x² = C ∂²y/∂t², then v = 1/√C.
  • The derivation assumes small slopes, so τx ≈ τ along the whole string.
  • At a crest the curvature is negative, so the segment accelerates down; in a trough it accelerates up.

Part 3 · Speed of sound in matter (Lecture 3b)

07Wave speed in a fluid: bulk modulus

For every mechanical wave:

wave speed = (restoring forceinertia)1/2 = √(restoring forceinertia)

In a fluid, the restoring force comes from how hard it is to compress. That stiffness is the bulk modulus B: squeeze the fluid with an extra pressure ΔP, see how much its volume changes.

B = −V0 ΔPΔV   or   ΔP = −B ΔV/V0 V0 = initial volume B = −V0 dPdV small changes v = √(B/ρ) ρ = m/ΔV, the inertia

The minus sign is there because a positive change in pressure gives a negative change in volume. With it, B comes out positive. Units: ΔV/V0 has none, so B has the units of pressure, Pa.

Compare with the string: v = √(tension/linear density) became v = √(B/ρ). Same recipe, different ingredients.

Analogy · a spring and a bowling ball

B is the stiffness of the spring, ρ is the heaviness of the ball on it. A stiff spring snaps back fast; a heavy ball slows everything down. Water is very stiff (hard to compress) and fairly heavy; air is very soft and very light.

Tricky bits
  • Don't drop the minus sign when solving for ΔV: more pressure means ΔV < 0.
  • B is not pressure itself. It has pressure units, but it's a property of the material (how much pressure per fractional squeeze).
  • ρ here is volume density (kg/m³), unlike the string's μ (kg/m).
  • Forgetting the square root gives absurd speeds like 2 × 106 m/s for mercury. Sanity check: liquids are around 1000 to 1500 m/s.

08Proof: ΔPm = (vρω) sm

Section 4 stated how the pressure amplitude relates to the displacement amplitude. Here's where it comes from. Take a thin slab of fluid in a pipe of cross-section A, between x and x + Δx. When the wave passes, its two faces move by different amounts, s(x,t) and s(x + Δx,t), so the slab's volume changes.

The proof, step by step (lecture 3b pp. 4 to 6)

1. Change in volume.

ΔV = A[s(x + Δx, t) − s(x, t)] = A Δx ∂s∂x using  ∂s∂x = limΔx→0 s(x + Δx) − s(x)Δx

2. Fractional change. The initial volume is V = AΔx, so

ΔVV = AΔx ∂s/∂xAΔx = ∂s∂x   ①

3. Bulk modulus. From §7, ΔV/V = −ΔP/B   ②. Set ① = ②:

−ΔP/B = ∂s/∂x

4. Plug in the wave. Let s(x,t) = sm cos(kx − ωt):

−ΔP/B = ∂/∂x[sm cos(kx − ωt)] = −k sm sin(kx − ωt)

5. Match to ΔP = ΔPm sin(kx − ωt). The sines cancel:

−ΔPm/B = −k sm  →  ΔPm = B k sm

6. Use B = ρv² (from v = √(B/ρ)):

ΔPm = ρv²k sm

7. Last step (not written in the notes): v k = ω, so ρv²k = ρv(vk) = vρω, giving ΔPm = (vρω) sm. ∎

Check it: two routes, one answer
Example: air at 0 °C (standard values). Both lines always agree, which is the whole point of the proof.
Analogy · an accordion

If both ends of an accordion move left by the same amount, it doesn't squeeze at all; it just slides. It only squeezes when one end moves more than the other. That difference per length is ∂s/∂x, and it's what sets the pressure.

Tricky bits
  • The notes write the derivative in step 4 as ∂/∂s. It should be ∂/∂x (you differentiate s with respect to position).
  • The sketch on p. 4 labels the slab under +ΔP as "expands." By the sign in B, a positive ΔP means the slab shrinks (ΔV < 0). The algebra works either way, because the sign is carried by ∂s/∂x.
  • Why a sine from a cosine? Differentiating cos gives −sin. That's the 90° shift between s and ΔP from §4.
  • Two equivalent final forms: B k sm and vρω sm. Use whichever matches the data you're given.

09Speed of sound in an ideal gas, and the Mars example

For an ideal gas, the bulk modulus and density can both be written in terms of temperature, and they combine into:

v = √(γRT/M) T: absolute temperature (K)  ·  R = 8.31 J/(mol·K)  ·  M: molar mass (kg/mol) γ = CP/CV: 1.67 monatomic, 1.40 diatomic, 1.3 triatomic M = NA · m,   NA = 6.022 × 1023 m = mass of one molecule (kg)

Hotter gas means faster molecules, which pass the push along faster. Heavier molecules (bigger M) are more sluggish. Pressure doesn't appear at all.

Example (lecture 3b, p. 8): speed of sound on Mars. A typical Martian morning has T = −100 °C, and the atmosphere is (taken as) entirely CO2. Find (i) the speed of sound that morning and (ii) the approximate T (K) at which the speed would double.

Full solution

(i) CO2 is triatomic, so γ = 1.3.

T = 273 − 100 = 173 K MCO₂ = MC + 2MO = 12 g/mol + 2(16 g/mol) = 44 g/mol = 0.044 kg/mol v = √(γRT/MCO₂) = √[(1.3)(8.31)(173)/0.044] = √42 475 = 206 ≈ 210 m/s

(ii) The notes pose this but don't work it, so here is the solution. Everything except T is fixed, so v ∝ √T. To double v, T must go up by 2² = 4:

T′ = 4 × 173 K = 692 K  (about 419 °C)

Analogy · passing a rumour

Sound is a push handed from molecule to molecule. Warm, light molecules are fast messengers. Cold, heavy CO2 on a Martian morning is a slow one, which is why sound there moves at about 60% of its speed in Earth air.

Tricky bits
  • Kelvin, always. T = −100 °C is 173 K. A negative T under a square root is a sure sign you forgot.
  • M in kg/mol. Using 44 instead of 0.044 makes v √1000 ≈ 31.6× too small.
  • Doubling v needs 4× T, not 2×. Speed goes as √T.
  • γ for CO2 is 1.3 (triatomic). Air is mostly N2 and O2, so 1.40.
  • The lecture rounds 206 m/s to "≈ 210 m/s". Either is fine with the work shown.

10Wave speed in a solid

In a solid rod, the restoring force comes from the material's stiffness when stretched or squashed: Young's modulus Y (the elastic modulus). Sound travels faster in stiffer materials.

v = √(Y/ρ) ρ = material density Y = σ/ε,   σ = F/A (stress),   ε = ΔL/L (strain) e.g. Al: Y = 7 × 1010 N/m²

Pull on a rod of length L with force F over cross-section A (stress σ) and it stretches by ΔL (strain ε). Y is the ratio: how much stress you need per unit of fractional stretch.

Analogy · a stiff ruler vs a wet noodle

Tap one end of a metal ruler and the other end feels it almost instantly: the atoms are linked by very stiff springs. A noodle is soft, so the push takes its time getting through.

Tricky bits
  • Strain has no units (m/m), so Y has stress units: N/m² = Pa, same as B.
  • Stiffer isn't automatically faster. Steel is about 3× stiffer than aluminium but also about 3× denser, so their speeds are nearly equal. Always look at the ratio Y/ρ.
  • Solids beat gases by a lot because their modulus is ~105 to 106 times bigger, while their density is only ~103 times bigger.
  • The notes give Y for aluminium but not its density. Use ρ = 2700 kg/m³ unless a problem gives you one.
