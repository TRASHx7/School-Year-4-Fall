General Course Information - All You Need to Know

This page summarizes the general expectations for ENEL 500 Capstone. The course is intentionally run like a real engineering project: information posted on D2L, instructions provided through course channels, and communication with the teaching team should be treated as part of the course "contract." Students are responsible for reading, understanding, and following the posted terms and expectations.

Course goals and objectives
ENEL 500 is designed to give you hands-on experience with the major steps involved in an engineering project. You will apply engineering knowledge, learn new skills as needed, and work toward a solution that is accountable to an external stakeholder.
By the end of the course, you should have practiced how to:
1. Identify a problem and envision a solution.
2. Form a team and recruit the skills needed to move the project forward.
3. Explore alternative solutions before committing to a design path.
4. Implement the best feasible solution within time, resource, and budget constraints.
5. Analyze and mitigate technical, delivery, and project-management risks.
6. Manage project time, resources, and budget.
7. Develop, test, validate, and finalize the product or deliverable.
8. Document objectives, progress, activities, decisions, and design details.
9. Communicate professionally with your team and external parties.
10. Be accountable to an external stakeholder.

Capstone Projects - All You Need to Know

Required project stream
Industry-sponsored projects only.

What counts as an industry-sponsored project?
A project jointly defined and scoped by your team and an external industry sponsor to solve a real problem in the sponsor's line of work. The deliverable must be directly relevant to the sponsor and within the technical scope of Electrical & Software Engineering (ESE).

Who can sponsor?
Any organization whose problem aligns with ESE knowledge: engineering and high-tech firms, utilities, oil & gas, food and health sectors, public institutions, and research labs. Sponsors do not need to be in Alberta; local sponsors are preferred for easier meetings and communication.

How to find a sponsor (practical starting points)
- Previous employers (internships, co-ops, summer jobs).
- Professional & personal networks (friends, relatives, classmates, community contacts).
- Internships: your host company may sponsor next year's capstone.
- Help each other: if your internship employer isn't a fit for your team, they might be perfect for a classmate's team.
- Why sponsors say yes: the course organizes focused teams, produces prototypes/analyses tied to the sponsor's needs, and can seed follow-on work.

Will the instructor provide projects?
- Short answer: no. It is your responsibility to find projects and form a team.
- Occasional opportunities: At times, local industry contacts share potential project ideas. When this happens, the course coordinator will post them as they come in via course channels.
- Do not wait for postings: The number, scope, and timing of such opportunities are unpredictable, so you should actively pursue your own sponsor.

CEAB design requirement - what your capstone must include
CEAB requires that the engineering curriculum culminate in a significant design experience that builds on prior coursework and, preferably, includes teamwork and project management.
"The engineering curriculum must culminate in a significant design experience which is based on the knowledge and skills acquired in earlier course work and which preferably gives students an exposure to the concepts of teamwork and project management." - Canadian Engineering Accreditation Board (CEAB)
CEAB defines engineering design as:
"Engineering design integrates mathematics, basic sciences, engineering sciences and complementary studies in developing elements, systems, and processes to meet specific needs."

Quick eligibility checklist (use this before you submit)
- Engineering design is central: you are creating/adapting an element, system, or process to meet a stated need (not just analyzing or testing).
- Teamwork is integral: a multi-person team with defined roles and collaboration.
- Project management is practiced: scope, schedule, milestones, risk, and stakeholder communication.
If you can genuinely check all three, you're on the right track for ESE capstone eligibility.

Important rules
- University research labs may sponsor only if the lab proposes the project. Students should not solicit projects from professors.
- Teams with their own idea without an industry sponsor (a new product/service with business potential) must register in ENGG 503/504 instead.
- Multi-department teams must register in ENGG 501/502.

Process & feedback
- The course coordinator provides feedback only after your proposal is officially submitted and reviewed with the full set of proposals.
- Borderline projects will receive specific tips and guidelines to elevate them to the required level during the late-September review.

Fit & scope check (before you commit)
- The problem statement and deliverables align with ESE capabilities.
- A sponsor contact is available for periodic check-ins and feedback.
- Any confidentiality or IP needs can be handled through standard course processes.

Examples to guide your scoping
- Qualifies: designing and prototyping an embedded control system for a sponsor's device; developing and validating a software-hardware data pipeline that meets performance and reliability requirements; re-engineering a process with quantitative trade-off analysis and verification.
- Does not qualify (by itself): literature reviews, routine maintenance, pure data collection, or unstructured coding without defined requirements and verification.

When reaching out to potential sponsors and asking to provide a project, make sure you provide them with the attached letter to potential sponsor. This should help answer some of their main questions. If they have addition questions, ask them to reach out to the course instructor directly.

Capstone Roles & Responsibilities - All You Need to Know
This page outlines who does what in your industry-sponsored capstone project and how to work together effectively.

--- Project Manager (a.k.a. Team Manager) ---
Purpose: Owns logistics and coordination so the team can focus on technical work.
Important clarification: The Project Manager role does not exclude the manager from day-to-day technical work. Treat PM duties as an additional layer of responsibility undertaken alongside technical contributions; the benefit is practical leadership experience.

Core responsibilities (non-exhaustive):
- Plan and run meetings (agenda, minutes, action items, owners, due dates).
- Maintain the project plan (scope, schedule/milestones, risks, comms cadence).
- Coordinate with the industry sponsor representative, academic advisor, TA, and instructor.
- Ensure deliverables (proposal, design roadmap, reviews, demo, final report) are submitted on time and in the required formats.
- Track decisions and change control; keep stakeholders informed.

Rotation:
- The Project Manager role should rotate among team members. You may plan rotations across Fall and Winter terms; the team decides timing.
- If you choose one PM for the entire project, that student still participates in technical work; PM duties are additional. The benefit: practical leadership/management experience.

Re-evaluation:
- Any team member may request a PM change review at any time. The team must take such requests seriously and decide promptly.

Issue escalation:
- Concerns about the PM -> discuss with your TA first. If unresolved -> engage the course instructor.
- Shared accountability: The health and progress of the project is everyone's responsibility.
- Important: Unresolved disputes left until the end of term may lead to penalties for all team members. Be proactive.

--- Technical Academic Advisor (faculty/graduate delegate) ---
Purpose: Ensure your technical decisions are sound and that the solution meets ESE standards.

What "formal advisor" means:
- A committed advisor for the full academic year who will evaluate the team's technical solutions and attend three evaluation sessions.
- Typical workload: about 4-5 hours across the year. Advisors do not mark reports; they provide technical guidance, endorse the technical soundness of your design, and attend the fall, winter, and final oral project evaluation sessions.

Who can serve as an academic advisor:
- Any academic member of the faculty with relevant expertise (assistant professor, associate professor, professor, lecturer, senior lecturer, or equivalent teaching-stream role) may serve as an advisor.
- The advisor does not necessarily have to be from ESE. Expertise is what matters.
- The academic may designate a graduate student (PhD or Master's) to act as the team's advisor under their oversight.
- Advisors may come from any relevant department across the University of Calgary, not only ESE.
- Teams may have multiple advisors (formal and/or informal); however, at least one formal academic advisor is required.

Minimum requirement & deadline:
- By the time you submit the Project Information Google Form (typically late September to mid-October; see course timeline), your team must have at least one formal academic advisor.
- If you do not secure an advisor by the deadline, you must drop the course.

Working with your advisor:
- Keep the advisor in the loop: share your design roadmap, major decisions, and updates.
- Consult them when making important design choices; ensure they agree with the approach.
- You may engage additional advisors (faculty or grad students) formally or informally if helpful. Between your TA and advisor(s) there should be adequate technical coverage for guidance and evaluation.

Advisor expectations (minimum):
- Ensures the team's technical decisions are sound.
- Available for both Fall and Winter terms.
- Attends the fall, winter, and final oral evaluation sessions.
- Meets with the team as needed, when requested by the team, to guide them through the project.
- Switching advisors: If suitability becomes an issue, speak with the instructor immediately.

How to find an advisor (practical steps):
- Identify faculty whose expertise matches your project's core technical work (department website, course pages, Google Scholar/publications).
- Email and/or visit: if a professor is too busy, ask if a graduate student can be delegated.
- Be clear: no report marking required; expected commitment about 4-5 hours/year plus three evaluations.
- You may approach faculty in any relevant department across the University of Calgary when appropriate.
- ESE faculty profiles: https://schulich.ucalgary.ca/electrical-computer/faculty-members

Purpose: Liaison with the sponsoring organization; voice of the client/project owner.
Assignment: Each team must have an assigned sponsor representative. All requests, evaluations, and meetings are arranged through this representative.
Deadline: by the Project Information Form deadline (see course timeline and dates).

Must-Haves vs Should-Haves vs Nice-to-Haves
As you prepare to work on your design roadmap document, it is essential that each project team clearly defines and categorizes their deliverables into three groups: Must-Haves, Should-Haves, and Nice-to-Haves. This categorization serves as a way to set clear expectations for the project's success and progression.

Must-Haves: These are the non-negotiable, minimum requirements that your team needs to deliver in order to successfully complete the course. Without these, the project is considered incomplete or unsuccessful. Think of these as your core deliverables that demonstrate you've met the essential objectives of your project. Teams should prioritize Must-Haves from the beginning and allocate the necessary resources and time to ensure their completion. The quality of these must be solid, and no compromises should be made on their delivery.

Should-Haves: These are important features or deliverables that significantly enhance your project but are not absolutely critical for its completion. While the sponsor and your evaluators would be pleased to see the Should-Haves included, not delivering all of them will not impact your overall success in the course. However, delivering on Should-Haves can demonstrate strong planning, efficient resource management, and advanced problem-solving skills, showing that your team has exceeded basic requirements.

Nice-to-Haves: These are additional, value-adding deliverables that go above and beyond the project's essential scope. Nice-to-Haves are often features or enhancements that superstar teams achieve, reflecting creativity, innovation, and a strong understanding of the problem space. Successfully incorporating Nice-to-Haves is a sign of excellence and can greatly impress sponsors and stakeholders, though they should only be pursued if Must-Haves and Should-Haves have been sufficiently addressed.

Agreement with the Sponsor
It is crucial that these categories and their contents are mutually agreed upon between your team and your sponsor. Engaging in this conversation early on will help align expectations, prioritize efforts, and manage scope effectively. Ensure you have clear documentation that outlines each category, which will guide both your team and the sponsor throughout the project timeline.

Important Note: Remember that while Must-Haves define the minimum success criteria, the Should-Haves and Nice-to-Haves give you opportunities to differentiate your project. Teams should be strategic and realistic about what they can achieve within the project timeline, but also think ambitiously about how they can exceed expectations.

Using Gen AI in ENEL 500

AI Use Disclosure: I, Dr. Hamid Zareipour, used ChatGPT (GPT-5 Thinking) as a writing assistant to brainstorm the structure, draft sections, tighten language, and generate the "appropriate uses" table for this course's AI policy. I provided the high-level requirements and public references (University of Calgary guidance on communicating generative-AI use to students and APEGA practice notices) and then edited, reorganized, and finalized the text. No personal or sponsor-confidential information was shared with AI tools. All claims, requirements, and examples were reviewed by me for accuracy and alignment with institutional and professional expectations. I accept full responsibility for the final content.

Purpose. These guidelines tell you how you may use generative AI (writing, coding, images, data assistance, etc.) in ENEL 500 (ESE Capstone) while meeting academic integrity and professional expectations.

Policy stance (allowed with responsibility). You may use AI tools to assist with your work provided that you:
- Disclose your use clearly in every deliverable.
- Own the results: you are fully responsible for the correctness, safety, originality, and fitness of anything you submit.
- Verify and validate all AI-assisted content (text, designs, code) with appropriate checks and tests.
- Protect confidentiality and IP: do not upload sponsor-confidential or restricted data to public tools.

1) What kinds of AI use are appropriate?
Use AI as a copilot, not an author of record. The table below gives typical uses and required safeguards.

Task: Writing (proposals, roadmaps, reports, slides)
  Common, acceptable AI uses: Brainstorming outlines; rephrasing for clarity/grammar; audience-tuning; formatting; summarizing your own text; generating figure/table scaffolds; checking for tone/conciseness.
  You must still: Keep technical content yours. Fact-check all claims. Cite real sources you actually read. Add an AI Use Statement at the end of the doc.

Task: Research support
  Common, acceptable AI uses: Finding keywords, standards, test ideas; generating reading lists to pursue; drafting interview questions.
  You must still: Do not cite AI as a source. Read and cite primary sources. Verify standards in original documents.

Task: Math & analysis
  Common, acceptable AI uses: Setting up equations; unit conversions; code to reproduce calcs; sensitivity ideas.
  You must still: Show derivations and units. Recalculate independently. Spot-check with hand calcs or trusted tools.

Task: Software engineering
  Common, acceptable AI uses: Boilerplate/templates; test stubs; code reviews; refactoring suggestions; docstrings; explaining errors; generating small utility functions.
  You must still: Ensure you understand all code. Run tests, static analysis, and security checks. Review licenses; avoid non-permissive code. Add AI Use Statement in repo/README and in reports.

Task: Data work
  Common, acceptable AI uses: Cleaning suggestions; visualization options; pseudocode for pipelines.
  You must still: Do not upload restricted data to public tools. Validate outputs statistically; record data lineage.

Task: Visuals
  Common, acceptable AI uses: Drafting diagrams from your descriptions.
  You must still: Verify accuracy; prefer tools that export editable vectors.

Not acceptable uses (examples).
- Submitting AI-generated text, code, or designs without disclosure.
- Using AI to fabricate citations, results, or meeting notes.
- Relying on AI outputs without independent verification ("AI said so").
- Uploading confidential sponsor data, third-party IP, or exam/peer-evaluation prompts to public tools.
- Bypassing learning outcomes (e.g., asking AI to write your entire design section or produce code you cannot explain).

2) Disclosure: the AI Use Statement (required)
Add a short AI Use Statement to every major deliverable (proposals, roadmap, reports, slides, etc.) and to your repo README if you have a codbase. Include:
- Tools & versions (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot; version if known), and mode (public web, enterprise, local).
- What it did (e.g., grammar editing; code suggestions for module X; generated initial outline for Section 3).
- Data exposure: whether any sponsor or personal data were provided (should be No for public tools); describe anonymization if used.
- Verification steps: how you checked the outputs (tests, reviews, calculations, standard references).
Example (document footer):
AI Use Statement. We used ChatGPT to brainstorm problem statements and to improve clarity in Sections 2 and 5. We used GitHub Copilot for unit-test scaffolds in sensor_service.py. No sponsor-confidential data were shared with AI tools. All specifications, calculations, and code were independently verified by the team via hand checks, unit tests, and review against the vendor datasheet Rev. C.
Keep an AI log in your team logbook: prompts (or a summary), key outputs, and your verification notes.

3) Verification & validation (you own the results)
Always apply appropriate engineering checks to AI-assisted work:
- Text & claims: trace every fact to a verifiable source; read standards/papers you cite; avoid hallucinated references.
- Calculations: show steps, units, and assumptions; reproduce with an independent method where feasible.
- Code: compile/run; write unit/integration tests; run linters/static analysis; conduct peer code reviews; scan dependencies.
- Design: perform requirements traceability, safety/risk analysis, tolerance checks, worst-case considerations; confirm with the advisor/sponsor.
- Attribution: if AI proposed a non-obvious algorithm, explain it in your own words and justify its selection versus alternatives.

4) Data, confidentiality, and IP
- Do not paste sponsor-confidential or restricted data/code into public AI tools. Use approved enterprise or offline tools only if data sharing is disabled.
- Assume prompts/outputs may be stored by providers. Anonymize or synthesize when seeking help.
- Respect software licenses; do not accept suggestions that insert non-permissive code or content without proper rights.
- Follow any NDAs and the sponsor's tooling restrictions. When unsure, ask your sponsor rep in writing.

5) Engineering professionalism alignment (why this policy exists)
These expectations mirror professional practice: you may rely on tools and the work of others only with appropriate oversight, you must be able to take responsibility for the work product, and work products must be authenticated/approved by accountable professionals. In the capstone course, accountability sits with you, reviewed by your advisor/sponsor/TA.

Missing disclosure, misrepresentation, or unverified AI-assisted content may be treated as academic misconduct and may affect your grades across deliverables or failing the course.

Guidelines for Students: Handling Team Conflicts and Challenges

Purpose
Working in a team is one of the core learning outcomes of ENEL 500.
Differences in communication style, workload balance, and expectations are normal, but how your team handles them determines your success.
If challenges occur, your Teaching Assistant (TA) is your first point of contact. The goal is to resolve issues professionally and internally before they reach the course instructor.

1) Raising a Concern
If you feel that:
- Workload is not being shared fairly,
- Communication or participation is breaking down, or
- A conflict is affecting progress or morale,
then follow these steps:
- Try to resolve the issue within the team first: talk directly, respectfully, and focus on the work, not the person.
- If the issue persists or feels too uncomfortable to address alone, contact your TA (privately) and provide a short written summary of the concern (one or two paragraphs).
- The TA will arrange a meeting with the full team to discuss and resolve the matter.

2) Team Conflict Meeting
When your TA calls a meeting:
- Attendance is mandatory for all team members.
- Meetings are normally held online (e.g., Zoom, Teams) with cameras on.
- The meeting may be recorded so that an accurate record can be kept.
- The purpose is to clarify issues, listen to all perspectives, and agree on next steps, not to assign blame.
During the meeting:
- Speak respectfully, wait your turn, and listen actively.
- Focus on facts and behaviors, not personalities.
- Be honest and specific about challenges.
- Keep the conversation professional; avoid defensive or emotional language.
- Collaborate on a realistic plan to move forward.

3) Agreeing on Actions
By the end of the meeting:
- The group will identify clear action items: what needs to be done, by whom, and by when.
- Each action will include:
    - A short description of the task.
    - The responsible team member(s).
    - A deadline or timeframe.
- Everyone must agree on the plan before the meeting ends.
Within 48 hours, your TA will email a meeting summary listing:
- The issues discussed,
- The agreed actions and responsible members, and
- The follow-up timeline.
Each team member must "Reply All" within 24 hours if they have any comments, corrections, or additions.
If no reply is received, it is assumed that the summary accurately reflects the team's agreement.

4) Follow-Up and Accountability
- Treat the meeting summary as an action plan.
- Complete your assigned tasks on time and keep your teammates informed.
- The TA will check in (typically within a week) to confirm that the agreed actions have been completed and that the situation has improved.
- If progress is made, the issue is considered resolved.
- The team should record a short note in the team logbook describing the resolution or process improvement.
If the conflict continues or new issues emerge:
- A second meeting will be scheduled that may include the course instructor.
- Repeated or unresolved issues can affect individual peer evaluations and, in serious cases, course standing.

5) Professional Expectations
- Be respectful and objective. Keep communication about tasks and solutions.
- Stay engaged. Non-participation or ignoring messages worsens the situation.
- Be accountable. Take ownership for missed deadlines or misunderstandings and outline how you'll correct them.
- Document everything. Keep meeting notes and updates in your team logbook.
- Use the proper communication path: Team -> Project Manager -> TA -> Instructor.

6) Shared Responsibility and Professional Conduct
In ENEL 500, teamwork is not optional; it is fundamental.
Every member shares equal responsibility for maintaining a productive, respectful, and professional team environment.
When issues arise, it is not one person's problem; it is the team's problem to solve collectively.
How a team handles conflict directly reflects on all members, not just those involved in the dispute.
If a team repeatedly fails to address internal issues, or if conflicts lead to missed deadlines, sponsor dissatisfaction, or poor evaluations, the entire team may face consequences, including grade penalties or disciplinary actions.

Key Expectations
- Conflict is normal. Every professional engineering team encounters differences of opinion, personality, or work style.
- Breaking the team apart because of disagreements is not acceptable. The expectation is that you will work through issues, not around them.
- All members must actively participate in resolving conflicts.
- Avoid silence or indifference; non-participation in conflict resolution is viewed as a lack of professionalism.
- You are training for real-world engineering practice. In industry, your teammates, clients, or supervisors will not always be your friends or share your communication style.
- Learning to navigate diverse personalities, expectations, and pressures is a crucial part of professional growth.
- Focus on solutions, not blame. What matters most is how the team collaborates to move forward productively.

Professional Reality
In the workplace, you are evaluated not only on your technical skills, but also on your ability to work effectively with others under pressure, uncertainty, and disagreement.
This course simulates that environment deliberately.
Your ability to communicate respectfully, remain professional, and contribute to conflict resolution is a core component of your learning experience in ENEL 500.

7) When to Contact the Instructor Directly
You may bypass the normal process and contact Dr. Zareipour immediately if:
- There are allegations of harassment, discrimination, or academic misconduct,
- A teammate has stopped participating entirely and cannot be reached,
- The conflict involves ethical or sponsor-related concerns, or
- The situation is affecting your well-being or feels unsafe.

8) Key Takeaways
- Most issues can be resolved through clear communication and accountability.
- The TA's role is to help the team rebuild trust and focus on progress, not to assign penalties.
- Early action prevents escalation; don't wait until evaluations or deadlines to raise concerns.
- Conflict management is part of your professional training. How you handle these situations will define your effectiveness as an engineer.
