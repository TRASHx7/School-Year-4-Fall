Buck II and the boost converter

Two tools from Lecture 2 do almost all the work here: volt-second balance on the inductor and charge balance on the capacitor. You'll use them to size the buck's L, analyse a converter that steps voltage up, size the buck's C (where the usual shortcut fails), and see what happens when a real diode refuses to carry negative current.

Part 1 · Buck, revisited

01Recall: the two balance principles

Three ingredients make converter analysis easy. First, the small ripple approximation (SRA): the output ripple is tiny, so write v ≈ V inside expressions. Second, look at the inductor over one switching period. Third, assume steady state: iL ends each period exactly where it started.

From those you get two rules. They work for every converter in this course:

Volt-second balance: ⟨vL⟩Ts = 0 buck: (Vg − V)D + (−V)D′ = 0 ⇒ V = DVg, M(D) = D Charge balance: ⟨iC⟩Ts = 0 buck: IL = V/R Ripple: ΔiL = DTs(Vg − V)L,   peak = IL + ΔiL/2 ΔiL is peak-to-peak

Why volt-second balance? The inductor law vL = L diL/dt means the change in current over a period is (1/L)∫vLdt. In steady state that change is zero, so the area under vL must be zero. Charge balance is the same idea for the capacitor: iC = C dv/dt, and v repeats every period.

Analogy · a bank account

The inductor current is a bank balance. During DTs you deposit at a rate of (Vg − V)/L; during D′Ts you withdraw at V/L. If deposits and withdrawals don't cancel every month, the balance drifts forever. "Steady state" means the books balance each period.

Tricky bits
  • ΔiL is peak-to-peak. The peak is IL + ΔiL/2, not IL + ΔiL. Some textbooks (Erickson) use half of this as their Δi, so check which one a formula expects.
  • D′ means 1 − D. It's the fraction of the period spent in position 2.
  • Balance is only true in steady state. During start-up or a load step, ⟨vL⟩ ≠ 0 and that's exactly what makes iL change.

02Choosing L for the buck

The load changes, and the average inductor current follows it. For the buck, IL = Io = V/R. So as R ranges from Rmax down to Rmin, IL ranges up to IL,max = V/Rmin.

Designers usually allow ΔiL to be 20% to 40% of IL,max. Smaller ripple needs a bigger, heavier inductor. Bigger ripple means higher peak currents and more stress on the switches. So we want the smallest L that keeps ΔiL at or under X% of IL,max.

L > Rmin(Vg − V)Vg fs(X/100%) buck, ripple ≤ X% of IL,max
Worked derivation (lecture): minimum L for the buck
  1. Start from the ripple:ΔiL = DTs(Vg − V)/L
  2. Set it equal to the allowed ripple, X% of the largest average current:ΔiL = (X/100%) · V/Rmin
  3. Substitute D = V/Vg and Ts = 1/fs:(V/Vg)(Vg − V)/(fsL) = (X/100%) · V/Rmin
  4. V appears on both sides and cancels. Solve for L; any L larger than this gives less ripple:L > Rmin(Vg − V) / [Vg fs (X/100%)]
Analogy · a flywheel getting kicked

The inductor is a flywheel. Each period it gets a kick of strength (Vg − V) lasting DTs, and its speed wobbles by ΔiL. A heavier flywheel (bigger L) or shorter kicks (higher fs) means less wobble. How much work the flywheel is doing (the load current) doesn't change the wobble at all. The load only matters because the spec says "wobble no more than X% of the biggest speed".

Tricky bits
  • Use Rmin, not Rmax. The ripple itself doesn't depend on R. R only appears because the spec is a percentage of IL,max, which flows at the heaviest load (smallest R).
  • Convert the percentage. X = 20% goes in as 0.20. Plugging in 20 makes L a hundred times too small.
  • Units. fs in Hz (100 kHz = 100 000), answer in henries. 7.5 × 10−5 H is 75 µH.
  • L scales as 1/fs. Doubling the switching frequency halves the inductor. That's the main reason converters switch fast.

Part 2 · The boost converter

03The boost converter and its two states

A buck steps voltage down (V < Vg). A boost steps it up (V > Vg). The boost moves the inductor to the input side. Q1 connects the inductor's right end to ground, and Q2 connects it to the output. The two switches are complementary: Q1 is on for DTs, then Q2 is on for D′Ts.

Analogy · the hydraulic ram pump

A ram pump lifts water higher than its source with no motor. Water runs downhill through a pipe and out a drain valve, picking up speed (state ①: Q1 on, the inductor current builds). Then the drain slams shut. The moving water can't stop instantly, so it forces its way through a one-way valve into a higher tank (state ②: Q2 on, the inductor pushes current into the higher output voltage). The inductor's momentum is what lifts the voltage.

Tricky bits
  • In state ① the load is fed only by C. Q2 is open, so iC = −v/R: the capacitor discharges into R.
  • vL is negative in state ②. Since V > Vg, vL = Vg − V < 0, so iL ramps down. Don't flip the sign because "it's a boost".
  • iL is the input current here. In the buck, iL fed the output. In the boost, the inductor is in series with the source, so the source always supplies iL.

04Volt-second balance: the conversion ratio M(D)

Apply the same rule as the buck. State ① lasts DTs with vL = Vg. State ② lasts D′Ts with vL = Vg − V. The areas must cancel.

Worked derivation (lecture): solving for V
  1. Average the inductor voltage over one period and set it to zero:⟨vL⟩ = (1/Ts) ∫0Ts vL dt = 0
  2. Split the integral into the two states:= (1/Ts)[ Vg·DTs + (Vg − V)·D′Ts ]
  3. Expand and use D + D′ = 1:= VgD + VgD′ − VD′ = Vg − VD′
  4. Set to zero:V = Vg/D′
M(D) = VVg = 1D′ = 11 − D starts at 1 when D = 0, grows without limit as D → 1

The lecture's question: what does a practical converter's ratio look like as D → 1? It rises with the ideal curve at first, then peaks and falls away. Analogy · a seesaw

Volt-second balance is a seesaw. On one side sits Vg for a time D. On the other sits (V − Vg) for a time D′. If you make D′ short (sit close to the pivot), you need a much heavier weight (V − Vg) to balance. That's why V shoots up as D′ shrinks.

Tricky bits
  • D = 0.5 gives M = 2, not 1.5. The ratio is 1/(1 − D), which is nonlinear.
  • Solving for D. D = 1 − Vg/V. For 12 V to 48 V: D = 1 − 0.25 = 0.75.
  • M ≥ 1 always for the ideal boost. At D = 0 the output just equals Vg (through L and Q2).
  • D = 1 is not "infinite voltage". Q1 would stay on forever, shorting the source through L, and nothing would reach the output.

05Charge balance: the inductor current

Now the capacitor. In state ① it only discharges (iC = −V/R). In state ② the inductor refills it (iC = IL − V/R). The charge in must equal the charge out.

Worked derivation (lecture): solving for IL
  1. Average the capacitor current and set it to zero:⟨iC⟩ = (1/Ts) ∫0Ts iC dt = 0
  2. Split into the two states:= (1/Ts)[ −(V/R)·DTs + (IL − V/R)·D′Ts ]
  3. Simplify with D + D′ = 1:= −V/R + ILD′
  4. Set to zero, then use D′ = Vg/V from §4:IL = V/(RD′) = V²/(RVg)
IL = VRD′ = V2RVg = Io/D′, bigger than the load current

A neat check: multiply both sides by Vg. You get VgIL = V²/R. Input power equals output power, which it must for an ideal (lossless) converter.

Analogy · a delivery driver on part-time hours

The inductor is a driver who can only drop parcels at the output during D′ of each hour. The town (load) needs Io parcels per hour, steadily. So the driver has to carry Io/D′ per hour while on shift. At D′ = 0.25, that's four times the town's demand.

Tricky bits
  • For the boost, IL ≠ Io. In the buck they were equal. Here IL = Io/D′ = M·Io.
  • Two forms, same thing. V/(RD′) and V²/(RVg) are equal because D′ = Vg/V. Use whichever matches the given data.
  • IL,max uses Rmin. The next section needs IL,max = V/(RminD′).

06Sizing L and C for the boost

Both calculations use state ①, because it's the simplest: vL = Vg (constant) and iC = −V/R (constant). Constant slopes mean straight-line ramps, and a single ramp gives the full peak-to-peak swing.

ΔiL = VgDTsL  ⇒  L > Vg(1 − Vg/V)fs(X/100%)IL,max IL,max = V/(RminD′) ΔvC = V DTsRC  ⇒  C > V − VgR fs(X/100%)V requirement ΔvC < (X/100%)V
Worked derivation (lecture): minimum L for the boost
  1. Requirement:ΔiL < (X/100%) IL,max
  2. The current rises over state ①, where vL = Vg:ΔiL = (1/L) ∫0DTs vL dt = VgDTs/L
  3. Substitute ΔiL = (X/100%) IL,max, D = 1 − Vg/V and Ts = 1/fs, then rearrange:L > Vg(1 − Vg/V) / [fs(X/100%) IL,max]
Worked derivation (lecture): minimum C for the boost
  1. Requirement:ΔvC < (X/100%) V
  2. In state ① the capacitor loses charge at a constant rate V/R, so v falls in a straight line. The drop is the ripple:ΔvC = −(1/C) ∫0DTs iC dt = −(1/C)(−V/R)DTs = V DTs/(RC)
  3. Substitute ΔvC = (X/100%)V, D = 1 − Vg/V and Ts = 1/fs. V cancels from D·V = V − Vg:C > (V − Vg) / [R fs(X/100%) V]

Analogy · a water tower during a pump outage

During state ① the pump (the inductor) is cut off from the town, so the water tower (C) alone supplies it at a steady rate V/R. The tower level drops in a straight line for the whole outage, DTs. Size the tower so that drop is at most X% of the normal level.

Tricky bits
  • Boost ΔiL is VgDTs/L. Don't reuse the buck's DTs(Vg − V)/L; the inductor sees Vg during the on-time here.
  • D here is 1 − Vg/V, not V/Vg. Mixing up the buck and boost duty ratios is the most common error.
  • Which R? The lecture writes R in the C formula. The capacitor discharges fastest at the heaviest load, so use the smallest R you must support for a worst-case design.
  • Using the SRA on iC is fine here (iC jumps between big levels). It will not be fine for the buck: see §7.

Part 3 · Buck output ripple

07Back to the buck: where the SRA breaks

Try the boost method on the buck capacitor. KCL at the output node over the whole period gives iC = iL − v/R. Apply the SRA to both iL and v and you get iC = IL − V/R. But charge balance said IL = V/R for the buck. So this predicts iC = 0 and a perfectly flat v = V. The real converter disagrees: iC swings about zero and v ripples about V. The approximation threw away exactly what we need.

The fix: apply the SRA to v only. Both terms put ripple into iC: ΔiL from the inductor and Δv/R from the load. A typical design allows ΔiL of 20% to 40% of IL, but Δv of only about 1% of V. Since IL = V/R, Δv/R ≈ 0.01IL. So the inductor contributes 20 to 40 times more ripple to iC than the load does.

iC = iL − V/R = iL − IL the ripple of iL alone: a triangle centred on zero

Analogy · asking the average sea level how big the waves are

The average height of the ocean is flat. If you replace the sea with its average before measuring the waves, you'll conclude there are no waves. For the buck capacitor, the inductor ripple is the wave. You can average away the tiny wobble in v, but never the wobble in iL.

Tricky bits
  • The SRA is about what's small compared with what you're computing. Sizing L: v's ripple is negligible, so v ≈ V is fine. Sizing the buck's C: iC has zero average, so its ripple is the whole signal.
  • Why the boost got away with it. The boost's iC is −V/R in ① and IL − V/R in ②: large levels. The small iL ripple only slightly tilts the tops.

08From capacitor charge to C for the buck

Since iC = C dv/dt, the change in v between two instants is the charge delivered divided by C:

v(t2) − v(t1) = 1C∫t1t2 iC dt = ΔQC  ⇒  ΔvC = ΔQC

v rises while iC > 0 and falls while iC < 0. So v is minimum at the upward zero crossing of iC (t1) and maximum at the downward one (t2). Between them iC > 0, so ΔQ is the charge in the positive lobe, and ΔvC is the peak-to-peak ripple.

Where are the crossings? Each ramp of iL is linear, so IL sits exactly halfway between Imin and Imax. Then iC = iL − IL swings between ±ΔiL/2 and crosses zero at the midpoint of each ramp:

t1 = DTs/2,   t2 = DTs + D′Ts/2  ⇒  base = ½(D + D′)Ts = Ts/2 for any D ΔvC = ΔiL8Cfs  ⇒  C > Vg − V8VgLfs2(X/100%)
Worked derivation (lecture): minimum C for the buck
  1. Requirement:ΔvC < (X/100%) V
  2. The positive lobe is a triangle, height ΔiL/2, base Ts/2:ΔQ = ∫t1t2 iC dt = ½ · (Ts/2) · (ΔiL/2) = ΔiLTs/8
  3. That charge raises v from its minimum to its maximum:ΔQ = C[v(t2) − v(t1)] = CΔvC ⇒ ΔvC = ΔiL/(8Cfs)
  4. Substitute ΔvC = (X/100%)V and ΔiL = V(Vg − V)/(VgLfs), then rearrange (V cancels):C > (Vg − V) / [8VgLfs2(X/100%)]

Analogy · a bathtub with a pulsing tap

The drain takes out a steady IL. The tap (iL) pulses a little above and below that. Whenever the tap beats the drain, the water level rises; when it falls behind, the level drops. The total swing in level is the extra water from one "ahead" stretch (ΔQ) divided by the tub's floor area (C).

Tricky bits
  • The base is Ts/2 for every D. Half of the rising ramp plus half of the falling ramp is always half a period.
  • The height is ΔiL/2, not ΔiL. That, the ½ for a triangle and the Ts/2 base give the 8.
  • v peaks at t2, not at DTs. At DTs, iC is largest, so v is rising fastest. It keeps rising until iC crosses zero.
  • fs is squared. With L and C fixed, doubling fs halves ΔiL and halves ΔQ again: ΔvC drops to a quarter.

Part 4 · Real switches and DCM

09Practical realization: the diode buck

So far the switches were ideal. Real ones are semiconductors. The buck's two positions can be built two ways:

  1. One active switch plus one diode: the diode buck. Q1 is a MOSFET, or an IGBT with an anti-parallel diode.
  2. Two active switches: the synchronous buck.

The lecture analyses (a). The diode has no gate. The circuit turns it on and off, not a controller. When Q1 turns off, the inductor insists on keeping iL flowing, so it pulls the switch node down until D1 is forward biased. The diode then carries iL around the output loop.

In continuous conduction mode (CCM), iL > 0 for the whole period. Then the diode behaves exactly like switch position 2, and everything from Lecture 2 still holds: M = D.

Analogy · a check valve

A diode is a one-way check valve in a water pipe. It opens by itself whenever water pushes forward and slams shut if the flow tries to reverse. Nobody operates it. An active switch is a tap a person controls, and it will pass flow either way once opened.

Tricky bits
  • In CCM, nothing changes. The diode buck in CCM has the same waveforms and M = D as the ideal buck.
  • The diode's weakness is direction. It cannot carry a negative iL. That single fact is what creates DCM in the next section.
  • The diode current equals iL while Q1 is off: it jumps up to the peak IL + ΔiL/2 at DTs and ramps down to IL − ΔiL/2 at Ts.

10Light loads: CCM, BCM and DCM

In CCM, iL(0) = IL − ΔiL/2 and iL(DTs) = IL + ΔiL/2. Now notice which parts depend on the load:

IL = V/R load dependent ΔiL = V(Vg − V)VgLfs not load dependent

As the load lightens (R rises), IL falls but the ripple stays put. The bottom of the triangle, IL − ΔiL/2, sinks toward zero:

  • BCM (boundary conduction mode), IL = ΔiL/2: iL just reaches zero at the end of the period, the instant Q1 turns on again. The diode current also just reaches zero.
  • DCM (discontinuous conduction mode), IL < ΔiL/2: iL hits zero early. The diode turns off. With Q1 also off, nothing can drive iL, so it stays at zero for the rest of the period: a third interval, D3Ts ≠ 0.

Analogy · a swing you can only push forward

iL is a swing that must only ever move forward (the diode forbids backward). With a strong, steady rider (heavy load) it never slows to a stop between pushes: CCM. With a light rider it coasts to a stop before the next push and just hangs there: DCM. BCM is the exact moment it stops right as the next push arrives.

Tricky bits
  • The condition is IL < ΔiL/2, half the peak-to-peak ripple, not ΔiL.
  • In interval ③, vL = 0 because iL is stuck at zero (diL/dt = 0), not because any voltage is zero. The switch node floats up to v.
  • In DCM, M ≠ D. The CCM results no longer apply. §11 derives the new ratio.
  • D + D2 + D3 = 1. D2Ts is the diode's on-time; D3Ts is the dead time at zero current.

11Deriving the conversion ratio in DCM

The road has four steps: (1) three intervals, not two; (2) volt-second balance gives M in terms of D and an unknown D2; (3) a second expression for IL fixes D2; (4) combine to get M in known quantities (D, L, R, Ts).

Worked derivation (lecture), step by step
  1. Volt-second balance over three intervals⟨vL⟩ = (1/Ts)[(Vg − V)DTs + (−V)D2Ts + 0·D3Ts] = (Vg − V)D − VD2 = 0
    Interval ③ contributes nothing because vL = 0 there.
  2. First result, still with an unknownM = V/Vg = D/(D + D2)
    Problem: volt-second and charge balance give two equations, but DCM has three unknowns: V, IL and D2. We need one more.
  3. IL, first way: charge balance⟨iC⟩ = 0 ⇒ IL = V/R
    Same as always for the buck: the capacitor passes no DC.
  4. IL, second way: the triangle's area⟨iL⟩ = (1/Ts)·½(D + D2)Ts·ΔiL = ½(D + D2)ΔiL  (1)
    iL is a triangle of height ΔiL on a base (D + D2)Ts, then zero. This is where D2 enters.
  5. The peak comes from interval ①ΔiL = (1/L)∫0DTs vL dt = (Vg − V)DTs/L  (2)
    iL starts from zero, so the rise over ① is also the peak value.
  6. Combine (2) into (1)IL = (Vg − V)(DTs/2L)(D + D2)
    Now we have IL two ways, one of them containing D2.
  7. Equate and solve for D + D2, with K ≡ 2L/(RTs)V/R = (Vg − V)(DTs/2L)(D + D2) ⇒ D + D2 = (K/D)·V/(Vg − V) = (K/D)·M/(1 − M)
    Divide through by Vg to turn V/(Vg − V) into M/(1 − M).
  8. Substitute into M = D/(D + D2)M = (D²/K)·(1 − M)/M ⇒ KM² + D²M − D² = 0
    D2 is gone. Only known quantities remain.
  9. Take the positive rootM = 2 / [1 + √(1 + 4K/D²)] = 2 / [1 + √(1 + 8L/(D²RTs))]
    The negative root would give a negative output voltage, which is impossible here.

Analogy · a detective one clue short

Two clues (volt-second and charge balance) can't pin down three suspects (V, IL, D2). The third clue is the shape of iL: a triangle whose area you can compute. Once you have IL two ways, set them equal and the extra suspect drops out.

Tricky bits
  • Check the mode first. If K ≥ 1 − D the converter is in CCM and M = D. Plugging a CCM case into the DCM formula gives a wrong value, smaller than D.
  • In DCM, IL is not the midpoint of the ramp. It's the triangle's area divided by Ts: ½(D + D2)ΔiL.
  • K uses Ts. K = 2L/(RTs) = 2Lfs/R. It's dimensionless.
  • Algebra shortcut. V/(Vg − V) = M/(1 − M). Dividing top and bottom by Vg is the step people miss.

12The M(D, K) curves and where the boundary lies

K compares the inductor to the load. Small K (a light load or a small L) is deep DCM: M runs well above D, and V → Vg long before D → 1. Each curve rejoins the CCM line M = D once the converter is back in CCM. For K ≥ 1 it never leaves it. Why? Evaluate the DCM condition IL < ΔiL/2 using CCM values (V = DVg):

Worked derivation (lecture): the CCM/DCM boundary
  1. In CCM:IL = DVg/R,   ΔiL/2 = (Vg − V)DTs/(2L) = VgD(1 − D)Ts/(2L)
  2. DCM when IL < ΔiL/2:DVg/R < VgD(1 − D)Ts/(2L)
  3. Cancel DVg and rearrange:K = 2L/(RTs) < 1 − D ≡ Kcrit(D)
DCM if K < 1 − D;   CCM if K > 1 − D Kcrit ≤ 1 for every D
  • The boundary moves with D, and a buck enters DCM at small D first (Kcrit = 1 − D is biggest there).
  • Kcrit ≤ 1 for every D. So K ≥ 1 (a large L, a heavy load) keeps IL ≥ ΔiL/2 at any duty ratio: never DCM.

Analogy · flywheel size versus demand

K is the ratio of flywheel size (L) to how little the load asks for (R). A big flywheel, or a load that asks for a lot, keeps the current spinning through every period. A small flywheel with a light load coasts to a stop between kicks.

Tricky bits
  • Where a curve rejoins M = D: at D = 1 − K. For K = 0.1 that's D = 0.9; for K = 0.5, D = 0.5.
  • Small D enters DCM first. ΔiL/2 ∝ D(1 − D) while IL ∝ D, so their ratio ∝ (1 − D): biggest at small D.
  • Convention check. Here ΔiL is peak-to-peak everywhere. Erickson's Δi is half of it, so his DCM condition reads I < Δi.
