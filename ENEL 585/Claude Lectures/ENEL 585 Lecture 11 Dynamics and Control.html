Dynamics and Control Guide

A converter set to a fixed duty ratio drifts with every change in input or load, so its duty ratio has to come from feedback. This guide builds that feedback from the ground up: an averaged, then linearized model of the inverting buck-boost, its transfer functions and Bode plots (including the right-half-plane zero), the loop gain and phase margin, a PI compensator that works over the whole operating range, the cost of a digital sampling delay, feedforward, and finally the two nested loops (a fast current loop with a PR controller and a slow dc-link voltage loop) that run a grid inverter and a PFC rectifier.

Start hereBig picture

Up to now every converter in the course was analysed in steady state: pick \(D\), get \(V = M(D)\,V_g\). A real supply has to hold its output at a reference while the input voltage, the load and even the reference itself change, and it has to get from one steady state to the next without ringing wildly or going unstable. That needs feedback, and designing feedback needs a model of how the converter moves between steady states.

The lecture builds that model one step at a time on a single running example, an inverting buck-boost with \(V_g = 30\ \text{V}\), \(D = 0.6\), \(V = -45\ \text{V}\), \(R = 10\ \Omega\), \(L = 160\ \mu\text{H}\), \(C = 160\ \mu\text{F}\), \(f_s = 50\ \text{kHz}\). It then closes the loop, designs a compensator that works over the whole operating range, adds the effects of digital control, and finally reuses every idea to control an ac current into the grid.

Part 1 · A dynamic model

Section 1Why a converter needs control

A power supply must keep its output \(v\) within spec of a reference \(v_{ref}\) while the input \(v_g\) and the load \(R\) change. Feedback does this: measure \(v\), compare it with \(v_{ref}\), and let the error set the duty ratio \(d\). The loop has five jobs:

  • Regulate: the steady-state error \(v_{ref} - v\) is within spec, ideally zero.
  • Reject disturbances: after a step in \(v_g\) or the load, \(v\) returns to within spec of \(v_{ref}\).
  • Stay stable: \(v\) settles after any small disturbance, for every \(v_g\) and load in the range.
  • Be robust: meet the spec despite component tolerances and effects the model leaves out.
  • Respond well: for a step in \(v_{ref}\), the rise time, overshoot and settling time are within spec.

Why not just set \(d\) to the right constant? Because the "right" duty ratio depends on \(v_g\), the load and the losses, and a fixed \(d\) cannot follow them. The lecture shows four ways an open-loop buck misses 5 V (Example 1.1).

Stable means: after a small step \(\Delta v_{ref} \ll v_{ref}\), the output change \(\Delta v\) settles to a steady value. It may ring on the way and still be stable; it is unstable if the oscillation never dies (or grows). For a stable loop the response to a reference step is judged by three numbers, and the response to a load or line step by three more:

Example 1.1 (lecture): an open-loop buck misses its target
Buck with series resistance \(r = 0.1\ \Omega\) (switch plus inductor), set for \(v = 5\ \text{V}\) from \(v_g = 12\ \text{V}\) at \(1\ \text{A}\), so the load is \(R = 5\ \Omega\). The duty ratio is then held fixed.

With the resistance, the averaged output of the buck is \(v = d\,v_g - r\,i\), where \(i = \frac{v}{R}\) is the load current. At the design point:

\[\begin{aligned} 5\ \text{V} &= D\,(12\ \text{V}) - (0.1\ \Omega)(1\ \text{A})\\ D &= \frac{5.1\ \text{V}}{12\ \text{V}} = \mathbf{0.425} \end{aligned}\]

Input drops to 10 V (load still 5 Ω). Substitute \(i = \frac{v}{R}\) and solve for \(v\):

\[\begin{aligned} v &= D\,v_g\,\frac{R}{R+r} = 0.425\,(10\ \text{V})\,\frac{5}{5.1}\\ &= \mathbf{4.17\ \text{V}}\quad(\text{17 \% low}) \end{aligned}\]

Load rises from 1 A to 4.6 A (input back at 12 V): more drop across \(r\).

\[ v = 0.425\,(12\ \text{V}) - (0.1\ \Omega)(4.6\ \text{A}) = 5.1 - 0.46 = \mathbf{4.64\ \text{V}}\quad(\text{7 \% low}) \]

The lecture's simulations add two more failures: at start-up with \(d = D\) from the first period, the \(L\)-\(C\) filter rings and \(v\) peaks 62 % above 5 V; at a light load of 0.15 A the inductor current reaches zero each period (DCM) and \(v\) settles 49 % high.

No fixed \(D\) gives 5 V at every \(v_g\) and load: \(d\) must be set from the measured \(v\).
Filled in

Analogy

Driving with the accelerator taped at one position: on a flat road you might hold 100 km/h, but a hill, a headwind or extra passengers change your speed and nothing corrects it. Cruise control measures the speed and moves the throttle. The analogy breaks down at start-up: a car does not overshoot because of an internal resonance, while an \(L\)-\(C\) filter does.

Tricky bits
  • Overshoot is a fraction of the step size \(\Delta v_{ref}\), not of \(v_{ref}\) itself. A 0.1 V overshoot on a 0.5 V step is 20 %, not 2 %.
  • Rise time runs from 10 % to 90 % of the step, not from the instant of the step.
  • Ringing is not instability. Stable means the response settles; unstable means it never does.
  • "Full rejection" means the final error is exactly zero after a disturbance; the transient can still deviate.

Section 2Small changes around an operating point

Steady state, \(V = M(D)\,V_g\), says where \(v\) ends up but not how it gets there. \(L\) and \(C\) store energy, so \(v\) cannot follow \(d\) at once. In the running example, stepping \(d\) from 0.60 to 0.62 makes \(v\) ring at 375 Hz and take 12 ms to settle within 2 % of the change. A dynamic model gives equations for how \(v\) responds over time to \(d\) and \(v_g\); that is what decides whether a loop is stable and what lets us design the compensator \(G_c(s)\).

Two time scales. Vary \(d\) slowly, \(d(t) = 0.60 + 0.02\sin(2\pi f_m t)\) with \(f_m = 50\ \text{Hz}\), and \(v\) does two things at once: a 50 Hz swing from \(-49.0\ \text{V}\) to \(-41.4\ \text{V}\), with 0.34 V peak to peak of 50 kHz ripple on top. The model will describe only the slow part, \(v\) averaged over one switching period. That is valid when switching is much faster than both the variation of \(d\) and the converter's own ringing (50 Hz and 400 Hz against 50 kHz) and the ripple is small against the dc value (0.34 V on 45 V).

Steady state plus a small change. Leave the ripple out and split each signal into its operating-point (dc) value and a small variation:

\[ v(t) = V + \hat{v}(t),\qquad d(t) = D + \hat{d}(t),\qquad |\hat v| \ll |V|,\quad |\hat d| \ll D,\ 1-D \]
\(V\), \(D\) = operating point (steady-state output voltage in V, duty ratio with no unit) · \(\hat v\) = small variation of the output (V) · \(\hat d\) = small variation of the duty ratio (no unit)

For an inverting buck-boost in CCM the steady state and its slope (by the quotient rule) are

\[ V = -\frac{D}{1-D}\,V_g,\qquad \frac{dV}{dD} = -\frac{(1-D)+D}{(1-D)^2}\,V_g = -\frac{V_g}{(1-D)^2} \]
\[ \hat v \approx \frac{dV}{dD}\,\hat d \qquad\text{(slow changes only)} \]
\(V_g\) = input voltage (V) · \(\frac{dV}{dD}\) = slope of the steady-state curve, volts per unit of duty ratio (V)

Near the operating point the curve \(V(D)\) is close to its tangent; the gap grows with \(|\hat d|\). The tangent's error is \(\frac{|\hat d|}{1-D}\) of the true change. And the tangent is a dc result: it only holds when \(\hat d\) varies much slower than the converter rings. Faster \(\hat d\) changes both the size and the phase of \(\hat v\):

\(f_m\) of \(\hat d = 0.005\sin(2\pi f_m t)\)Size of \(\hat v\) against the tangent predictionLag behind the prediction
50 Hz2 % larger3°
400 Hz (near the 398 Hz resonance)4.0 times103°

So the model must give \(\frac{|\hat v|}{|\hat d|}\) and the lag at every frequency. That is Part 2.

Section 3The averaged model

Start, as in steady-state analysis, from the two switch states of the buck-boost in CCM, but with \(d(t)\) in place of \(D\): it may now change from one period to the next.

\[\begin{aligned} &\text{state 1, } 0 \le t \lt dT_s\ (Q_1\text{ on}): && L\frac{di_L}{dt} = v_g, && C\frac{dv}{dt} = -\frac{v}{R}\\ &\text{state 2, } dT_s \le t \lt T_s\ (Q_1\text{ off}): && L\frac{di_L}{dt} = v, && C\frac{dv}{dt} = -i_L - \frac{v}{R} \end{aligned}\]
\(i_L\) = inductor current (A) · \(v\) = output voltage, negative for this converter (V) · \(v_g\) = input voltage (V) · \(T_s\) = switching period (s) · \(L\) = inductance (H) · \(C\) = output capacitance (F) · \(R\) = load resistance (Ω)

Small ripple, but no longer constant. In steady state the small-ripple approximation replaced \(v_g\), \(v\), \(i_L\) by their dc values. Now they vary slowly, so replace each by its average over one switching period:

\[ \langle x(t)\rangle_{T_s} = \frac{1}{T_s}\int_{t-\frac{T_s}{2}}^{t+\frac{T_s}{2}} x(\tau)\,d\tau \]
\(\langle x\rangle_{T_s}\) = average of signal \(x\) over the switching period centred on \(t\) (same unit as \(x\)) · \(\tau\) = integration variable (s)

Within one period \(v_g\), \(v\), \(i_L\) barely change, but \(v_L\) and \(i_C\) jump between ① and ②, so each is averaged over both states, weighting ① by \(d\) and ② by \(d' = 1-d\):

\[\begin{aligned} \langle v_L\rangle_{T_s} &= \frac{1}{T_s}\Big[d\,T_s\,\langle v_g\rangle_{T_s} + d'T_s\,\langle v\rangle_{T_s}\Big] = d\,\langle v_g\rangle_{T_s} + d'\langle v\rangle_{T_s}\\ \langle i_C\rangle_{T_s} &= d\left(-\frac{\langle v\rangle_{T_s}}{R}\right) + d'\left(-\langle i_L\rangle_{T_s}-\frac{\langle v\rangle_{T_s}}{R}\right) = -\frac{\langle v\rangle_{T_s}}{R} - d'\langle i_L\rangle_{T_s} \end{aligned}\]

Averaging both sides of \(v_L = L\frac{di_L}{dt}\) and \(i_C = C\frac{dv}{dt}\) gives the switching-period averaged model:

\[\begin{aligned} L\frac{d\langle i_L\rangle_{T_s}}{dt} &= d\,\langle v_g\rangle_{T_s} + d'\langle v\rangle_{T_s}\\ C\frac{d\langle v\rangle_{T_s}}{dt} &= -\frac{\langle v\rangle_{T_s}}{R} - d'\langle i_L\rangle_{T_s} \end{aligned}\]
inputs \(d\) and \(v_g\); states \(\langle i_L\rangle_{T_s}\) and \(\langle v\rangle_{T_s}\)
\(d\) = duty ratio of the period (no unit) · \(d' = 1-d\) (no unit). In steady state the left sides are zero: volt-second balance and charge balance.

It is nonlinear. The terms \(d\langle v_g\rangle_{T_s}\), \(d'\langle v\rangle_{T_s}\) and \(d'\langle i_L\rangle_{T_s}\) are products of two signals that both vary. Double both and the term quadruples, whereas a linear term such as \(\frac{\langle v\rangle_{T_s}}{R}\) only doubles. That is why \(V\) is not proportional to the step in \(D\) (Example 3.2), and why Part 2's transfer functions need one more step: linearization (Section 4).

How good is it? At \(f_s = 50\ \text{kHz}\) (ripple 0.36 V peak to peak) the averaged model stays within 0.01 V of the switching converter's period average after a step of \(d\) from 0.60 to 0.61. At \(f_s = 10\ \text{kHz}\) (ripple 1.8 V) it is up to 0.55 V off during a 3.2 V transient and 0.13 V off in steady state. More ripple, more error, because in ① and ② the signals are further from their averages.

Analogy

A bank balance checked once a month. Within the month money jumps in on payday and out at every purchase (the switching), but the monthly average changes smoothly with your salary and your habits. The averaged model tracks that smooth monthly figure and ignores the day-to-day sawtooth. It fails if your salary itself changes every few days, which is the "slow against the switching" condition.

Tricky bits
  • State ② of this converter connects the inductor to \(v\) (negative), and the capacitor loses \(i_L\): watch the signs in \(\langle i_C\rangle_{T_s}\).
  • \(d'\) multiplies the state-② quantities: \(d'\langle v\rangle_{T_s}\) and \(d'\langle i_L\rangle_{T_s}\), never \(d\).
  • Volt-second balance (\(\langle v_L\rangle_{T_s} = 0\)) only holds in steady state. During a transient \(\langle v_L\rangle_{T_s}\) is what changes the current.
  • The averaged model still describes the large signal behaviour (it is just nonlinear). Linearizing is a further approximation.

Section 4Perturb and linearize

Write every averaged signal, and the duty ratio, as its dc value plus a small ac perturbation:

\[\begin{aligned} \langle v_g\rangle_{T_s} &= V_g + \hat v_g, & d &= D + \hat d, & d' &= D' - \hat d\\ \langle i_L\rangle_{T_s} &= I_L + \hat i_L, & \langle v\rangle_{T_s} &= V + \hat v && \end{aligned}\]
\(V_g\), \(D\) = the inputs at the operating point, held constant · \(I_L\), \(V\) = the resulting steady state (A, V) · \(\hat v_g\), \(\hat d\), \(\hat i_L\), \(\hat v\) = perturbations, each small against its own dc value (\(|\hat d|\) also against \(D'\)) · \(D' = 1-D\)

Insert the split into a product. For the first one:

\[ d\,\langle v_g\rangle_{T_s} = (D+\hat d)(V_g+\hat v_g) = \underbrace{DV_g}_{\text{dc}} + \underbrace{D\hat v_g + V_g\hat d}_{\text{linear}} + \underbrace{\hat d\,\hat v_g}_{\text{small}\times\text{small}} \]

Do this for the whole inductor equation. The dc terms cancel (volt-second balance at the operating point, \(DV_g + D'V = 0\), and \(\frac{dI_L}{dt} = 0\)); the second-order term \(\hat d(\hat v_g - \hat v)\) is negligible against \((V_g - V)\hat d\); what is left is linear. The capacitor equation works the same way (charge balance cancels the dc terms, and \(\hat d\,\hat i_L\) is dropped against \(I_L\hat d\)). The result is the small-signal model:

\[\begin{aligned} L\frac{d\hat i_L}{dt} &= D\,\hat v_g + D'\,\hat v + (V_g - V)\,\hat d\\ C\frac{d\hat v}{dt} &= -\frac{\hat v}{R} - D'\,\hat i_L + I_L\,\hat d \end{aligned}\]
linear, constant coefficients set by the operating point; inputs \(\hat d\) (control) and \(\hat v_g\) (disturbance)
\(V_g - V\) = coefficient of \(\hat d\) in the inductor equation (V) · \(I_L\) = coefficient of \(\hat d\) in the capacitor equation (A)

It holds when the perturbations are much smaller than the dc values (their products were dropped) and, as before, the variations are slow against the switching with small ripple.

One linear model per operating point. The coefficients belong to one \((D, V, I_L)\). If \(V_g\) drifts from 30 V to 15 V with \(v\) held at \(-45\ \text{V}\), \(D\) moves from 0.6 to 0.75 and the coefficients change (Example 4.3). The plan: linearize at each operating point (one set of transfer functions each), then design one \(G_c(s)\) that is stable and meets the targets at every one of them. That covers operating points that move slowly, like a \(V_g\) drift or a soft-start ramp. A fast, large move (for example \(V_g\) stepping from 30 V to 15 V at once, so \(d\) must move by 0.15) is outside every linear model; for that, integrate the nonlinear averaged model. A load step is different: in the ideal CCM converter \(D\) does not depend on \(R\), so \(\hat d\) is only nonzero during the transient.

Section 5The transfer functions Gvg and Gvd

The two small-signal equations are coupled, so solve them together. Divide by \(L\) and \(C\) and stack them as a state-space model:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\hat i_L\\ \hat v\end{bmatrix} =\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0 & \dfrac{D'}{L}\\ -\dfrac{D'}{C} & -\dfrac{1}{RC}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} \begin{bmatrix}\hat i_L\\ \hat v\end{bmatrix} +\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{D}{L} & \dfrac{V_g-V}{L}\\ 0 & \dfrac{I_L}{C}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{B}} \begin{bmatrix}\hat v_g\\ \hat d\end{bmatrix} \]
\(\hat{\mathbf x} = [\hat i_L,\ \hat v]^T\) = state vector · \(\hat{\mathbf u} = [\hat v_g,\ \hat d]^T\) = input vector · \(\mathbf A\), \(\mathbf B\) = constant matrices set by the operating point

Laplace transform with zero initial conditions (the model starts at the operating point): \(\frac{d}{dt}\) becomes \(s\), so \(s\hat{\mathbf x} = \mathbf A\hat{\mathbf x} + \mathbf B\hat{\mathbf u}\) and

\[ \hat{\mathbf x}(s) = (s\mathbf I - \mathbf A)^{-1}\mathbf B\,\hat{\mathbf u}(s),\qquad \hat v(s) = G_{vg}(s)\,\hat v_g(s) + G_{vd}(s)\,\hat d(s) \]
\(s\) = Laplace variable (rad/s) · \(\mathbf I\) = 2 by 2 identity · \(G_{vg}\) = line-to-output transfer function (\(\hat d = 0\); no unit) · \(G_{vd}\) = control-to-output transfer function (\(\hat v_g = 0\); V per unit duty ratio)

\(G_{vg}\) is how the disturbance \(\hat v_g\) reaches \(\hat v\); \(G_{vd}\) is what the controller acts through. Inverting \(s\mathbf I - \mathbf A\) by hand gives the determinant \(\Delta(s) = s^2 + \frac{s}{RC} + \frac{D'^2}{LC}\), and the second row of \((s\mathbf I-\mathbf A)^{-1}\mathbf B\) gives both transfer functions. Dividing the top and bottom by \(\frac{D'^2}{LC}\) puts them in standard form:

\[ G_{vg}(s) = G_{g0}\,\frac{1}{1+\dfrac{s}{Q\omega_0}+\left(\dfrac{s}{\omega_0}\right)^2},\qquad G_{vd}(s) = G_{d0}\,\frac{1-\dfrac{s}{\omega_z}}{1+\dfrac{s}{Q\omega_0}+\left(\dfrac{s}{\omega_0}\right)^2} \]
\[ G_{g0} = -\frac{D}{D'},\quad G_{d0} = -\frac{V_g}{D'^2},\quad \omega_0 = \frac{D'}{\sqrt{LC}},\quad Q = D'R\sqrt{\frac{C}{L}},\quad \omega_z = \frac{D'^2R}{DL} \]
\(G_{g0}\) = dc line gain, equal to \(M(D) = \frac{V}{V_g}\) (no unit) · \(G_{d0}\) = dc control gain, the slope \(\frac{dV}{dD}\) (V) · \(\omega_0\) = corner (resonant) frequency (rad/s), \(f_0 = \frac{\omega_0}{2\pi}\) (Hz) · \(Q\) = quality factor, height of the resonant peak (no unit) · \(\omega_z\) = zero frequency (rad/s), \(f_z = \frac{\omega_z}{2\pi}\) (Hz)

Three things to notice. The dc gains are the steady-state results you already know (\(M(D)\) and the tangent slope). Both transfer functions share the same denominator, from \(\mathbf A\) alone, so they ring at the same frequency. And only \(G_{vd}\) has a zero, written \(1 - \frac{s}{\omega_z}\) (not \(1 + \frac{s}{\omega_z}\)), which is zero at \(s = +\omega_z\): a right-half-plane zero (Section 8). Of the line-gain parameters, only \(Q\) contains \(R\).

Example 5.1 (lecture): inverting sI − A
\[\begin{aligned} s\mathbf I-\mathbf A &= \begin{bmatrix}s & -\dfrac{D'}{L}\\ \dfrac{D'}{C} & s+\dfrac{1}{RC}\end{bmatrix}\\ \Delta(s) &= s\left(s+\frac{1}{RC}\right) + \frac{D'^2}{LC}\\ (s\mathbf I-\mathbf A)^{-1} &= \frac{1}{\Delta(s)}\begin{bmatrix}s+\dfrac{1}{RC} & \dfrac{D'}{L}\\ -\dfrac{D'}{C} & s\end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}\]

The second row times \(\mathbf B\) gives the output voltage:

\[ \hat v(s) = \underbrace{\frac{-\dfrac{DD'}{LC}}{\Delta(s)}}_{G_{vg}(s)}\hat v_g(s) + \underbrace{\frac{\dfrac{I_L}{C}\,s - \dfrac{D'(V_g-V)}{LC}}{\Delta(s)}}_{G_{vd}(s)}\hat d(s) \]
Filled in

The lecture says "divide by \(\frac{D'^2}{LC}\) and use the steady state" for \(G_{vd}\). The steps: the constant term of the numerator becomes \(-\frac{V_g-V}{D'}\), and with \(V_g - V = \frac{V_g}{D'}\) that is \(G_{d0} = -\frac{V_g}{D'^2}\). The \(s\) term becomes \(\frac{I_L L}{D'^2}s\); dividing by \(G_{d0}\) gives \(-\frac{s}{\omega_z}\) with \(\frac{1}{\omega_z} = \frac{I_L L}{V_g}\), and \(I_L = \frac{DV_g}{D'^2R}\) gives \(\omega_z = \frac{D'^2R}{DL}\).

Analogy

Tricky bits
  • \(\omega_0\) is in rad/s; divide by \(2\pi\) for \(f_0\) in Hz. Forgetting this is the classic factor-of-6.28 error.

  • \(\omega_z = \frac{D'^2R}{DL}\): the zero moves down (worse) at heavy load (small \(R\)) and large \(D\).

Section 6Frequency response and decibels

For a sinusoidal input at frequency \(f\), put \(s = j2\pi f\). Then \(|G_{vd}| = \frac{|\hat v|}{|\hat d|}\) is the ratio of the amplitudes and \(\angle G_{vd}\) is the phase of \(\hat v\) against \(\hat d\). Evaluate each factor of the standard form as a complex number in polar form, then multiply magnitudes and add angles.

A Bode plot draws \(G(j2\pi f)\) against \(f\) on a logarithmic axis, twice: its size in decibels and its phase in degrees. The log axis gives each decade (10 Hz to 100 Hz, 100 Hz to 1 kHz, ...) the same width, so 10 Hz to 25 kHz fits on one axis. Decibels squeeze a huge range: \(|G_{vd}|\) goes from 764 V at its peak to 0.45 V at 25 kHz (a ratio of 1700), which is 57.7 dBV and \(-6.9\ \text{dBV}\), 64.6 dB apart. Section 7Drawing Bode plots by hand

Write \(G_{vd}\) as a product of three factors:

\[ G_{vd}(s) = \underbrace{G_{d0}}_{\text{dc gain}}\cdot\underbrace{\left(1-\frac{s}{\omega_z}\right)}_{\text{zero factor}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{1+\dfrac{s}{Q\omega_0}+\left(\dfrac{s}{\omega_0}\right)^2}}_{\text{quadratic factor}} \]

In dB the magnitudes add, and the phases add. So learn each factor's plot once, then stack them. Straight-line asymptotes (the hand sketch) show which constant, and so which component, sets each feature.

\[ \text{one real pole: } G = \frac{1}{1+\dfrac{s}{\omega_0}} \]
Well below \(f_0\): 0 dB, 0°. Well above: \(-20\ \text{dB}\) per decade, \(-90^\circ\); the asymptotes meet at \(f_0\). At \(f_0\): \(|1+j| = \sqrt 2\), so \(-3\ \text{dB}\) and \(-45^\circ\). Phase sketch: 0° at \(\frac{f_0}{10}\) to \(-90^\circ\) at \(10f_0\), within 5.7° of the true curve.
\[ \text{one real zero: } G = 1 \pm \frac{s}{\omega_0} \]
\(1+\frac{s}{\omega_0}\) (zero at \(s = -\omega_0\), left half plane, LHP): the pole upside down, \(+20\ \text{dB}\) per decade and phase 0° to \(+90^\circ\). \(1-\frac{s}{\omega_0}\) (zero at \(s = +\omega_0\), right half plane, RHP): the same magnitude, but the phase goes 0° to \(-90^\circ\), like a pole.
\[ \text{quadratic: } G = \frac{1}{1+\dfrac{s}{Q\omega_0}+\left(\dfrac{s}{\omega_0}\right)^2} \]
Well above \(f_0\): \(-40\ \text{dB}\) per decade (twice a pole) and \(-180^\circ\). At \(f_0\) the denominator is exactly \(\frac{j}{Q}\), so \(|G| = Q\) (\(20\log_{10}Q\) above the asymptotes) and the phase is \(-90^\circ\). Phase asymptote: 0° at \(f_0\,10^{-\frac{1}{2Q}}\) to \(-180^\circ\) at \(f_0\,10^{\frac{1}{2Q}}\). For \(Q \le \frac{1}{\sqrt 2} \approx 0.71\) there is no peak; \(Q = 0.5\) is a double real pole (\(-6\ \text{dB}\) at \(f_0\)); \(Q \lt 0.5\) is two distinct real poles.

Building \(G_{vd}\) for the running example: a flat line at \(45.5\ \text{dBV}\), a resonant peak 12 dB above it at \(f_0 = 398\ \text{Hz}\) (\(Q = 4\)), then \(-40\ \text{dB}\) per decade; above \(f_z = 2.65\ \text{kHz}\) the zero's \(+20\) turns this into \(-20\ \text{dB}\) per decade. The phase goes to \(-180^\circ\) after the resonance, and the RHP zero adds another \(-90^\circ\), so it heads past \(-180^\circ\) toward \(-270^\circ\). For \(G_{vg}\): the same picture without the zero (flat at \(|G_{g0}| = 1.5\), 3.5 dB, then the peak, then \(-40\)); changing \(R\) moves only the peak.

Section 8The RHP zero: a wrong-way start

Step \(d\) from 0.60 to 0.62. The output should end more negative (\(-3.75\ \text{V}\) more, by the tangent), but it first moves the wrong way. The circuit reason:

  • At \(t = 0\), raising \(d\) shortens the diode's conduction time and lengthens the time \(L\) sees \(V_g\). The average diode current is \(\langle i_D\rangle_{T_s} = d'\langle i_L\rangle_{T_s}\), and \(\langle i_L\rangle_{T_s}\) cannot step, so \(\langle i_D\rangle_{T_s}\) drops.
  • For \(0 \lt t \lt 62\ \mu\text{s}\): \(\langle i_C\rangle_{T_s} = -\frac{\langle v\rangle_{T_s}}{R} - \langle i_D\rangle_{T_s} \gt 0\). The diode delivers less than the load takes, so \(C\) discharges and \(|\langle v\rangle_{T_s}|\) falls, though it must end larger.
  • At \(t = 62\ \mu\text{s}\), \(\langle i_C\rangle_{T_s} = 0\); after that the inductor current has grown enough and \(|\langle v\rangle_{T_s}|\) grows toward its new value.

In the transfer function this is the right-half-plane zero. Split \(G_{vd}\) into a plain second-order response \(\hat v_p\) and the zero factor:

\[ \hat v_p(s) = \frac{G_{d0}}{1+\dfrac{s}{Q\omega_0}+\left(\dfrac{s}{\omega_0}\right)^2}\hat d(s),\qquad \hat v(s) = \left(1-\frac{s}{\omega_z}\right)\hat v_p(s)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \hat v(t) = \hat v_p(t) - \frac{1}{\omega_z}\frac{d\hat v_p}{dt} \]
\(\hat v_p\) = response without the zero (V) · \(\omega_z\) = RHP zero frequency (rad/s). Both have the same final value \(G_{d0}\hat d\).

Right after the step, \(\hat v_p\) is heading negative fast, so \(-\frac{1}{\omega_z}\frac{d\hat v_p}{dt}\) is positive and briefly wins: \(\hat v\) starts positive. A RHP zero does not make the plant unstable (that would be a RHP pole), but it limits how fast a loop can be made, because the controller first sees the error get worse.

Section 9The loop, the PWM and the sign

Go around the feedback loop piece by piece:

  • The sensor \(H(s)\) scales \(v\) to the controller's signal level. A resistor divider gives a constant \(H\); a filter or isolation stage adds poles.
  • The junction compares \(Hv\) with \(v_{ref}\): the error is \(v_{ref} - Hv\).
  • The compensator \(G_c(s)\) turns the error into a control voltage \(v_c\).
  • The PWM turns \(v_c\) into the gate signal of \(Q_1\), with duty ratio \(d\).
  • The converter turns \(d\) and \(v_g\) into \(v\); for small changes that is \(G_{vd}\) and \(G_{vg}\).

Modelling the PWM. A sawtooth resets to 0 at the start of each period (the switch turns on) and rises linearly to \(V_M\) over \(T_s\). The switch turns off when the sawtooth reaches \(v_c\), at \(dT_s = \frac{v_c}{V_M}T_s\). So the PWM is just a gain:

\[ d = \frac{v_c}{V_M}\quad (0 \le v_c \le V_M),\qquad D = \frac{V_C}{V_M},\qquad \hat d = \frac{\hat v_c}{V_M} \]
\(v_c\) = control voltage from the compensator (V) · \(V_C\), \(\hat v_c\) = its dc value and small variation (V) · \(V_M\) = peak of the PWM sawtooth (V)

The sign. For a buck, \(G_{d0} = V_g \gt 0\): if \(v\) drops, \(Hv\) falls below \(v_{ref}\), the error and \(d\) rise, and \(v\) rises back. Negative feedback, as drawn. For the inverting buck-boost, \(G_{d0} = -\frac{V_g}{D'^2} \lt 0\): if \(v\) drops (more negative), the error and \(d\) rise, and \(v\) goes more negative. Positive feedback. The fix is a \(-1\) ahead of \(H\), so the loop senses \(-v\) and uses a positive reference.

\[ \hat v = G_{vd}\,\hat d + G_{vg}\,\hat v_g,\qquad \hat d = \frac{\hat v_c}{V_M},\qquad \hat v_c = G_c\,\hat v_e,\qquad \hat v_e = \hat v_{ref} - H(-\hat v) = \hat v_{ref} + H\hat v \]
\(\hat v_e\) = small-signal error (V) · \(\hat v_{ref}\) = small change of the reference (V) · \(H\) = sensor gain (no unit). Every signal is a dc part plus a small change; the dc parts set \(D\) and \(V\), the small-signal loop decides stability, tracking and rejection.

Section 10The loop gain T

Multiply the blocks once around the loop, leaving out the junction's minus sign. That product is the loop gain:

\[ T(s) = -\frac{H\,G_c(s)\,G_{vd}(s)}{V_M} \]
\[ \hat v_e = \frac{\hat v_{ref}}{1+T},\qquad \frac{\hat v}{\hat v_{ref}} = -\frac{1}{H}\,\frac{T}{1+T},\qquad \hat v = \frac{G_{vg}}{1+T}\,\hat v_g \]
\(T\) = loop gain (no unit; the minus sign comes from the \(-1\) and cancels \(G_{d0} \lt 0\)) · \(G_c\) = compensator (no unit here) · \(\frac{1}{1+T}\) = how much of the reference change is left as error, and how much a line disturbance is reduced

At each frequency, a change in \(\hat v\) returns around the loop as \(-T\) times itself. Where \(|T| \gg 1\) the error \(\hat v_e \approx \frac{\hat v_{ref}}{T}\) is small whatever the angle of \(T\), and \(\hat v \approx -\frac{\hat v_{ref}}{H}\): the output follows. Where \(|T| \ll 1\), \(1+T \approx 1\): the loop does not correct. If the loop is stable (next section), feedback reduces the error wherever \(|1+T| \gt 1\).

Example 10.1 (lecture): deriving the closed loop

With \(\hat v_g = 0\), go around the loop from the error:

\[\begin{aligned} \hat v &= G_{vd}\,\frac{G_c\,\hat v_e}{V_M} = -\frac{T}{H}\,\hat v_e\\ \hat v_e &= \hat v_{ref} + H\hat v = \hat v_{ref} - T\,\hat v_e\\ \hat v_e &= \frac{\hat v_{ref}}{1+T},\qquad \hat v = -\frac{1}{H}\,\frac{T}{1+T}\,\hat v_{ref} \end{aligned}\]

With \(\hat v_{ref} = 0\) instead, \(\hat v = G_{vg}\hat v_g - T\hat v\), so \(\hat v = \frac{G_{vg}\hat v_g}{1+T}\).

Both the tracking error and the line disturbance are divided by \(1+T\)

  • The closed-loop output is \(\frac{T}{1+T}\) of \(-\frac{\hat v_{ref}}{H}\): remember the \(\frac{1}{H}\).

  • Large \(|T|\) helps only if the loop is stable; that test is next.

Section 11Crossover, phase margin and overshoot

The closed-loop poles are the roots of \(1+T = 0\), that is \(T = -1\): \(|T| = 1\) and \(\angle T = -180^\circ\) together. So read the Bode plot of \(T\) at the frequency where \(|T|\) crosses 1:

\[ |T(f_c)| = 1\ (0\ \text{dB}),\qquad \phi_m = 180^\circ + \angle T(f_c) \]
\(f_c\) = crossover frequency (Hz) · \(\phi_m\) = phase margin (degrees): how far \(\angle T\) is above \(-180^\circ\) at \(f_c\), the extra lag the loop can tolerate before going unstable

The test: if \(T\) has no right-half-plane poles and \(|T|\) crosses 1 once, the loop is stable if \(\phi_m \gt 0\) and unstable if \(\phi_m \lt 0\). Why: a step contains every frequency. At the frequency where \(\angle T = -180^\circ\), the junction's minus sign brings a signal back around the loop in phase, multiplied by \(|T|\) each pass. With \(\phi_m \lt 0\) that frequency is below \(f_c\) where \(|T| \gt 1\), so it grows; with \(\phi_m \gt 0\) it is above \(f_c\) where \(|T| \lt 1\), so it dies away. Less \(\phi_m\) means more ringing.

Estimating overshoot. For the loop \(T = \frac{\omega_1}{s\left(1+\frac{s}{\omega_2}\right)}\) (an integrator and a pole) the closed loop is exactly second order with

\[ Q \approx \frac{\sqrt{\cos\phi_m}}{\sin\phi_m},\qquad \zeta = \frac{1}{2Q},\qquad \%\mathrm{OS} = 100\,e^{-\frac{\pi\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}}\qquad (0 \lt \phi_m \lt 90^\circ) \]
\(Q\) = closed-loop quality factor (no unit) · \(\zeta\) = damping ratio (no unit) · \(\%\mathrm{OS}\) = percent overshoot of a reference step · \(\omega_1\), \(\omega_2\) = integrator gain and pole of the example loop (rad/s)

For a real design: read \(f_c\) and \(\phi_m\) on the complete \(T\) at the edges of the operating range, estimate the overshoot with these formulas, then verify where \(\phi_m\) is lowest by simulating a small reference step (for example 2 % of \(v_{ref}\)). Larger \(\phi_m\) gives lower \(Q\): less ringing and overshoot.

Section 12Shaping T and the ripple limit

\(H\), \(V_M\) and \(G_{vd}\) are fixed; \(G_c\) is ours to choose, and its Bode plot adds to theirs. What we want from \(|T|\):

  1. Toward dc, \(|T| \to \infty\). The reference is dc, and both the error and a line change's \(\hat v\) are divided by \(1+T\), so both go to zero.
  2. At \(f_c\), cross 1 once, so the phase-margin test applies, with \(\phi_m\) large enough for the overshoot allowed.
  3. Choose \(f_c\). The loop follows the parts of a step below \(f_c\), so higher \(f_c\) is faster. But it must leave phase for \(\phi_m\) against the converter's lag (resonance and RHP zero), and stay well below \(f_s\).

Why well below \(f_s\). The PWM model \(\frac{1}{V_M}\) assumed \(v_c\) constant over a period. Take an ideal buck: the switch node is \(v_{sw} = V_g\,q\), where \(q\) is the gate signal (1 for \(dT_s\), else 0). That is \(dV_g\) plus harmonics of \(f_s\). The output filter attenuates them, and the feedback carries what is left back to \(v_c\):

\[ F(s) = \frac{v(s)}{v_{sw}(s)} = \frac{1}{LCs^2 + \frac{L}{R}s + 1},\qquad A_q = \frac{2}{\pi}\sin(\pi D) \le \frac{2}{\pi} \approx 0.637 \]
\[ \frac{A_c}{V_M} = \underbrace{\frac{V_g\,|F\,H\,G_c(f_s)|}{V_M}}_{|T(f_s)|}\,A_q \]
\(F\) = output-filter transfer function (no unit) · \(A_q\) = peak amplitude of the gate signal's component at \(f_s\) (no unit) · \(A_c\) = peak ripple at \(f_s\) on \(v_c\) (V) · \(G_{vd} = V_gF\) for the buck, so the bracket is exactly \(|T(f_s)|\)

So design for \(|T(f_s)| \ll 1\): ripple on \(v_c\) is a fraction \(|T(f_s)|\,A_q\) of the sawtooth height, and a large one makes the comparator cross several times or at the wrong instant.

\[ |T(f_s)| = \frac{f_c}{f_s} = \frac{1}{20} \]
\(|T(f_s)| = \frac{1}{20}\), so the ripple on \(v_c\) is at most \(\frac{0.637}{20} = 3.2\,\%\) of \(V_M\)
Filled in

Analogy

A thermostat that reads a thermometer sitting right next to a fan heater that pulses every second: if it reacts to every pulse, it chatters. You want it to respond to the room's slow temperature, not to the pulses, so its "speed" must be far below the pulse rate.

Tricky bits
  • High \(|T|\) at low frequency is good; high \(|T|\) at \(f_s\) is bad. The loop gain must fall in between.
  • \(A_q\) peaks at \(D = 0.5\), not at \(D = 1\); at \(D = 0.5\) it is \(\frac{2}{\pi}\), not 1 or 0.5.
  • With a \(-20\ \text{dB}\) per decade slope, \(|T(f_s)| = \frac{f_c}{f_s}\); with \(-40\) it would be \(\left(\frac{f_c}{f_s}\right)^2\).

Section 13The PI compensator over the whole range

The proportional-integral (PI, or lag) compensator has two knobs:

\[ G_c(s) = G_{cm}\left(1+\frac{\omega_L}{s}\right) = G_{cm}\,\frac{s+\omega_L}{s} \]
\(G_{cm}\) = gain above the corner (no unit) · \(\omega_L = 2\pi f_L\) = corner (rad/s), \(f_L\) in Hz. Part 4 · Delay and feedforward

Section 15The sampling delay

A digital controller does three things each period: sample at the valley of a symmetric (triangle) PWM carrier, compute the new duty ratio, and load it at the next valley, where it is held for one period. The PWM's effect is centred half a period after loading, because the new duty moves both pulse edges symmetrically about the period's midpoint. So the duty that reacts to a measurement acts, on average, about one and a half periods after it:

\[ \tau \approx T_s + \frac{T_s}{2} = 1.5\,T_s,\qquad G_d(s) = e^{-s\tau} \]
\[ |e^{-j\omega\tau}| = 1,\qquad \angle e^{-j\omega\tau} = -\omega\tau\ \text{rad},\qquad \phi_m = \underbrace{180^\circ + \angle T_0(f_c)}_{\phi_{m0}} - 360^\circ f_c\,\tau \]
\(\tau\) = total delay from sample to effect (s) · \(T_s = \frac{1}{f_s}\) = switching period (s) · \(G_d\) = delay block (a time shift \(x(t-\tau)\) has Laplace transform \(e^{-s\tau}X(s)\)) · \(T_0\) = loop gain without the delay · \(\phi_{m0}\) = phase margin without the delay (°)

The delay leaves \(|T|\) and \(f_c\) unchanged but subtracts phase that grows linearly with frequency. Insert \(G_d\) between the duty command and the converter: \(T = T_0\,G_d\), and the closed-loop denominator becomes \(1 + T_0G_d\). Choose \(G_c\) and \(f_c\) with the delay included. Moving the sample closer to the load instant shortens the wait (the computation must still finish before loading):

Sample before duty loadTotal delay \(\tau\)
One period (as above)\(1.5\,T_s\)
Half a period\(T_s\)
Just before load\(\approx 0.5\,T_s\)

To use pole-zero tools, replace the exponential by the first-order Padé approximation:

\[ e^{-s\tau} \approx \frac{1-\frac{s\tau}{2}}{1+\frac{s\tau}{2}} \]
an all-pass with a RHP zero at \(\frac{2}{\tau}\): magnitude 1, phase \(-2\arctan\frac{\omega\tau}{2}\); close to \(-\omega\tau\) near crossover, departs at higher frequencies

Section 16Feedforward from the input voltage

With feedback alone, the controller supplies the whole duty ratio, and an input change is corrected only after it has produced an output error. But the ideal CCM steady state already says which duty gives the wanted output for the measured input. For the buck-boost loop of Section 9 (target \(\langle v\rangle_{T_s} = -\frac{v_{ref}}{H}\)), set \(\langle v\rangle_{T_s} = -\frac{d\,\langle v_g\rangle_{T_s}}{1-d}\) equal to the target and solve for \(d\):

\[ d_{\mathrm{ff}} = \frac{v_{ref}}{v_{ref} + H\langle v_g\rangle_{T_s}},\qquad d = d_{\mathrm{ff}} + d_{\mathrm{fb}},\qquad d_{\mathrm{fb}} = \frac{v_c}{V_M} \]
\(d_{\mathrm{ff}}\) = feedforward duty ratio, predicted from the measured input (no unit) · \(d_{\mathrm{fb}}\) = feedback correction (no unit) · \(v_c\) = compensator output (V). With one physical PWM, command the voltage \(V_Md_{\mathrm{ff}} + v_c\).

An input rise now reduces \(d_{\mathrm{ff}}\) at once, without waiting for an error. Keep the feedback: it corrects losses, model errors and load changes, and the converter's dynamics and RHP zero are still there. Feedforward does not change the loop gain, so it does not change stability.

Reference steps need shaping. At a reference step, \(d_{\mathrm{ff}}\) jumps to the duty for the new equilibrium, and that duty step excites the \(L\)-\(C\) resonance (there is no supply change for it to cancel). Here the resonance is above the feedback crossover, so feedback cannot damp it. The cure is to low-pass filter the command (\(\tau = 5\ \text{ms}\) in the lecture removes the overshoot). Do not filter the measured \(v_g\): that would delay the correction of a disturbance that has already arrived.

Part 5 · Controlling an ac current

Section 17The current reference from P and Q

Now the converter is a single-phase full-bridge inverter feeding the grid \(v_g\) through an inductor \(L\). The goal: deliver a chosen real power \(P\) and reactive power \(Q_g\) by controlling \(i_L\). Take the grid voltage as the phase reference (Lecture 6): \(\underline V_g = \hat V_g\angle 0\), bridge voltage \(\underline V_t = \hat V_t\angle\delta\). Then

\[ \underline I_L = \frac{\underline V_t - \underline V_g}{j\omega_1L} = \underbrace{\frac{\hat V_t\sin\delta}{\omega_1L}}_{I_P} + j\underbrace{\frac{\hat V_g - \hat V_t\cos\delta}{\omega_1L}}_{I_Q} \]
\[ \underline S_g = \tfrac12\underline V_g\underline I_L^*:\quad P = \tfrac12\hat V_gI_P,\quad Q_g = -\tfrac12\hat V_gI_Q\quad\Longrightarrow\quad i_P^* = \frac{2P^*}{\hat V_g},\quad i_Q^* = -\frac{2Q_g^*}{\hat V_g} \]
\(\hat V_g\), \(\hat V_t\) = peak grid and bridge voltages (V) · \(\delta\) = angle of \(\underline V_t\) (rad) · \(\omega_1 = 2\pi f_1\) = grid angular frequency (rad/s) · \(I_P\), \(I_Q\) = peak in-phase and quadrature current (A) · \(P\) (W), \(Q_g\) (var) = power absorbed by the grid · star = reference (command)

A phase-locked loop (PLL) follows the grid and supplies \(\sin\theta\) in phase with \(v_g\), \(\cos\theta\) 90° ahead, \(\hat V_g\) and \(\omega_1\). The current waveform to track is

\[ i^* = i_P^*\sin\theta + i_Q^*\cos\theta \]
\(\theta\) = grid angle from the PLL (rad). At unity power factor \(i_Q^* = 0\). An outer dc-link loop may set \(P^*\) (Section 20). In PLECS's single-phase PLL, use the synchronous output as \(\sin\theta\) and negate the 90°-delayed output to get \(\cos\theta\).

What the switches control. Around the loop, \(L\frac{di_L}{dt} = v_t - v_g\). With bipolar PWM (Lecture 7), \(v_t = \pm v_d\), \(m = 2d-1\) and \(\langle v_t\rangle_{T_s} = m\langle v_d\rangle_{T_s}\). Averaged over one period (\(f_s \gg f_1\), \(m\) held per period), \(m\) sets the slope of the current:

\[ L\frac{d\langle i_L\rangle_{T_s}}{dt} = m\langle v_d\rangle_{T_s} - \langle v_g\rangle_{T_s} \]
\(m\) = modulation index (−1 to 1, no unit) · \(v_d\) = dc-link voltage (V) · \(d\) = duty ratio of the bridge's first leg

Section 18Feedforward plus feedback for the current

As with the buck-boost, feedback uses the error \(e = i^* - \langle i_L\rangle_{T_s}\), and \(G_c(s)\) turns it into a voltage correction \(v_c\). But the averaged circuit equation predicts the bridge voltage needed to follow \(i^*\) even when \(e = 0\):

\[ v_{t,\mathrm{ff}} = \langle v_g\rangle_{T_s} + L\frac{di^*}{dt},\qquad m = \frac{\langle v_g\rangle_{T_s} + L\frac{di^*}{dt} + v_c}{\langle v_d\rangle_{T_s}} \]
\(v_{t,\mathrm{ff}}\) = predicted bridge voltage (V) · \(v_c\) = feedback correction (V) · \(v_L^* = L\frac{di^*}{dt} + v_c\) = requested inductor voltage (V). Ideal averaged model, before delay and saturation (\(|m| \le 1\)).

With \(\langle v_t\rangle_{T_s} = m\langle v_d\rangle_{T_s} = v_t^*\), the grid term cancels and \(L\frac{d\langle i_L\rangle_{T_s}}{dt} = v_L^*\). In code, sample at the PWM valley (as in Section 15) to approximate \(\langle i_L\rangle_{T_s}\).

Differentiate the reference analytically. With \(i_P^*\), \(i_Q^*\) constant and \(\dot\theta = \omega_1\), the derivative of a sinusoid leads it by 90°, so the \(sL\) block becomes

\[ v_{L,\mathrm{ff}} = \omega_1L\left(i_P^*\cos\theta - i_Q^*\sin\theta\right),\qquad v_{g,\mathrm{ff}} = \hat V_g\sin(\theta + \omega_1\tau) \]
\(v_{L,\mathrm{ff}}\) = \(L\frac{di^*}{dt}\) built from the PLL's sine and cosine, no numerical differentiation (V) · \(v_{g,\mathrm{ff}}\) = grid voltage predicted at the moment the command acts, \(\tau\) after the sample (V) · \(\tau = 1.5T_s\) (s)

The plant for feedback design. Split each averaged signal into a nominal sinusoid (subscript 0) and a perturbation. The nominal waveforms satisfy \(L\frac{di_{L0}}{dt} = v_{t0} - v_{g0}\); subtracting leaves \(L\frac{d\hat i_L}{dt} = \hat v_t - \hat v_g\), already linear. With ideal grid feedforward \(\hat v_L = \hat v_L^*\), so

\[ G_p(s) = \frac{\hat i_L(s)}{\hat v_L(s)} = \frac{1}{sL},\qquad T(s) = G_c(s)\,G_p(s) = \frac{G_c(s)}{sL} \]
\(G_p\) = current plant (A per V, so \(\frac{1}{\Omega}\) after \(s\)) · \(G_c\) here has units of Ω · the reference feedforward is outside the loop, so it does not appear in \(T\)

With exact feedforward and zero initial perturbations, \(\hat i_L = \hat i^*\). The implemented \(v_{L,\mathrm{ff}}\) omits the terms from changes in \(i_P^*\), \(i_Q^*\), so feedback must supply those, plus any grid mismatch. Which \(G_c\) removes a residual 60 Hz error? Section 19.

Section 19The proportional-resonant controller

Imperfect feedforward leaves a sinusoidal error at the grid frequency for feedback to correct. A PI has infinite gain at dc but only finite gain at 60 Hz, so it leaves a steady 60 Hz error. Put the gain where the error is. Resonant action has infinite gain at \(\omega_1\):

\[ \text{ideal PR: } G_c(s) = K_p + \underbrace{\frac{2K_is}{s^2+\omega_1^2}}_{G_r(s)},\qquad \text{practical PR: } G_c(s) = K_p + \underbrace{K_r\frac{2\omega_bs}{s^2+2\omega_bs+\omega_1^2}}_{G_r(s)} \]
\(K_p\) = proportional gain (Ω), sets the gain far from \(f_1\) and so the crossover: \(K_p = 2\pi f_cL\) · \(K_i\) = resonant gain of the ideal form (Ω per second) · \(K_r\) = resonant gain at \(f_1\) (Ω): at \(s = j\omega_1\), \(G_r = K_r\) and \(G_c = K_p + K_r\) · \(\omega_b = 2\pi f_b\) = damping (rad/s); the resonant term's 3 dB band is about \(f_1 \pm f_b\) when \(f_b \ll f_1\)

In a stable, unsaturated loop the ideal PR removes a steady sinusoidal error at exactly \(f_1\). The practical form trades infinite gain for a finite peak of chosen width: more \(K_r\) gives less residual error (band unchanged); more \(f_b\) tolerates more grid-frequency drift (peak unchanged). The cost of both: a finite remaining error, and phase lag above \(f_1\), which eats phase margin. Implement \(G_c\) on \(e\); it needs \(\omega_1\), while the PLL's angle builds \(i^*\).

Section 20The dc-link voltage loop

Until now an ideal source held \(v_d\). Now a source \(i_d\) feeds the dc-link capacitor \(C\) (a PV panel or its dc-dc stage, a battery, a machine through an active rectifier, or a resistor), delivering a set power \(p_d\) independent of \(v_d\). Averaged over a line cycle, neglecting losses and changes in the mean inductor energy, the capacitor stores the imbalance:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12Cv_d^2\right) = p_d - P\quad\Longrightarrow\quad Cv_d\frac{dv_d}{dt} = p_d - P \]
\(v_d\) = dc-link voltage (V) · \(C\) = dc-link capacitance (F) · \(p_d\) = incoming power (W), a disturbance · \(P\) = power exported to the grid (W), set through the current loop

The inverter regulates the link through \(P\): if \(p_d\) rises, \(v_d\) rises until the inverter exports more, and to restore \(V_d^*\) it briefly exports more than \(p_d\). Choose \(V_d^*\) above the peak of \(v_t^*\) (set by \(v_g\), \(L\) and the current) with margin for the ripple, so \(|m| \le 1\) always.

The plant. With the current loop treated as 1 (\(P \approx P^*\)), perturb \(v_d = V_d + \hat v_d\), \(p_d = P_0 + \hat p_d\), \(P^* = P_0 + \hat p^*\), drop the product \(C\hat v_d\frac{d\hat v_d}{dt}\), and hold \(\hat p_d = 0\):

\[ CV_d\frac{d\hat v_d}{dt} = \hat p_d - \hat p^*,\qquad \frac{\hat v_d(s)}{\hat p^*(s)} = -\frac{1}{CV_ds} \]
a negative integrator: extra export removes capacitor energy · \(V_d\) = operating dc-link voltage (V) · \(\hat p^*\) = power-command perturbation (W)

Close the loop with the right sign. High voltage must command more export, so use measured minus reference, \(e_v = v_d - V_d^*\). That cancels the plant's minus sign, and a PI removes steady error (a gain alone would need a steady \(e_v \ne 0\) to hold \(P^* = p_d\)):

\[ \hat p^* = G_{cv}(s)\,\hat e_v,\qquad G_{cv}(s) = G_{cvm}\left(1+\frac{\omega_{Lv}}{s}\right),\qquad T_v(s) = \frac{G_{cv}(s)}{CV_ds} \]
\[ |T_v(f_{co})| = 1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad G_{cvm} = \frac{2\pi f_{co}\,CV_d}{\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{f_{Lv}}{f_{co}}\right)^2}} \]
\(G_{cvm}\) = PI gain above its corner (W per V) · \(\omega_{Lv} = 2\pi f_{Lv}\) = PI corner (rad/s) · \(f_{co}\) = voltage-loop crossover (Hz) · \(T_v\) = voltage loop gain (no unit); closed-loop poles satisfy \(1 + T_v = 0\)

Choosing the numbers. \(f_{co}\) must be well below 120 Hz (Section 21): 10 Hz. Put the PI corner below crossover to limit its lag there: \(f_{Lv} = \frac{f_{co}}{5} = 2\ \text{Hz}\). Regulate the mean of \(v_d\) and let the 120 Hz ripple be.

Section 21Two loops: slow outside, fast inside

The voltage loop commands the current loop: it sets \(i_P^* = \frac{2P^*}{\hat V_g}\), and the inner loop tracks \(i^* = i_P^*\sin\theta + i_Q^*\cos\theta\). Averaged over a line cycle, \(P = \frac12\hat V_gi_P\) for any \(i_Q\), so \(\frac{P}{P^*} = \frac{i_P}{i_P^*}\).

Why the outer loop must be slow. Single-phase power pulses at twice the line frequency. With \(v_g = \hat V_g\sin\theta\) and \(i_L = i_P\sin\theta\):

\[ p_g = v_gi_L = P\,(1-\cos 2\theta) \]
\(p_g\) = instantaneous grid power (W) · \(P\) = its average (W) · \(2\theta\): the pulsation is at 120 Hz on a 60 Hz grid

Even when \(P = p_d\) on average, the capacitor's energy rises and falls at 120 Hz, so \(v_d\) ripples. If the voltage PI follows that ripple, \(P^*\) and \(i_P^*\) oscillate at 120 Hz, and multiplying a varying amplitude by \(\sin\theta\) puts a 180 Hz third harmonic in \(i^*\). So keep \(|T_v|\) small at 120 Hz: \(f_{co}\) well below it (10 Hz here, \(|T_v| = 0.081\) at 120 Hz).

Why the inner loop must be much faster. The voltage loop sees the whole current loop as one block \(G_i = \frac{\hat p}{\hat p^*}\), and \(T_v = \frac{G_{cv}\,G_i}{CV_ds}\). A change in \(\hat p^*\) at \(f\) moves \(\hat i^*\) at \(60 \pm f\) Hz, where \(|T_i| \gg 1\) when \(f_c \gg 60\ \text{Hz}\), so \(G_i \approx 1\). Nothing of the voltage loop is inside \(T_i\) (the bridge's \(\times v_d\) cancels the \(\frac{1}{v_d}\) in \(m\)). Model \(G_i \approx \frac{1}{1+\frac{s}{2\pi f_c}}\) and it costs \(\arctan\frac{f_{co}}{f_c}\) of phase at \(f_{co}\):

\[ \phi_{m} \approx 90^\circ - \arctan\frac{f_{Lv}}{f_{co}} - \arctan\frac{f_{co}}{f_c},\qquad \text{rule of thumb } f_c \ge 10f_{co} \]
\(f_c\) = current-loop crossover (Hz) · \(f_{co}\) = voltage-loop crossover (Hz) · \(T_i = \frac{G_c}{sL}\) = current loop gain

Section 22Totem-pole and boost PFC

The totem-pole PFC is the full bridge run as a unity-power-factor rectifier. In the positive half-cycle \(Q_4\) stays on; \(Q_2\) stores energy in \(L\) and \(Q_1\) transfers it to the dc link. In the negative half-cycle \(Q_3\) stays on and \(Q_1\), \(Q_2\) swap roles. Only leg \(a\) switches at \(f_s\); leg \(b\) changes over at the grid zero crossings, so switching loss is confined to \(Q_1\), \(Q_2\).

\[ \langle v_t\rangle_{T_s} = (d_a - d_b)\langle v_d\rangle_{T_s},\qquad d_b = \begin{cases}0 & v_g \gt 0\\ 1 & v_g \lt 0\end{cases},\qquad d_a = m + d_b \]
\(d_a\), \(d_b\) = on-fractions of \(Q_1\) and \(Q_3\) (no unit), each clamped to \([0, 1]\) · \(m\) = requested modulation from the inverter's controller (no unit)

With \(d_a - d_b = m\) away from the duty limits, the inverter's current plant, feedforward and PR controller carry over unchanged (bipolar PWM used \(d_b = 1-d_a\) instead). Power now flows into the bridge, so with \(i_L\) still defined toward the grid, rectification needs \(i_P^* \lt 0\) and \(P^* \lt 0\). The voltage plant stays \(-\frac{1}{CV_ds}\); the voltage controller just commands negative power.

At the zero crossing \(i^*\) crosses zero with a nonzero slope, so tracking it needs \(\langle v_L\rangle_{T_s} = L\frac{di^*}{dt} \ne 0\). Just after the crossing the voltage available to build \(|i_L|\) is at most \(|v_g|\), which is nearly zero, so \(d_a\) hits its limit and the current lags its reference until \(|v_g|\) grows; the recovery time depends on the controller. Both duties must change in the same PWM period at the crossing; updating only one gives the wrong bridge voltage and can cause a large current spike.

The boost PFC (Lecture 10): a diode bridge feeds a boost converter \(|v_g|\), and \(Q_1\) shapes \(\langle i_L\rangle_{T_s}\) like \(|v_g|\). With \(Q_1\) on, \(v_L = |v_g|\); off, \(v_L = |v_g| - v_d\). Averaged in CCM:

\[ L\frac{d\langle i_L\rangle_{T_s}}{dt} = d\,|v_g| + (1-d)(|v_g| - v_d) = |v_g| - (1-d)\,v_d\quad\Longrightarrow\quad d = 1 - \frac{|v_g| - v_L^*}{v_d} \]
\[ i^* = \hat I^*|\sin\theta|,\qquad v_L^* = L\frac{di^*}{dt} + v_c,\qquad v_c = G_c(s)\left(i^* - \langle i_L\rangle_{T_s}\right) \]
\(v_L^*\) = requested inductor voltage (V) · \(\hat I^*\) = current amplitude set by the voltage loop (A) · at \(v_L^* = 0\), \(d = 1 - \frac{|v_g|}{v_d}\), the steady-state boost duty of Lecture 10. The current loop uses a PI (as in Part 3) since \(i^*\) is a rectified sine.

Analogy

The totem pole is a two-lane road where one lane (leg \(b\)) only changes direction twice per cycle, while all the busy traffic (switching) runs in the other lane. Changing the slow lane's direction must happen at the same instant as the fast lane adjusts, or cars collide (the current spike).

Tricky bits
  • Rectification with \(i_L\) defined toward the grid means \(i_P^* \lt 0\) and \(P^* \lt 0\).
  • \(d_a = m + d_b\): in the negative half-cycle add 1. The bipolar rule \(d_a = \frac{1+m}{2}\) does not apply here.
  • Boost PFC uses \(|v_g|\) (after the diode bridge) and \(i^* = \hat I^*|\sin\theta|\).
  • In \(d = 1 - \frac{|v_g| - v_L^*}{v_d}\), a positive \(v_L^*\) increases \(d\).
