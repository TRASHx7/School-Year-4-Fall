Transformers and the flyback converter

A transformer is two windings sharing one magnetic flux. Understand where that shared flux comes from, what it costs (magnetizing current) and what escapes (leakage), and the flyback falls out naturally: it is a transformer used on purpose as an energy store, charged while the switch is on and emptied into the output while it is off.

Part 1 · The ideal transformer

01Why isolate, and the ideal transformer

A transformer does two jobs: galvanic isolation (no wire connects input to output) and changing voltage and current levels. Inside a converter, that buys you safety, a floating supply (for example a high-side gate drive), big step-up or step-down ratios without extreme duty cycles, and several isolated outputs from one core.

We model it in two layers. The ideal transformer only captures the turns ratio: it neither loses nor stores energy. The practical model (Part 2) adds magnetizing and leakage inductance around it.

Voltages are positive at the dots. With i1 entering its dot and i2 leaving its dot:

v1 = N1N2 v2     v1i1 = v2i2  ⇒  i1 = N2N1 i2

Power matches at every instant, p1(t) = p2(t). Whatever waveform you apply, sine or not, appears on the other side immediately, scaled. Nothing is stored or delayed.

Worked example (lecture): N1 : N2 = 2 : 1
Given: 20 V on the primary, the secondary supplies 2 A.
v2 = (N2/N1) v1 = (1/2)(20 V) = 10 V
i1 = (N2/N1) i2 = (1/2)(2 A) = 1 A
Check: p1 = 20 V × 1 A = 20 W = p2 = 10 V × 2 A. Voltage halves, current doubles.

Analogy · a lever

A lever trades force for distance: push the long end a long way with a small force, and the short end moves a little with a big force. The work in equals the work out. A transformer trades voltage for current the same way, and the turns ratio is the lever arm ratio.

Tricky bits
  • The current ratio is flipped. Voltage scales with N1/N2, current with N2/N1. If you forget which, use power: the side with more voltage has less current.
  • The ideal element passes DC. Try the DC button above: the equations don't care. A real transformer can't do this (you'll see why in §6). The ideal element is a bookkeeping device, not a physical part.
  • Dot convention matters. "Positive at the dots" fixes the sign of v2. The flyback (§10) depends on a secondary connected with the opposite polarity.

02Magnetic field, flux, and why a core

Current in a winding creates a magnetic field. The flux density B (tesla) describes its strength and direction. The part of B perpendicular to a surface tells you how much field passes through each square metre. Add that up over the surface and you get the flux Φ (weber):

Φ = ∫A B·n̂ dA   →   Φ = BA for uniform B normal to the surface 1 Wb = 1 T·m²

Flux lines are closed loops. Everything that goes up through the winding has to come back down somewhere outside it. The lecture compares three cases with the same winding (100 turns, 0.10 A) and the same measuring point on the axis:

CaseB on axisWhat limits it
Coil in air0.47 mTThe field spreads through the whole surrounding volume.
Straight core (rod)3.78 mT (about 8×)Flux crowds into the rod, but every loop still returns through air. That air section limits the flux.
Closed core360 mT (about 100× the rod)Each loop closes inside the core. The field outside stays small.
Try it: same winding, three magnetic paths
Analogy · a water pump and pipes

The winding is a pump pushing water (flux) around a loop. In open air the water spreads everywhere and the flow at the centre is a trickle. A rod is a pipe for part of the loop, but the water still has to find its way back through a swamp. A closed core is a complete pipe loop: the same pump now drives a strong, well-directed flow, and anything placed along the pipe (a second winding) sees all of it.

Tricky bits
  • The weakest part of the loop wins. The rod only gets 8× because the air return path dominates. Closing the path is what gives the big jump (about 766× the air case).
  • Why a closed core makes a transformer possible: a second winding placed anywhere on the centre limb links nearly the same Φ. That shared flux is the whole mechanism of §4.
  • Units: mT vs T and cm² vs m² catch people. 1 cm² = 10−4 m²; 1 mm² = 10−6 m².

03Flux linkage and Faraday's law

A turn links flux when that flux passes through the surface bounded by the turn. A winding links the flux once per turn, so if every turn links the same flux, the flux linkage is

λ = Σk=1N Φk = NΦ units: V·s (same as Wb)

Faraday's law (terminal voltage, passive sign convention) says the winding voltage is the rate of change of flux linkage. Integrate it and you see that voltage sets how λ changes, not what λ is:

v1 = dλ1dt = N1dΦdt     λ1(t) = λ1(0) + ∫0t v1(τ) dτ
Worked example (lecture): +20 V for 5 µs, N1 = 50
Δλ1 = ∫ v1 dt = (20 V)(5 µs) = 100 µV·s (area under the voltage)
ΔΦ = Δλ1 / N1 = 100 µV·s / 50 = 2 µWb

Analogy · a bathtub

Voltage is the tap, flux linkage is the water level. The tap only sets how fast the level rises or falls. It says nothing about how much water was already in the tub. Open the tap at 20 "units" for 5 µs and the level rises by exactly 100, whatever it started at.

Tricky bits
  • λ vs Φ. λ is per winding (N times Φ). Φ is the core's flux. Divide by N to go from one to the other.
  • Zero volts means constant flux, not zero flux. With v1 = 0, dλ/dt = 0, so λ just stays where it was.
  • Area, not height. A big voltage for a short time can give the same Δλ as a small voltage for a long time. Always multiply V by time.

04Shared flux explains the turns ratio

If both windings link the same changing core flux, Faraday's law applies to each with the same dΦ/dt. The result: every turn, on either winding, sees the same voltage.

v1 = N1dΦdt,   v2 = N2dΦdt   ⇒   v1N1 = v2N2 volts per turn is the same

That's exactly the ideal transformer's voltage equation from §1, now with a physical reason. The dots mark the ends that rise together. More turns give more voltage for the same flux change. Flux that is not shared is leakage (§7).

Try it: volts per turn
Uses the §3 example: ΔΦ = 2 µWb in 5 µs, so dΦ/dt = 0.4 V per turn. N2 = 25 is this guide's choice (a 2 : 1 ratio).
Analogy · stairs in one building

Two staircases in the same building climb the same total height. Each step (turn) has the same rise (volts per turn). A staircase with more steps covers more height. Sharing the building is what forces the steps to match.

Tricky bits
  • This ratio holds only for the shared flux. Leakage adds a small extra voltage drop in a real transformer (§7).
  • The ratio says nothing about the current needed to create the flux. That missing current is the magnetizing current (§5).

Part 2 · The real transformer

05Magnetizing current and Lm

Leave the secondary open (i2 = 0) and apply an alternating voltage to the primary. The ideal element predicts i1 = 0, since there's no load. A real winding still draws current. Why? The voltage sets how the flux changes (§3), but flux in the core needs current in the winding to produce it (§2). That current is the magnetizing current im.

In a linear model, flux linkage is proportional to that current. The constant is the magnetizing inductance Lm, drawn in parallel with the ideal primary:

λ1 = N1Φ = Lmim   ⇒   v1 = Lmdimdt Under load:  i1 = N2N1i2 + im     Stored energy: W = λ²2Lm = ½Lmim²
Large Lm (mains transformer)Small Lm (flyback, gapped core)
im ≈ 0, so it behaves like the ideal element.The delivered energy passes through im: it's stored as W = λ²/2Lm, then released.
Worked example (lecture waveform): +20 V for 5 µs, then −10 V for 10 µs
The slide plots v1, λ1 and im. Reading the peaks: λ1 reaches 100 µV·s while im reaches 0.10 A.
Rise: Δλ1 = (20 V)(5 µs) = 100 µV·s
Fall: Δλ1 = (−10 V)(10 µs) = −100 µV·s  back to zero each 15 µs: a triangle, like the buck inductor
Lm = λ1/im = 100 µV·s / 0.10 A = 1 mH (implied by the plot)
Peak energy: W = λ²/2Lm = (100 µV·s)² / (2 × 1 mH) = 5 µJ = ½(1 mH)(0.10 A)²

Analogy · a spring you have to wind

Even with nothing attached to the output shaft, winding a clock spring takes effort. That effort is the magnetizing current: the price of setting up the flux. A stiff spring (large Lm) barely needs any current for the flux you want. A soft spring (small Lm) takes more current but stores more energy for the same volt-seconds, which is exactly what a flyback wants.

Tricky bits
  • im is not a load current. It flows even with the secondary open, and adds on top of the reflected load current: i1 = (N2/N1)i2 + im.
  • Smaller Lm, more energy. W = λ²/2Lm: the voltage fixes λ, so halving Lm doubles the energy stored per cycle. That feels backwards if you're used to ½Li² with fixed current.
  • Same shapes as the buck inductor. Constant voltage gives a straight-line ramp in λ and im. Nothing new to learn, just a new name.

06Volt-second balance and core size

Just like the buck inductor, a transformer in steady state needs volt-second balance:

∫0Ts v1 dt = 0

Excess positive volt-seconds make im and the flux drift upward every cycle, toward saturation. This is why a real transformer can't pass DC.

Worked example (lecture): the unbalanced waveform
Same 20 V for 5 µs, but now −10 V for only 5 µs (Ts = 10 µs), Lm = 1 mH.
Up: (20 V)(5 µs) = 100 µV·s  →  im rises 100 µV·s / 1 mH = 0.10 A
Down: (10 V)(5 µs) = 50 µV·s  →  im falls 0.05 A
Net: +50 µV·s per cycle  →  im climbs 0.05 A every 10 µs
Cycle ends at 0.05, 0.10, 0.15 A, ... with peaks 0.10, 0.15, 0.20 A. It never settles.

Core size follows volt-seconds

From §3, the flux swing over the on-time is the volt-seconds divided by turns. Divide by the core area to get the flux density swing:

ΔΦ = 1N1∫0DTs v1 dt ≈ VDN1fs,    ΔΦ = AcΔB  ⇒  ΔB ≈ VDN1 · 1Acfs Limit is on the peak: Bmax < Bsat
Swing typeBmax
Bipolar (swings ± around zero)ΔB/2
Unipolar (from zero)ΔB
DC bias (flyback in CCM)Bdc + ΔB/2

VD/N1 is fixed by the conversion ratio, so the design knob is Acfs: raise fs and the core shrinks. That's why converters isolate at the switching frequency, not at the 60 Hz mains.

Analogy · a swing and a push

Push a swing forward harder than you pull it back and it goes higher every cycle until it hits the frame (saturation). Balanced pushes keep the same arc. And a faster, smaller push rhythm (higher fs) needs a smaller arc, so you can use a smaller swing set (core).

Tricky bits
  • Drift is per cycle. Net volt-seconds per cycle ÷ Lm gives the current added each cycle. Multiply by the number of cycles for the total.
  • Which Bmax? The flyback in CCM carries a DC magnetizing current, so its flux never returns to zero. Use Bdc + ΔB/2, the worst of the three cases for the same ΔB.
  • Frequency is in the denominator. Double fs with the same V, D and N1: ΔB halves, or you can halve Ac for the same ΔB.

07Leakage and the equivalent model

Shared flux links both windings through the core. Leakage flux links one winding without linking the other: its return path passes through the surrounding air. Leakage flux still stores energy and still needs current, so it acts like a small inductor in series with each winding.

Click each part of the model
v1 = vm + Ll1di1dt primary terminal = magnetizing voltage + leakage drop

In the drawing, Ll2 has already been moved to the primary side as n²Ll2 (see §8 for why it scales by n²).

Analogy · a leaky hose

The core is a hose carrying water (flux) from the primary to the secondary. Leakage is water that squirts out of a crack near one end and never reaches the other. You still had to pump that water, so it costs you something, but the other side never feels it.

Tricky bits
  • Parallel vs series. Lm is in parallel (it's set by the voltage across the winding, the shared flux). Leakage is in series (it's set by each winding's own current).
  • A perfect transformer has Lm → ∞ (no magnetizing current) and Ll1, Ll2 → 0 (no leakage). Then only the ideal element is left.
  • Leakage voltage is Ll1·di1/dt, so it's worst when current changes fast, as in a switching converter.

08Reflecting elements through the ideal transformer

Let n = N1/N2. The ideal element gives v1 = n v2 and i1 = i2/n, so any impedance on the secondary looks n² times bigger from the primary:

v1i1 = n²v2i2   ⇒   R → n²R,   L → n²L,   C → C/n²
Worked derivation (lecture): why L becomes n²L
On the secondary: i2 = v2 / (jωL)
Ideal element: i1 = i2/n  and  v2 = v1/n
i1 = v2 / (n jωL) = (v1/n) / (n jωL) = v1 / (jω (n²L))
So the primary sees an inductor of n²L. Same steps for R (n²R). For C the impedance is 1/(jωC), so multiplying the impedance by n² means dividing C by n².

Analogy · a gearbox

Through a gearbox, a load's resistance to turning looks bigger or smaller depending on the gear ratio. The ratio shows up twice (once for speed, once for torque), so the load scales by the ratio squared. A transformer does the same with voltage and current.

Tricky bits
  • Which way? Secondary to primary: multiply impedance by n² (n = N1/N2). Primary to secondary: divide by n².
  • C goes the other way. Because its impedance is 1/(jωC), C is divided by n² when R and L are multiplied.
  • For a step-up transformer n < 1, so n² < 1 and secondary elements look smaller from the primary.

09Measuring the model: open- and short-circuit tests

You measure the inductance at the primary terminals twice.

  • Secondary open (i2 = 0): no current enters the ideal primary, so the n²Ll2 branch carries nothing. You see Loc = Ll1 + Lm.
  • Secondary shorted (v2 = 0): the ideal primary is shorted too, so Lm sits in parallel with n²Ll2. You see Lsc.
Loc = Ll1 + Lm Lsc = Ll1 + Lm ∥ n²Ll2 ≈ Ll1 + n²Ll2 needs Lm ≫ n²Ll2 Split the leakage evenly: Ll1 ≈ n²Ll2 ≈ Lsc/2  ⇒  Lm ≈ Loc − Lsc/2

Two measurements, three unknowns, so the even split is an assumption. The ≈ holds for grid transformers, phase-shifted full bridges and flybacks, but not for resonant converters.

Loads with the lecture's example: Loc = 1.00 mH, Lsc = 20 µH. Toggle the test to see which parts of the model the meter sees.
Analogy · two doors in a hallway

Water flows down a hallway (Ll1) to a fork: a huge side room (Lm) and a narrow corridor leading on (n²Ll2). Close the far end (open circuit) and all water goes into the big room. Open the far end wide (short circuit) and water takes the easy corridor, so the big room barely matters.

Tricky bits
  • Short-circuit test measures leakage, not Lm. The small n²Ll2 in parallel with the big Lm is ≈ n²Ll2.
  • Don't forget the half. Lm ≈ Loc − Lsc/2, not Loc − Lsc, because only Ll1 (half of Lsc) is in the open-circuit reading.
  • When it fails: resonant converters deliberately use an Lm not much bigger than the leakage, so Lm ∥ n²Ll2 is no longer ≈ n²Ll2.

10The flyback: store energy, then deliver it

The flyback is a simple and very common isolated dc-dc converter, typically for 1s to 10s of watts: auxiliary supplies for gate drivers or control boards, and chargers for small batteries (phones, laptops).

The model we analyse is an ideal transformer with a deliberately small Lm in parallel with its primary. Lm is the energy store:

Em = ½Lmim² Q1 is on for DTs, off for the rest of Ts
Switch on: storeSwitch off: deliver
vg sits across Lm, so im ramps up and the stored energy grows. The reversed secondary holds D1 off, so nothing leaves the transformer yet. C feeds the load. im can't stop (it's an inductor current). It transfers to the secondary through the turns ratio, D1 conducts, and im ramps down as Lm hands its energy to the output.

Note the dots. The secondary is connected so that turning Q1 on makes the diode reverse biased. That is the whole trick: the input and output never conduct at the same time.

Running example (Vg = 20 V, D = 1/3, Ts = 15 µs, Lm = 1 mH, im from 0.1375 A to 0.2375 A).
imEm = ½Lmim²
Analogy · a bucket brigade with a flywheel

Think of spinning up a flywheel with a hand crank (switch on), then letting go and connecting the flywheel to a generator (switch off). You never crank and generate at the same moment. The flywheel is Lm; its speed is im; the energy it holds is ½Lmim².

Tricky bits
  • Not a "normal" transformer. In a mains transformer both windings carry current at once. In a flyback they take turns, so it's really a pair of coupled inductors that stores energy.
  • Energy per cycle × fs = power. In CCM only the part between im,min and im,max is delivered: ½Lm(imax² − imin²). The demo shows this equals V²/R.
  • That's why the flyback wants a small Lm (gapped core), the opposite of a mains transformer (§5).

11Two operating states

In continuous conduction mode (CCM), im never reaches zero, so there are two states. Write vL (the voltage across Lm) and iC (the capacitor current) in each. Then apply the small ripple approximation (SRA): replace v by its average V and im by its average Im.

StateExactWith SRA
① 0 ≤ t < DTs
Q1 on, D1 off		vL = vg
iC = −v/R		vL = Vg
iC = −V/R
② DTs ≤ t < Ts
Q1 off, D1 on		vL = −(N1/N2) v
iC = (N1/N2) im − v/R		vL = −(N1/N2) V
iC = (N1/N2) Im − V/R

Where do the state ② terms come from? With D1 on, the secondary is clamped to the output v. The ideal transformer reflects that to the primary as (N1/N2)v, with the minus sign from the reversed secondary. The current im leaves Lm through the ideal primary, and the ideal element sends (N1/N2)im out of the secondary into the C and R node.

Analogy · a relay race with one runner

im is a single runner who never stops. In state ① he runs laps on the primary track, getting faster. In state ② he's handed over to the secondary track (scaled by the turns ratio) and slows down as he pushes the load. The capacitor is the water station that keeps the load going while the runner is on the other track.

Tricky bits
  • Sign of vL in state ②. It's negative: the output is reflected back as −(N1/N2)V. That negative voltage is what resets Lm.
  • Turns ratio direction. Both reflected terms use N1/N2: output voltage times N1/N2 to reach the primary, primary current times N1/N2 to reach the secondary.
  • In state ① the ideal primary carries no current. The diode blocks the secondary, so i2 = 0 and all of the input current is im.

12Volt-second balance: V and the conversion ratio M(D)

Steady state first: solve for the averages. The same two tools as the buck and boost, just new state equations. Inductor volt-second balance on Lm gives V.

Worked derivation (lecture)
⟨vL⟩Ts = (1/Ts) ∫0Ts vL dt = 0
= (1/Ts) [ VgDTs − (N1/N2) V D′Ts ] = VgD − (N1/N2) V D′
With D′ = 1 − D:  V = (N2/N1) · D/(1 − D) · Vg
Running example: V = (25/50)(1/3)/(2/3)(20 V) = (0.5)(0.5)(20 V) = 5 V.
M(D) = VVg = N2N1 · D1 − D
  • M = N2/N1 at D = 0.5. Below that it steps down, above it steps up (relative to N2/N1).
  • D/(1 − D) is the buck's D times the boost's 1/(1 − D): the flyback is a buck-boost with a transformer in it.
  • The turns ratio is a free design choice: it sets where D sits for a given V/Vg.
Try it: M(D) and the turns ratio
Loads with the running example: Vg = 20 V, 50 : 25, D = 1/3 gives V = 5 V.

Analogy · a bicycle's two gear sets

The duty cycle is the rear derailleur (fine, continuous adjustment). The turns ratio is the front chainring (picked once, at design time). Choose the chainring so the rear derailleur works in its comfortable middle range instead of at the extremes.

Tricky bits
  • N2/N1, not N1/N2. The output side's turns go on top. A quick check: more secondary turns should mean more output voltage.
  • Solving for D: set D/(1 − D) = (N1/N2)M, call it x, then D = x/(1 + x).
  • Why choose the turns ratio? Without it (a plain buck-boost), a 300 V to 12 V supply would need D ≈ 0.04: tiny pulses, poor control. A turns ratio lets D sit near 0.3 to 0.5.

13Charge balance: the average magnetizing current Im

Capacitor charge balance on C gives the average magnetizing current.

Worked derivation (lecture)
⟨iC⟩Ts = (1/Ts) ∫0Ts iC dt = 0
= (1/Ts) [ −(V/R) DTs + ((N1/N2) Im − V/R) D′Ts ]
= −V/R + (N1/N2) Im D′
⇒ Im = (N2/N1) · 1/(1 − D) · V/R
Running example: Im = (0.5)(1.5)(5 V / 20 Ω) = (0.75)(0.25 A) = 0.1875 A.
Im = N2N1 · 11 − D · VR

Analogy · a part-time delivery driver

The load needs a steady V/R all the time, but the magnetizing current only delivers during the D′ fraction of each period (and gets scaled by the turns ratio on the way). A driver working 2/3 of the day must carry 1.5× the average demand while on shift. That 1/(1 − D) factor is the "part-time" penalty.

Tricky bits
  • Im is a primary-side current. It's bigger or smaller than the load current by the turns ratio and by 1/(1 − D).
  • Changing the turns ratio also changes D. Substituting D from M(D): Im = (N2/N1 + M)·V/R. A bigger N1/N2 lowers Im (but see §15 for the cost).
  • Sanity check with power. Average input current = D·Im (under SRA), and Vg·D·Im should equal V²/R.

Part 4 · Ripple, switch stress and DCM

14Ripple: Δim and Δv

The averages fix V and Im. Now the waveshapes around them. As for the buck, integrate the state equation over one interval. The on interval is the easy one because both slopes are constant there.

Worked derivations (lecture)
Magnetizing current ripple, peak-to-peak, from vL = Vg during 0 ≤ t < DTs:
Lm dim/dt = vL  ⇒  Δim = (1/Lm) ∫0DTs vL dt = Vg D Ts / Lm
Output voltage ripple, peak-to-peak, from iC = −V/R during 0 ≤ t < DTs (v falls by Δv):
C dv/dt = iC  ⇒  Δv = −(1/C) ∫0DTs iC dt = V D Ts / (R C)
Running example: Δim = (20)(1/3)(15 µs)/(1 mH) = 0.100 A (the lecture's triangle). Δv = (5)(1/3)(15 µs)/((20 Ω)(10 µF)) = 0.125 V.
Δim = VgDTsLm     Δv = VDTsRC both peak-to-peak im,max = Im + Δim/2    im,min = Im − Δim/2 CCM needs im,min > 0

Analogy · a water tower

The capacitor is a water tower feeding a town (R) at a steady rate. For DTs the pumps are off and the level drops linearly: Δv = (drain rate) × (time) / (tank size). A bigger tank (C) or a shorter gap (higher fs) means less droop.

Tricky bits
  • Peak-to-peak, not amplitude. Both formulas give the full swing. The peak is the average plus half the ripple.
  • Why the on interval? During state ① both slopes are constant (Vg/Lm and −V/(RC)), so one multiplication does it. In state ② iC changes as im falls, so v curves.
  • Frequency. Both ripples are proportional to Ts: double fs, halve both.
  • SRA check. The formulas only make sense if Δv ≪ V. Here 0.125 V vs 5 V is 2.5%.

15Maximum voltage across the switch

While Q1 is off (DTs ≤ t < Ts), D1 conducts and the output is reflected onto the primary. Q1 has to block the input plus that reflected output:

vQ1 = vg + N1N2 v VQ1,max = Vg + N1N2(V + Δv2) at the end of the off interval, where v peaks
Running example
Δv = 0.125 V (from §14), so the peak output is V + Δv/2 = 5.0625 V
VQ1,max = 20 V + 2 × 5.0625 V = 30.125 V

The trade-off: a large N1/N2 lowers Im but raises the switch stress. The turns ratio trades one for the other.

Try it: pick the turns ratio
Holds Vg = 20 V, V = 5 V, R = 20 Ω, Ts = 15 µs, C = 10 µF (running example) and recomputes D, Im and VQ1,max for each N1/N2.
Analogy · a seesaw

Push down on current (bigger N1/N2) and voltage stress rises on the other end. The designer's job is to find the balance point where both the switch's current rating and its voltage rating are comfortable.

Tricky bits
  • It's more than Vg. Students often size the switch for the input voltage only. The reflected output adds (N1/N2)V on top.
  • Use the peak of v (V + Δv/2), not V. The peak happens at the end of the off interval.
  • A real flyback also sees a spike from the leakage inductance (§7) when Q1 turns off. The lecture's formula leaves that out.

16The flyback in DCM

At light load the stored energy runs out before the period ends: im reaches zero and D1 turns off. Now there are three states:

StateDurationvLiC
① Q1 onDTsVg−V/R
② D1 onD2Ts−(N1/N2)V(N1/N2)im − V/R
③ Q1 off, D1 off, im = 0the rest0−V/R (C alone feeds the load)

D2 is set by the load, not the controller. You find it with volt-second and charge balance, the same two tools. DCM happens either at light load in a flyback designed for CCM, or at every load when DCM is the design choice (a smaller Lm, so D1 is already off when Q1 turns on).

Worked approach: solving the DCM case (beyond the slides, using the lecture's tools)

The lecture gives you the background and leaves the solution to you. Try it first, then check here.

1. Volt-second balance on Lm (state ③ adds nothing):
VgDTs − (N1/N2)V D2Ts = 0  ⇒  D2 = (N2/N1)(Vg/V) D
2. im starts at zero, so its peak is ipk = VgDTs/Lm (the Δim formula).
3. Charge balance: the diode current (N1/N2)im is a triangle of height (N1/N2)ipk lasting D2Ts. Its average must equal V/R:
(N1/N2) · ipk · D2/2 = V/R
4. Substitute D2 and ipk, and the turns ratio cancels:
V = Vg D √(R Ts / (2 Lm))
5. CCM/DCM boundary: CCM holds while Im > Δim/2, that is im,min > 0.
Example: running converter at R = 150 Ω: V = 20(1/3)√(150 × 15 µs / 2 mH) = 6.667 × 1.061 = 7.07 V, D2 = (0.5)(20/7.07)(1/3) = 0.471.
Flyback workbench at light load
Same running converter, but R = 150 Ω. Drag R down past 75 Ω to watch it return to CCM.
Analogy · an empty bucket

In CCM you never pour the whole bucket out before refilling. At light load, the town drinks so little that the bucket empties early and sits empty (state ③) until the next fill. The town (C and R) lives off the water tower in the meantime.

Tricky bits
  • M(D) from §12 no longer applies. In DCM V depends on the load (R), Lm and Ts. The turns ratio drops out.
  • vL = 0 in state ③ because im = 0 and stays there: no change in current means no inductor voltage.
  • Don't use Im · D′ for charge balance in DCM. The diode only conducts for D2Ts, and im is a triangle down to zero, so use the triangle's area.
  • The boundary for the running example is R = 75 Ω, where im just touches zero. That's exactly the lecture's slide 12 triangle (0 to 0.10 A).
