ENEL 585 · Lecture 9 · Study guide

Power semiconductor devices: which switch goes where

Every converter so far used ideal switches. Real ones are diodes, MOSFETs, IGBTs, thyristors and bidirectional switches, and each can only do part of the job. This lecture gives you one picture, the operating-region plot, that shows what each device can do, and a recipe that reads off what each switch in a circuit must do. Match the two and you've picked your device.

Part 1 · Describing a switch

01Three questions for every device

The main families of power semiconductor switch are diodes, MOSFETs, IGBTs, thyristors and bidirectional switches (there are others). Before worrying about ratings, losses, switching speed or cost, the lecture asks three questions of each one:

  1. Control. Can we decide when it turns on, and separately, when it turns off?
  2. Current. Which current direction can it conduct when it's on?
  3. Voltage. Which voltage polarity can it block when it's off?

Questions 2 and 3 are about sign (direction and polarity), not size. A 1200 V MOSFET and a 20 V MOSFET give the same answers. The size is a separate rating you check later.

Analogy · valves in a water system

Think of each switch as a valve. Question 1 asks whether it has a handle, and whether the handle can both open and close it. Question 2 asks which way water can flow through it when open. Question 3 asks which side it can hold back pressure from when shut. A check valve (the diode) has no handle at all; it opens and shuts because of the water pressure around it.

Tricky bits
  • "Controllable" has two halves. The thyristor can be turned on from its gate but not off. Always answer turn-on and turn-off separately.
  • Blocking is about the polarity you apply, not the one the device "likes". A diode conducts positive current but blocks negative voltage. Mixing these up is the most common error in this lecture.

02Reading an operating-region plot

Draw the switch with fixed references: isw flows into the + terminal of vsw (both at the top). Then plot vsw across and isw up. An ideal switch only ever sits on the axes:

  • Off: isw = 0, so it sits on the vsw axis and blocks whatever vsw the circuit applies.
  • On: vsw ≈ 0, so it sits on the isw axis and conducts whatever isw the circuit pushes.

A shaded quadrant means the switch can block voltage of that quadrant's polarity and conduct current in that quadrant's direction. In other words, it can use both half-axes that border the quadrant.

I: (+v, +i)  ·  II: (−v, +i)  ·  III: (−v, −i)  ·  IV: (+v, −i) shaded quadrants = {polarities it blocks} × {directions it conducts} counted anticlockwise from top right
Try it: test a device on the v-i plane
Pick a device, then test each half-axis. Green means the device can do it; red means it can't.
Analogy · a job description on a map

The plane is a map of every job a switch could be asked to do. The four half-axes are the four possible jobs: conduct up, conduct down, hold back +, hold back −. A device's shaded quadrants are the jobs on its CV. You hire it only if its CV covers every job the circuit will give it.

Tricky bits
  • The references never move. isw is defined as flowing into the + terminal. If real current flows out of that terminal, isw is negative. Don't redraw the arrow to follow the current.
  • The switch never sits inside a quadrant. A point inside would mean current and voltage at the same time, which is a lot of power burned in the switch. The shading is shorthand for "both edges of this quadrant are allowed".
  • Quadrant IV is +v, −i (bottom right), not −v. Sketch the axes every time rather than trusting memory.

Part 2 · The device family

03Diodes

A diode is uncontrollable: there's no gate. Whether it's on or off depends entirely on the circuit around it. With the anode at the top (the + terminal):

  • If the circuit pushes current forward (anode to cathode), it turns on and conducts isw > 0.
  • If the circuit tries to make the cathode higher than the anode, it turns off and blocks vsw < 0.
Diode, anode at +: conducts isw > 0, blocks vsw < 0  ⇒  quadrant II
The diode's plot
Analogy · a check valve

A plumbing check valve has a flap that swings open when water pushes forward and slams shut when water tries to flow back. Nobody operates it. It "decides" from the pressure around it, exactly like a diode.

Tricky bits
  • Quadrant II, not I. Positive current, but negative blocking voltage. A diode can't block positive vsw: positive voltage across it just turns it on.
  • You can't turn a diode off. It turns off by itself when the circuit tries to reverse its current. If you need a switch to stop conducting while current still flows forward, a diode can't do it.

04MOSFETs

A MOSFET is controllable: the gate turns it on and off. With the drain at the top (+ terminal):

  • When on, its channel conducts current in either direction.
  • When off, it blocks positive vsw only. It has a built-in body diode (drawn in grey, cathode at the drain), and that diode switches on as soon as vsw goes negative.
MOSFET, drain at +: conducts ± isw, blocks vsw > 0  ⇒  quadrants I and IV

Analogy · a tap with a back-flow bypass

A MOSFET is a tap with a handle, plus a small check valve plumbed around it pointing backwards. Open the tap and water flows either way. Close it and it holds back forward pressure, but any backward pressure just flows round through the bypass valve. So it can only ever hold back one side.

Tricky bits
  • Gated off does not mean blocking. If the circuit drives vsw negative, the body diode conducts no matter what the gate says.
  • Two ways to carry negative current. When on, negative current flows through the channel. When off, it can still flow through the body diode. Either way the MOSFET cannot stop negative current.

05IGBTs, with and without an anti-parallel diode

An IGBT is controllable from its gate. With the collector at the top (+ terminal), it conducts positive current only and blocks positive voltage only: a single quadrant.

Adding a diode in anti-parallel (across it, cathode to the collector) adds a path for negative current. The combination conducts in both directions: positive through the IGBT, negative through the diode. It still blocks positive voltage only, because the diode is reverse-biased when vsw > 0.

IGBT, collector at +: conducts isw > 0, blocks vsw > 0  ⇒  quadrant I IGBT + anti-parallel diode: conducts ± isw, blocks vsw > 0  ⇒  quadrants I and IV same as a MOSFET
Compare: IGBT alone, then with its diode

6500 V IGBT modules rated 250 A to 1000 A (source: dynexsemi.com, as cited in the lecture).

Analogy · a one-way tap, then a bypass

A bare IGBT is a tap that only passes water forwards and only holds back forward pressure. Plumb a check valve around it, pointing backwards, and you've rebuilt the MOSFET's arrangement from two separate parts.

Tricky bits
  • Diode direction. The anti-parallel diode's cathode goes to the collector. The other way round, it would conduct whenever vsw > 0 and the IGBT could never block anything.
  • The negative current isn't controlled. It flows through the diode, which has no gate. You can stop positive current (turn the IGBT off) but not negative current.
  • Why can't a plain IGBT block negative vsw? Not because it conducts: an IGBT has no body diode. Its reverse blocking rating is just tiny (often a few tens of volts, or not specified), so a real negative voltage would break it down. That's why it's treated as blocking positive voltage only, and why most IGBTs come packaged with the anti-parallel diode, which then carries any reverse current.
  • Same plot as a MOSFET, different device. On the v-i plane "MOSFET" and "IGBT + diode" look identical; the lecture groups them for that reason.

06Thyristors

A thyristor is half-controllable:

  • Turn-on is controllable: send a gate pulse while vsw > 0 and it turns on. A pulse is enough; it then stays on by itself (it latches).
  • Turn-off is not controllable: the external circuit has to bring its current to zero. Only then does it turn off.

With the anode at the top, it conducts positive current and blocks both polarities: before the gate pulse it holds off positive voltage, and it always holds off negative voltage.

Thyristor, anode at +: conducts isw > 0, blocks ± vsw  ⇒  quadrants I and II

Analogy · a toilet flush

Press the lever (the gate pulse) and the flush starts. Letting go of the lever does nothing: it keeps flushing until the tank is empty (the circuit takes the current to zero). You can start it whenever you like, but you can't stop it early.

Tricky bits
  • The gate pulse only works with vsw > 0. Pulse it while it's blocking negative voltage and nothing happens.
  • Removing the gate signal doesn't turn it off. Once on, it stays on until its current reaches zero. That makes it a poor fit for DC-DC converters, where the switch current never falls to zero on its own.
  • Quadrant II means reverse blocking, which a diode also does. The thyristor adds quadrant I: it can also hold off positive voltage until you fire it.

07Bidirectional switches

A bidirectional switch is controllable in both directions, with one gate per direction. It blocks both polarities and conducts both directions: all four quadrants.

  • Both gates on: conducts either way.
  • Both gates off: blocks either polarity.
  • One gate on, the other off: it behaves as a diode, conducting one way and blocking the other.

It replaces what used to take two back-to-back switches with a single device.

Bidirectional switch: conducts ± isw, blocks ± vsw  ⇒  quadrants I, II, III and IV
The bidirectional switch's plot

In the real world

IGLT65R055B2: a 650 V gallium nitride (GaN) bidirectional switch in a TOLT package (source: infineon.com, as cited in the lecture).

Analogy · a door with a lock on each side

Picture a door with one bolt for each direction of traffic. Draw both bolts and nothing passes. Release both and traffic passes both ways. Release one and it's a one-way door, which is the diode behaviour.

Tricky bits
  • "Back-to-back" means in series, facing opposite ways. Two MOSFETs in series, one flipped, give four quadrants: each one blocks one polarity. Two MOSFETs in parallel facing opposite ways block nothing, because one of the body diodes always conducts.
  • Why bother? Any switch across an AC voltage sees both polarities. A plain MOSFET there would conduct through its body diode every negative half cycle.

Rotating a device by 180° (connecting it the other way round) while keeping the same references flips the sign of both vsw and isw as the device sees them. Every shaded quadrant jumps to the opposite one:

(v, i) → (−v, −i)  ⇒  I ↔ III,   II ↔ IV

So the plot tells you two things: which device, and which way round to connect it.

Analogy · turning a check valve around

Take a check valve out of the pipe and put it back in facing the other way. The valve hasn't changed, but now it passes the flow it used to stop and stops the flow it used to pass. Both of its jobs flip at once, which is why both signs flip and the quadrant moves diagonally.

Tricky bits
  • Rotation is diagonal, not a mirror. Quadrant I goes to III, not to II or IV. Both the current direction and the blocking polarity flip.
  • The bidirectional switch doesn't change because it already covers all four quadrants.
  • When a question says "which way round", answer with a terminal name: "cathode at the switch node", "drain toward Vg".

09Choosing a switch type

Now flip the question around: given a circuit, which device does each switch need? The recipe from the lecture:

  1. Label vsw and isw on each switch the same way as on the device plots: + and the isw arrow both at the top terminal.
  2. Switch on: find the sign of isw it must conduct.
  3. Switch off: find the sign of vsw it must block.
  4. Together these fix the quadrant(s) it must operate in.
  5. Pick a device whose shaded quadrants cover them all, as drawn or rotated by 180°. The plot tells you which terminal goes at the top.
Device finder
Loaded with the lecture's Case 1, switch S2: on, it carries i2 = −iL < 0; off, it blocks +Vg. The finder should return a diode with its cathode at the + terminal.
Analogy · a shopping list against a menu

Steps 1 to 4 write the shopping list: the quadrants the circuit demands. Each device's plot is its menu. Buy the plainest device whose menu has everything on your list. A bidirectional switch has everything, but you don't order the whole menu when you only want one dish.

Tricky bits
  • Required quadrants multiply out. If a switch must conduct both directions and block positive voltage, it needs I and IV.
  • Quadrants aren't the whole story. Question 1 (control) still matters. A thyristor covers quadrant I, but it can only be used where the circuit brings its current to zero. A diode works only where the circuit itself turns it on and off at the right moments.
  • Try both orientations. A diode "doesn't fit" quadrant IV as drawn, but fits perfectly when rotated.

Part 4 · Worked example: two switches between Vg and Vo

10The setup and the two switch states

Two ideal switches S1 and S2 and an inductor L sit between a source Vg and a battery Vo. Because Vo is a battery, it can absorb or supply power, so the power

P = Vo IL can flow either way, depending on the sign of IL small ripple: iL ≈ IL, so iL keeps the sign of IL

The lecture looks at three cases: IL > 0 (P from Vg to Vo), IL < 0 (P from Vo to Vg), and IL of either sign, set by the operating point (P both ways).

The references: i1 flows left to right into S1, with + of v1 on the Vg side. i2 flows down into S2, with + of v2 at the switch node. Exactly one switch is on at a time.

StateThe switch that's on carriesThe switch that's off blocks
S1 on, S2 offi1 = iLv2 = +Vg (switch node at Vg)
S2 on, S1 offi2 = −iLv1 = +Vg (switch node at 0)

Key result: in every case, both switches block +Vg. Only the current signs change, and they follow iL.

What each switch must do, and what fits

Analogy · two doors into one corridor

The inductor current is a crowd walking along a corridor that won't stop. It has to get in through one of two doors, S1 (from the Vg side) or S2 (from ground). Exactly one door is open at a time. The closed door always has the same pressure difference across it (Vg). Which way the crowd walks through the open door depends on which way the corridor is flowing.

Tricky bits
  • i2 = −iL, not +iL. i2 is defined flowing down into S2. With iL > 0 the current actually flows up through S2 into the inductor, so i2 is negative.
  • Both switches block +Vg, never Vo. When S1 is on, the switch node sits at Vg, so v2 = Vg. When S2 is on, the node sits at 0, so v1 = Vg − 0.
  • "Small ripple" is doing real work. It's what lets us say iL never changes sign. Drag ΔiL above 2|IL| in the demo and watch both switches suddenly need an extra quadrant (more in §13).

11Case 1: power from Vg to Vo (IL > 0)

Worked example: Case 1, step by step
  1. S1, on: i1 = iL > 0. Off: v1 = +Vg.
  2. S2, on: i2 = −iL < 0. Off: v2 = +Vg.
  3. S1 needs (+v, +i): quadrant I. That's a MOSFET or an IGBT as drawn, drain (collector) toward Vg.
  4. S2 needs (+v, −i): quadrant IV. That's a diode rotated 180°, cathode at the switch node.
  5. Result: this is the buck converter of Lecture 2.

Analogy · a self-opening relief path

When the MOSFET S1 shuts, the inductor's current has to keep going, and the only way in is up through S2. That push opens the diode by itself, like a relief valve that opens when pressure builds. When S1 opens again, Vg appears across the diode backwards and it slams shut. Nobody needs to drive S2.

Tricky bits
  • Why a diode is allowed for S2: the circuit turns it on and off at exactly the right moments, forced by S1. That's the "uncontrollable" answer to question 1 working in your favour.
  • Diode direction: cathode at the switch node. Put it the other way and, when S1 turns on, the diode sees +Vg forwards and shorts the source through S1.
  • An IGBT alone is fine for S1 here, because i1 is never negative. It only needs a diode in Case 3.

12Case 2: power from Vo to Vg (IL < 0)

Worked example: Case 2, step by step
  1. S1, on: i1 = iL < 0. Off: v1 = +Vg.
  2. S2, on: i2 = −iL > 0. Off: v2 = +Vg.
  3. S1 needs (+v, −i): quadrant IV. That's a diode turned over, cathode toward Vg.
  4. S2 needs (+v, +i): quadrant I. That's a MOSFET or IGBT, drain at the switch node.
  5. Result: read right to left, this is a boost from Vo to Vg.

Analogy · the same pipe, flowing backwards

Reverse the flow in a pipe and every check valve has to be turned around, and the pump moves to the other end. Here the controlled switch moves from S1 to S2, and the diode moves from S2 to S1, facing the other way.

Tricky bits
  • Why it's a boost: seen from Vo, the inductor is at the input, the controlled switch S2 goes to ground, and a diode feeds the output Vg. That's the boost layout from Lecture 3, and Vg > Vo as a boost needs.
  • The voltages didn't change. Both switches still block +Vg. Only the current signs flipped, which is exactly why the quadrants moved from I to IV and back.
  • "Turned over" means rotated 180°. The diode is quadrant II as drawn and IV rotated, with cathode at the + of v1, which is the Vg side.

13Case 3: power either way (IL of either sign)

Worked example: Case 3, step by step
  1. S1, on: i1 = iL, either sign. Off: v1 = +Vg.
  2. S2, on: i2 = −iL, either sign. Off: v2 = +Vg.
  3. Both need (+v, +i) and (+v, −i): quadrants I and IV.
  4. That's a MOSFET, or an IGBT with an anti-parallel diode, for each switch: S1 drain toward Vg, S2 drain at the switch node.
  5. Result: one circuit that works as a buck from Vg to Vo and as a boost back.

Analogy · an electric car's drive

Pressing the accelerator sends power from the battery to the motor; braking sends it back to charge the battery. It's the same hardware both times; only the current direction changes. Case 3 is that idea on a small scale: two switches that can each carry current both ways.

Tricky bits
  • The Case 1 hardware can't run backwards. With IL < 0, S2 would need i2 > 0, but its diode (cathode at the node) only conducts i2 < 0.
  • Big ripple has the same effect as Case 3. If ΔiL > 2|IL|, then iL = IL ± ΔiL/2 crosses zero every period. With ideal switches, both then need quadrants I and IV even though IL > 0. With a diode as S2, the diode stops iL at zero instead: that's DCM from Lecture 3. Try IL = 1 A, ΔiL = 4 A in the demo.
  • Two IGBTs alone won't do. Each covers only quadrant I. Each needs its anti-parallel diode to reach quadrant IV.
