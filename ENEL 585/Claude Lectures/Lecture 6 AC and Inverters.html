AC fundamentals and the single-phase inverter

The whole lecture tells one story: take a DC source, chop it with four switches, smooth it with an inductor, and push clean 60 Hz power into the grid. Part 1 gives you the language to describe AC. Part 2 is the machine that makes it.

Part 1 · AC fundamentals

01Phasors: a sine wave as one arrow

In steady state, every voltage and current in an AC circuit wiggles at the same frequency (60 Hz for the grid). So two sine waves in the same circuit can only differ in two ways: how big they are (the peak) and how early or late they are (the phase). A phasor packs exactly those two numbers into one complex number.

x(t) = X̂ cos(ωt + θ)  ⟷  X = X̂∠θ = X̂ ejθ and back again: x(t) = Re{ X ejωt }
Analogy · the clock hand and its shadow

Picture a clock hand spinning 60 times a second, with a light shining straight down on it. Its shadow on the floor slides back and forth: that shadow is your cosine wave. The hand's length is the peak X̂, and where it points at t = 0 is the phase θ.

Every hand in the circuit spins at the same speed, like horses on one merry-go-round. Since they all turn together, you only need a single photo taken at t = 0 to know everything. That photo is the phasor. This is why we can drop the ejωt part: it is the spinning, and everyone shares it.

Try it: the arrow and its shadow
phasor and x(t)θ = 0 reference
Positive θ shifts the wave to the left (it peaks earlier, at t = −θ/ω). That is what "leading" means.
Tricky bits
  • This course uses peak phasors, not RMS. X̂ is the peak. RMS is X̂/√2. This choice is why a ½ shows up in every power formula later. Mixing the two conventions is the most common way to get a factor of 2 wrong.
  • Leading = positive angle = shifted left. It feels backwards, but a wave that is "ahead" reaches its peak at an earlier time, which is further left on a time axis.
  • Convert sines first. Phasors here are built on cosine. Use sin(ωt) = cos(ωt − 90°).

02Impedance, and solving one frequency at a time

Phasors turn calculus into algebra. With them, R, L and C all act like resistors with complex values, called impedances, and Ohm's law becomes V = Z I. KVL, KCL, series and parallel rules all work exactly as in DC circuits. You just do the arithmetic with complex numbers.

PartAt fundamental ωAt harmonic kωWhat happens
ResistorRRunchanged
InductorjωLjkωLgrows ×k
Capacitor1/(jωC)1/(jkωC)shrinks ÷k

60 Hz: ω₁ = 2π(60) ≈ 377 rad/s. The kth harmonic is at k × 60 Hz.

Analogy · heavy door, small bucket

An inductor is a heavy door on a hinge. Swing it back and forth slowly and it follows you. Try to shake it fast and it fights you hard. So at 3 times the frequency, it pushes back 3 times harder: reactance ×k.

A capacitor is a small bucket. A slow flow fills it up and then it blocks. A fast back-and-forth slosh never fills it, so it passes easily: reactance ÷k.

A resistor is a narrow pipe. It doesn't care how fast you push.

Adding phasors: arrows, not plain numbers

A resistor branch carries 4 A and a parallel inductor branch carries −j3 A. The source current is 4 − j3 = 5 A peak, not 7 A. Walk 4 km east, then 3 km north: you end up 5 km from where you started. The two currents peak at different moments, so they never add up to 7 at the same instant.

Distorted waves: split into notes, solve each, add

Analogy · a chord

A distorted waveform is like a chord on a piano: a main note at 60 Hz (the fundamental) plus quieter notes at 120, 180, 300 Hz and so on (the harmonics). Fourier says any repeating wave can be built this way.

  • Step 1. Split the source into one sinusoid per harmonic.
  • Step 2. Solve the circuit separately for each harmonic, using that harmonic's impedances (L gets k times bigger, C gets k times smaller).
  • Step 3. Convert each answer back to a time function and add them (superposition works because the circuit is linear).
Worked example from the slides: the 3rd harmonic

Series R = 3 Ω and an inductor with ωL = 4 Ω. The source also contains Vs,3 = 3∠0° V at 3ω.

At 3ω the inductor becomes j12 Ω (tripled). So

I3 = 33 + j12 = 0.059 − j0.235 = 0.243∠−76.0° A

Then add i3(t) = 0.243 cos(3ωt − 76°) to the fundamental current in the time domain.

Tricky bits
  • Never add phasors of different frequencies. A 60 Hz arrow and a 180 Hz arrow spin at different speeds, so a single snapshot means nothing. Add the time functions instead.
  • Remember this for Part 2: an inductor is a weak filter for low harmonics (only 3× at the 3rd) and a strong one for high harmonics (39× at the 39th). That single fact explains why PWM wins.

03Real and reactive power

When voltage and current are both sinusoids, the average power depends on how well they line up in time. Complex power captures both parts at once. With peak phasors:

S = ½ V I* = P + jQ P = ½ V̂ Î cosθ real power, W Q = ½ V̂ Î sinθ reactive power, var

Here θ is how far the voltage leads the current. With RMS values the ½ disappears: P = VrmsIrms cosθ.

  • Q > 0, lagging: current lags voltage. Inductive. Absorbs reactive power.
  • Q < 0, leading: current leads voltage. Capacitive. Supplies reactive power.
Analogy · the horse and the barge

A horse walks along the bank pulling a barge with a rope held at an angle. Only the part of the pull that points along the canal moves the barge forward. That useful part is P. The sideways part of the pull, Q, moves nothing forward, but the rope still has to carry it and the horse still feels it.

Physically, Q is energy that sloshes into an inductor's magnetic field or a capacitor's electric field and comes back out every cycle. It is borrowed and returned, so its average is zero, but it still makes current flow in the wires.

The key example: an inverter feeding the grid through an inductor

This circuit is the model for everything in Part 2. The inverter makes Vt, the grid is Vg, and an inductor sits between them. KVL gives the current:

Ig = Vt − VgjωL = V̂t sinθωL + j V̂g − V̂t cosθωL
Worked example: 340∠10° V into a 340∠0° V grid, ωL = 2 Ω

Ig = 340 sin10°/2 + j(340 − 340 cos10°)/2 = 29.5 + j2.6 = 29.6∠5.0° A (peak)

Sg = ½ (340)(29.5 − j2.6) → P ≈ 5.02 kW, Q ≈ −0.439 kvar

Sg is the power absorbed by the grid. So the grid takes in 5 kW of real power and gives out 439 var of reactive power.

Multiply out the current and you get two rules of thumb that are worth memorizing:

P = V̂t V̂g sinθ2ωL the angle θ sets real power Qg = V̂g(V̂t cosθ − V̂g)2ωL the magnitude V̂t mostly sets reactive power
Analogy · pushing a swing

To add energy to a child on a swing, you push slightly ahead of the swing's motion. The inverter voltage leading the grid by a small angle θ is that push: it sends real power into the grid. Making the inverter voltage bigger than the grid (same timing) doesn't add energy. It just shoves reactive power around.

Tricky bits
  • Don't forget the ½. With peak phasors, S = ½VI*. Leaving it out doubles your answer.
  • Conjugate the current, not the voltage.
  • Watch the sign convention. Qg = −0.439 kvar "absorbed" means the grid is supplying 439 var. Where does it go? Into the inductor, which needs ½ωLÎ² = ½(2)(29.6²) ≈ 0.88 kvar. Because the inverter and grid voltages have equal magnitude, they each supply about half.

04Total harmonic distortion (THD)

Converters switch, so the current they push or pull is not a perfect sine wave, even when the grid voltage is. THD is one number that says how much junk rides along with the useful 60 Hz part.

Irms = √( I1² + I2² + I3² + … ) THD = √( I2² + I3² + … )I1 × 100%

Harmonic RMS values are Ik = Îk/√2. The top of the THD fraction is everything except the fundamental; the bottom is the fundamental only.

Analogy · off-key notes

THD is the combined loudness of all the off-key notes compared to the melody note. Why square-and-root? Different frequencies are independent, like the east and north legs of a walk, so they combine like the sides of a right triangle.

Worked example: fundamental 10 A, 5th 0.6 A, 7th 0.8 A

THD = √(0.6² + 0.8²)/10 = 1.0/10 = 10%. (The 0.6 and 0.8 form a 3-4-5 triangle, so the harmonics total 1.0 A.)

Measuring it

  • Capture a whole number of cycles at steady state, e.g. 6 cycles of 60 Hz = 0.100 s (one cycle is 16.67 ms).
  • Run an FFT and read RMS amps at 60, 120, 180 Hz and so on.
  • Plug into the formula. For three-phase gear, repeat for each phase.

The lesson from real inverter data

The same inverter gave about 0.66% current THD on a perfect sinusoidal grid, but about 20% when the grid voltage had just 3% of 11th harmonic in it. A low THD number measured on an ideal grid tells you very little about how the inverter behaves on a real, messy one.

Tricky bits
  • The denominator is I1, not Irms. THD can exceed 100%.
  • Peak or RMS doesn't matter for THD as long as you're consistent, because the √2 cancels.
  • A whole number of cycles matters: otherwise the FFT bins don't line up with the harmonics and energy smears into neighbouring bins (spectral leakage).

05Power factor: two ways to waste the wire

Power factor says what fraction of the current-carrying effort actually delivers power:

kp = PVrms Irms = cosθ × I1Irms displacement factor × distortion factor, where I1Irms = 1√(1 + THD²)

Here θ is the angle between the voltage and the fundamental current, and THD is written as a fraction (10% = 0.10).

  • Displacement: the 60 Hz current is out of step with the voltage. Costs cos θ.
  • Distortion: harmonic currents add to Irms (they heat wires and use up ratings) but, with a sinusoidal grid voltage, deliver zero average power.
Analogy · the delivery truck

A truck's capacity is Vrms×Irms; the cargo that actually gets delivered is P. Displacement is boxes loaded sideways, wasting space. Distortion is packing peanuts: they fill the truck, nobody paid for them, and you still pay for the fuel.

Tricky bits
  • Why do harmonics deliver no average power? Multiply a 60 Hz voltage by a 180 Hz current. They keep drifting in and out of step, so the product is positive half the time and negative half the time, and it averages to zero.
  • Even perfectly in-phase current can have kp < 1. A square-wave-like current (THD 48%) has kp ≈ 0.90 even with θ = 0.
  • Small THD barely matters: 10% THD gives 1/√1.01 = 0.995.

Part 2 · The single-phase inverter

06The H-bridge

Four switches in two legs. Leg A has S1 on top and S3 below; leg B has S2 on top and S4 below. Each leg connects its midpoint to either the + or the − rail of the DC bus. The output is the difference between the two midpoints, vt = vA − vB, which drives current through the inductor into the grid:

L digdt = vt − vg
Switches onA is tied toB is tied tovt
S1, S4+ rail− rail+Vdc
S2, S3− rail+ rail−Vdc
S1, S2+ rail+ rail0
S3, S4− rail− rail0

Compactly, vt = (S1 + S4 − 1)Vdc, where each S is 1 (on) or 0 (off) and each leg's bottom switch is the opposite of its top.

Analogy · a reversing switch for a battery

Think of connecting a battery to a lamp with jumper cables. You can hook it up forwards (+Vdc), backwards (−Vdc), or clip both lamp wires to the same battery terminal (0). The H-bridge does exactly this, electronically, thousands of times a second.

Tricky bits
  • Shoot-through: never turn on S1 with S3, or S2 with S4. That connects + to − directly and shorts the DC bus. Huge current, dead switches.
  • The bridge cannot make a sine wave. It can only output +Vdc, 0 or −Vdc. The trick of the rest of the lecture, called modulation, is choosing when to switch so that the 60 Hz part (the fundamental) of vt has the amplitude and phase you want. The inductor then smooths the current.
  • From §3: the amplitude and phase of vt's fundamental are what set P and Q into the grid.

07Square-wave modulation

The simplest plan: S1, S4 on for the first half cycle, S2, S3 on for the second half. The output is a ±Vdc square wave at 60 Hz. Each switch turns on and off only once per cycle, so switching losses are tiny.

Its Fourier series contains only odd harmonics:

V̂t,k = 4Vdckπ,   k = 1, 3, 5, 7, … peak; even harmonics are zero Fundamental ≈ 1.27 Vdc · 3rd = ⅓ of it · 5th = ⅕ · voltage THD = 48.3%
Analogy · drawing a circle with a square

A square wave is a very rough sketch of a sine wave. The sketch contains the right main tone, plus a loud 3rd harmonic at a third of the volume, a 5th at a fifth, and so on. They are all close to 60 Hz, which is the problem.

Why it's not good enough

  • Amplitude is fixed at 4Vdc/π. The only way to change it is to change Vdc itself. (Phase is adjustable by shifting the switching times.)
  • The harmonics are low-order, and the inductor barely filters them. With the §3 example (fundamental 340 V, ωL = 2 Ω, Ig = 29.6 A):
HarmonicV̂t,hInductor hωLCurrent Îh% of 29.6 A
3rd113.3 V6 Ω18.9 A64%
5th68.0 V10 Ω6.8 A23%
7th48.6 V14 Ω3.5 A12%
Tricky bit · the fundamental is taller than the square

The fundamental's peak (1.27 Vdc) is bigger than the square wave's own height (Vdc). That seems impossible, but the square wave sits at full height for the whole half cycle, while a sine only touches its peak for an instant. To carry the same "area", the best-fit sine has to overshoot. This is why nothing else in this lecture can beat 4Vdc/π.

08Phase-shift modulation

Now run each leg as its own 50% square wave, but slide leg A later and leg B earlier by αT1/2 each. Whenever both midpoints sit on the same rail, vt = 0. The output becomes three-level: +Vdc, 0, −Vdc, with zero gaps of length αT1.

V̂t,k = 4Vdckπ |cos(kπα)|  ⇒  fundamental V̂t,1 = 4Vdcπ cos(πα) α = 0: square wave (max output) · α = 0.5: zero output
Analogy · two curtains

Each leg is a curtain that opens and closes once per cycle. The output is the light that gets through when one is open and the other is closed. Sliding the two curtains' timing closer together narrows the window, so less light (a smaller fundamental) gets through. α is how far you've closed the window.

Try it: slide α and watch the harmonics
vt/Vdcfundamentalharmonic peaks

Bonus: you can delete one harmonic

Pick α so that cos(kπα) = 0, which means kπα = 90°, so α = 1/(2k). α = 1/6 kills the 3rd; α = 0.1 kills the 5th. Try both in the demo.

Tricky bits
  • α sets amplitude only. To change the phase, shift all four gate signals together in time.
  • You can null one harmonic, never all of them. The rest are still low-order and still hard to filter.
  • Still only 2 switchings per device per cycle, same as the square wave.

09Bipolar sinusoidal PWM (SPWM)

The big idea: switch fast (kHz) instead of once per cycle, and use the duty ratio to control the average. Over one short switching period Ts, if the output is +Vdc for a fraction d and −Vdc for the rest:

⟨vt⟩ = (2d − 1) Vdc d = 0.5 gives 0, d = 1 gives +Vdc

Now make d rise and fall slowly as a sine, and the local average traces a sine: maVdc sin(ω1t). In practice this is done by comparing a sine reference with a fast triangle carrier: reference above carrier → S1, S4 on (+Vdc); otherwise S2, S3 on (−Vdc). It is "bipolar" because the output always jumps between +Vdc and −Vdc, never resting at 0.

KnobDefinitionWhat it controls
mareference peak ÷ carrier peakfundamental size: V̂t,1 = maVdc (linear for ma ≤ 1)
mffsw ÷ f1where the harmonics sit: in clusters around mf, 2mf, 3mf, …
Analogy · newspaper photos and a dog whistle

Look closely at a printed newspaper photo: it's only black dots on white. From arm's length your eye averages them into smooth greys. SPWM is the same: only ±Vdc up close, a smooth sine on average. The inductor plays the role of "arm's length".

And the harmonics don't disappear, they get moved. SPWM shifts the noise up near the switching frequency, like moving a noise to a pitch only dogs can hear. The inductor, which is deaf to high frequencies, easily blocks it.

Reading the harmonic table

Textbook tables give each harmonic as V̂t,h = chVdc. For ma = 0.8: c1 = 0.800, cmf = 0.818, cmf±2 = 0.220.

Worked example: Vdc = 425 V, ma = 0.8, mf = 39, ωL = 2 Ω
  • Fundamental: 0.8 × 425 = 340 V. ✓ matches the grid.
  • Carrier harmonic h = 39 (2340 Hz): V̂ = 0.818 × 425 = 347.7 V. Current = 347.7 / (39 × 2) = 4.46 A (15%).
  • Sidebands h = 37, 41: V̂ = 0.220 × 425 = 93.5 V. Currents ≈ 1.26 A and 1.14 A (about 4% each).
  • Compare the square wave's 3rd: 18.9 A (64%).
Tricky bits
  • SPWM's voltage THD is huge (≈146% at ma = 0.8), much worse than the square wave's 48%. Yet its current is far cleaner. Why? Current = Vh/(hωL). The 39th harmonic's voltage is big, but it has to push through 39 times the reactance. Voltage THD alone is a misleading way to compare modulation methods when an inductor sits in between.
  • Voltage ceiling: in the linear range the fundamental can't exceed Vdc (ma = 1), which is less than the square wave's 1.27Vdc. So you need Vdc > V̂g. Overmodulation (ma > 1) gets you more, up to 4Vdc/π, but brings the low-order harmonics back.
  • The price: about 2mf switchings per device per cycle, so more switching loss.

The whole lecture in one picture

Square wave, phase shift and SPWM are three ways of sketching a sine wave with only three crayons (+, 0, −). The square wave uses two big strokes. Phase shift adds a gap. SPWM uses thousands of tiny strokes, so the mess ends up at a scale the inductor simply smooths away.
