ENEL 585 · Lecture 2 · Study guide

The buck converter: stepping DC down without burning power

A resistor divider lowers a voltage by turning the extra into heat. A buck converter flips a switch between the source and ground very fast, then lets an LC filter keep only the average. This lecture builds that idea and then the two tools you'll use on every converter in the course: inductor volt-second balance and capacitor charge balance.

Part 1 · Switch, don't burn

01Why not a resistor divider?

The simplest way to get a lower voltage across a load RL is to put a resistor R in series with it. Both resistors carry the same current, so the source voltage splits between them:

v = vg RLR + RL,    η = PloadPin = RLR + RL = vvg

The lecture gives two reasons this is a bad power supply:

  1. Efficiency is low. With R = RL, half the power heats R: η = 50%.
  2. The output depends on the load. If RL changes, v changes. Nothing holds it at the value you designed for.
Worked example (lecture): vg = 100 V
RL = R:   v = 100 × R/(R + R) = 100 × ½ = 50 V RL = 3R:   v = 100 × 3R/(R + 3R) = 100 × ¾ = 75 V same circuit, different load, 25 V higher η (RL = R) = I²RL / I²(R + RL) = R/2R = 50%
Analogy · brakes vs gears

A divider is like holding a car's speed down a hill by riding the brakes. It works, but every bit of "extra" is turned into heat, and the speed you get depends on how heavy the car is (the load). A switching converter is like a gearbox: it trades voltage for current instead of throwing energy away, and the ratio stays put no matter the load.

Calculator: divider vs buck when the load changes
Loads with the lecture's numbers: vg = 100 V, RL = R. Press "RL = 3R" to reproduce 75 V.
divider output vbuck output V = DVg
Tricky bits
  • For a divider, η = v/vg exactly. Same current in both resistors, so power splits like the voltages. Stepping 48 V down to 12 V can never beat 25% efficiency. (Making R adjustable, like a linear regulator, fixes the voltage but not this efficiency limit.)
  • Don't mix up the two problems. Low efficiency is about wasted power; load dependence is about regulation. The lecture's two numeric examples test one each.
  • The buck line in the demo is flat because V = DVg has no R in it. You'll prove that in §8 (it holds in continuous conduction, §10).

02The buck converter and its two switch positions

Replace the resistor with a switch followed by an L, C filter. The switch is single-pole double-throw: its common terminal (the node feeding the inductor) connects either to the top of the source (position 1) or to ground (position 2). Call the voltage at that node vs. The circuit only ever has two configurations:

ConfigurationSwitchSwitch-node voltageWhere iL comes from
①position 1vs = vgthe source
②position 2vs = 0freewheels through the ground path
Try it: click the switch to flip it
Analogy · a tap that is only fully open or fully shut

A half-open tap wastes pressure inside the valve. A tap that's either wide open (no pressure drop across it) or tightly shut (no flow through it) wastes nothing in the valve itself. An ideal switch is the same: closed means v = 0 across it, open means i = 0 through it, so p = vi = 0 either way. All the "in between" is done by timing, not by burning.

Tricky bits
  • Position 2 is not optional. The inductor's current can't stop instantly. If you simply opened the path, vL = L di/dt would spike to a huge voltage. Position 2 gives iL somewhere to go while the source is disconnected.
  • vs is the voltage at the switch node (the inductor's input), measured to ground. It only takes two values, vg and 0. Everything between comes from averaging.
  • In ② the source is out of the circuit, but current still flows through L and the load. Look at the highlighted path in the demo.

03Duty ratio and the average switch voltage

The switch repeats the same pattern every switching period Ts (switching frequency fs = 1/Ts). It sits in position 1 for a fraction D of each period, the duty ratio, D ∈ [0, 1]:

  • Interval ①, from 0 to DTs: vs = vg
  • Interval ②, from DTs to Ts (length (1 − D)Ts): vs = 0

The average over one switching period is written ⟨vs⟩Ts. It's just the area under the waveform divided by Ts.

Worked derivation (lecture): the average switch voltage
⟨vs⟩Ts = 1Ts ∫0Ts vs(t) dt definition of the average = 1Ts [ ∫0DTs Vg dt + ∫DTsTs 0 dt ] split into interval ① and ② = DTsTs Vg area of the rectangle is Vg × DTs = D Vg Ts cancels

Plotted against D, ⟨vs⟩Ts is a straight line from 0 (D = 0) to Vg (D = 1).

Try it: drag D and watch the area
vs(t)⟨vs⟩Ts = DVg
Analogy · a flickering lamp

Switch a lamp on and off faster than your eye can follow. If it's on 30% of the time, it looks 30% as bright. Your eye is doing the averaging. In the buck converter, the LC filter plays the role of the eye (§4).

Tricky bits
  • D is the fraction of time in position 1 (connected to the source), not position 2. Interval ② lasts (1 − D)Ts.
  • The average depends on D only, not on Ts. Switching twice as fast at the same D gives the same ⟨vs⟩. (Frequency matters for ripple, §11.)
  • Notation. Lowercase vg is the (possibly varying) source voltage; uppercase Vg is its DC value. ⟨·⟩Ts means "averaged over one switching period".

04The LC filter and the output voltage

From the filter's point of view, the switch is just a square-wave source vs driving L, C and R. So the lecture redraws it that way. The L and C form a second-order low-pass filter: slow content passes, fast content is blocked.

At DC (zero frequency) the inductor is a short circuit and the capacitor is an open circuit. So the DC part of vs reaches the output untouched:

⟨v⟩Ts = ⟨vs⟩Ts = D Vg ≡ V

The switching-frequency content (the square wave's harmonics at fs, 2fs, …) is heavily attenuated, so v(t) is V plus a small ripple.

Try it: what the filter lets through
Example values (not from the lecture): Vg = 100 V, R = 10 Ω, L = 100 µH, C = 10 µF, fs = 100 kHz.
vs(t) and its spectrumv(t) and its spectrum
Analogy · a car on a washboard road

Drive over a road with rapid bumps. Good suspension (the LC filter) lets the car body follow the road's average height and ignore the fast bumps. Change the average height of the road (D) and the car follows. Make the bumps faster (higher fs) and the body barely notices them.

Tricky bits
  • The filter doesn't change the average. L and C set how big the ripple is, not the DC value. V = DVg whatever L, C and R are (in CCM).
  • "Second-order" is why it works so well. Above the filter's corner frequency f0 = 1/(2π√(LC)), a second-order filter's gain falls roughly as (f0/f)². Put fs ten times above f0 and the switching harmonics shrink by about 100×. (f0 is from circuits courses, not the slides.)
  • This argument only works because this converter has one switch node feeding one filter. For messier converters you need §8's tool.

Part 2 · Three ingredients for volt-second balance

The filter argument gave V = DVg quickly, but it leans on this converter being simple. The lecture shows a harder circuit (two inductors L1, L2 and two capacitors C1, C2) where "average of the switch node" doesn't directly give you V. 05Ingredient 1: the small ripple approximation

Split the output voltage into its average and the leftover wiggle:

v(t) = V + vripple(t) If ‖vripple(t)‖ ≪ V, then v(t) ≈ V small ripple approximation

The ripple in the lecture's sketches is greatly exaggerated. In a well-designed converter it can be under 1% of V. So whenever v(t) shows up in an equation for the inductor voltage, we replace it with the constant V. That one move is what turns vL into a flat line in each interval (§6).

Try it: how small is "small"?
Same example converter as §4 (D = 0.5, L = 100 µH, fs = 100 kHz, R = 10 Ω). Change C.
Analogy · the depth of a lake

Ask how deep a lake is and you say "50 m", not "50 m plus or minus the little waves on top". The waves are real, but for the question you're asking they're noise. The small ripple approximation says the same about v(t) when you work out vL.

Tricky bits
  • Apply it to the capacitor (output) voltage, not to the inductor current. The inductor current ripple ΔiL is often a sizable fraction of IL, and it's exactly what we calculate in §11.
  • You can't use it to find the output ripple itself. If you set v(t) = V, the ripple is zero by assumption. Finding Δv needs a different approach (a later lecture).
  • "≪" has no hard cutoff. The approximation is good when the ripple is a percent or so; it gets rough if the ripple is, say, 10%.

06Ingredient 2: the inductor over one switching period

The goal is the inductor current iL(t). The inductor law, rearranged, says the voltage sets the slope of the current:

vL(t) = L diL(t)dt  ⇔  diL(t)dt = 1L vL(t)

So plot vL first, then iL. To find vL, the lecture redraws the SPDT switch as two switches: switch 1 in series from the source, switch 2 from the switch node to ground. Exactly one is on at a time.

IntervalSwitchesExact vLSmall rippleiL slope
① 0 to DTs1 on, 2 offvg − v(t)Vg − V(Vg − V)/L, rising
② DTs to Ts2 on, 1 off−v(t)−V−V/L, falling

Both rows come from KVL around the loop: vL = vs − v, with vs = vg in ① and 0 in ②. Because vL is (approximately) constant in each interval, iL ramps in straight lines: up during ①, down during ②, a triangle ripple around its average IL.

Try it: vL sets the slope of iL
Starts at the example converter: Vg = 100 V, D = 0.5 (so V = 50 V), L = 100 µH, fs = 100 kHz, R = 10 Ω.
vL(t)iL(t)IL
Analogy · pushing a heavy cart

The inductor is a heavy cart and vL is how hard you push. A constant push makes the speed (current) rise at a constant rate: a straight ramp. Pull back with a constant force and the speed falls at a constant rate. The cart keeps rolling forward the whole time; it's just slowing down.

Tricky bits
  • Negative vL does not mean negative current. In ② the current is still positive, it's only decreasing. (If it decreased all the way to zero, you'd leave CCM; see §10.)
  • vL in ① is Vg − V, not Vg. The output voltage pushes back on the inductor from the other side.
  • Straight ramps depend on the small ripple approximation. In reality v(t) wiggles slightly, so the ramps curve slightly (you can see this on the lecture's plots).
  • The plot quietly assumes iL ends the period where it started. Is that guaranteed? That's §7.

07Ingredient 3: start-up, transient and steady state

No, iL ending where it began isn't automatic. When the converter starts from rest, each period ends higher than it began. The lecture names the two regimes:

Transient:   iL(ta) ≠ iL(ta + Ts) the state changes period to period Steady state:   iL(tb) = iL(tb + Ts) every period looks like the last
Worked derivation (lecture): why ∫vL dt = 0 in steady state
L diLdt = vL(t) start from the inductor law ∫tbtb+Ts diL = 1L ∫tbtb+Ts vL(t) dt integrate over one period iL(tb + Ts) − iL(tb) = 1L ∫ vL dt left side is the net change in current 0 = 1L ∫ vL dt  ⇒  ∫tbtb+Ts vL(t) dt = 0 steady state: net change is zero
Try it: start the converter from rest
A full circuit simulation of the example converter (Vg = 100 V, L = 100 µH, C = 10 µF, R = 10 Ω, fs = 100 kHz), starting with iL = 0 and v = 0. The LC filter rings before it settles, and with ideal switches the current can briefly swing negative. The lecture's sketch shows a smoother rise; the idea is the same. Pick a period to inspect it.
iL(t)v(t), right axisnet change per period
Analogy · filling a bath with the drain open

Turn on the tap with the drain open. At first the level rises every minute. Eventually the level stops rising: water in per minute equals water out per minute. The surface still sloshes within each minute, but at the start of every minute it's back at the same height. That's steady state, and "no net change per cycle" is exactly ∫vL dt = 0.

Tricky bits
  • Steady state doesn't mean constant. iL still ramps up and down inside each period. It only has to repeat.
  • During start-up, ∫vL dt over a period is not zero. It's positive while the current is climbing (and can go negative if it overshoots). Balance laws only apply once things have settled.
  • Nothing here is specific to the buck. Any inductor in any converter in periodic steady state has zero net volt-seconds per period. That's why the tool generalizes.

Part 3 · Balance laws and what they give

08Inductor volt-second balance

Putting the three ingredients together: small ripple makes vL constant in each interval, the inductor law turns that into straight ramps, and steady state says the ramps must return to their start. The result is the principle:

⟨vL⟩Ts = 1Ts ∫0Ts vL(t) dt = 0 in steady state

In words: the volt-seconds applied to the inductor over one period cancel. The area of vL above the axis equals the area below it.

Worked derivation (lecture): solving for V
0 = 1Ts [ DTs (Vg − V) + (1 − D)Ts (−V) ] area of ① plus area of ② 0 = D(Vg − V) − (1 − D)V Ts cancels 0 = DVg − DV − V + DV expand 0 = DVg − V  ⇒  V = D Vg the DV terms cancel

Same answer as the filter argument in §4, reached without ever talking about filters.

Try it: set a wrong V and watch the current ratchet
Vg = 100 V, L = 100 µH, Ts = 10 µs (example values). Drag the trial V away from DVg.
positive volt-seconds (①)negative volt-seconds (②)iL over five periods
Analogy · a bank account that must break even

Every period, interval ① deposits (Vg − V)·DTs volt-seconds into the inductor and interval ② withdraws V·(1 − D)Ts. If deposits beat withdrawals, the balance (the current) grows every month. Steady state is the one V where they exactly cancel.

Tricky bits
  • The area of ① is (Vg − V)·DTs, not Vg·DTs. Use the inductor voltage, not the source voltage.
  • Signs. The ② term is (1 − D)Ts·(−V). Dropping the minus gives nonsense like V = −DVg/(1 − 2D).
  • L and fs vanish. The balance equation never contains L, and Ts cancels. That's why V = DVg holds for any inductor and any switching frequency (in CCM).
  • Since D ≤ 1, V ≤ Vg: a buck can only step down.

09Capacitor charge balance

The capacitor gets the same treatment, with voltage and current swapped. Since C dv/dt = iC(t), and in steady state v(tb) = v(tb + Ts), the capacitor current integrates to zero over a period:

⟨iC⟩Ts = 1Ts ∫0Ts iC(t) dt = 0 no net charge per period

Volt-second balance gave the average output voltage V. Charge balance gives the average inductor current IL. KCL at the output node gives the same expression in both intervals (the switches are to the left of the inductor, so they don't touch this node):

iC(t) = iL(t) − v(t)R  ≈  IL − VR
Worked derivation (lecture): solving for IL
⟨iC⟩Ts = 1Ts ∫0Ts iC(t) dt = 0 = 1Ts [ DTs (IL − VR) + (1 − D)Ts (IL − VR) ] same expression in ① and ② = IL − VR = 0 D + (1 − D) = 1 ⇒ IL = VR = DVgR
Try it: set a wrong IL and watch the output drift
Vg = 100 V, D = 0.5 (V = 50 V), L = 100 µH, C = 10 µF, fs = 100 kHz (example values).
charge incharge outv over five periods
Analogy · a water tower

The capacitor is a water tower between a pump (the inductor) and a town (the load). The level can bob up and down during the day, but if it's the same every morning, then on average the pump delivered exactly what the town used. For the buck, that means the average inductor current equals the load current V/R.

Tricky bits
  • The real iC isn't flat. The lecture writes iC ≈ IL − V/R, replacing the triangular iL(t) with its average. That's fine here because we only need the average of iC, and the triangle's ripple part averages to zero. The demo shows the true triangle.
  • Match the tool to the unknown. Volt-second balance (inductor) → a voltage. Charge balance (capacitor) → a current.
  • IL = V/R is a buck result. It works because the inductor feeds the output node directly. In other converters the inductor sits elsewhere and IL is not simply the load current.

10The conversion ratio, M(D)

The conversion ratio of a converter is its average output voltage over its input voltage. For the buck, volt-second balance hands it to you directly:

M = VVg,    buck: M(D) = VVg = D in continuous conduction mode (CCM)

The asterisk in the lecture matters: this holds in continuous conduction mode, where iL never falls to zero. Discontinuous mode comes in a later lecture.

Calculator: what duty ratio do I need?
Loads with the lecture's source, Vg = 100 V, and the divider's 50 V target. Try 75 V too.
Analogy · a gear ratio you set with a clock

M is the converter's voltage "gear ratio", and D is the lever that sets it. Unlike the divider, the ratio doesn't slip when the load changes. Like a real gearbox, an ideal buck trades voltage for current: power in equals power out, so the input current is D times the output current. (That power argument isn't on the slides, but it follows from the ideal, lossless parts.)

Tricky bits
  • M depends only on D (in CCM): not on L, C, R or fs.
  • M ≤ 1. Asking a buck for more than Vg is impossible; that needs a boost (next lecture).
  • Divider vs buck at the same ratio. Both can give 50 V from 100 V. The divider does it at 50% efficiency and drifts with load; the ideal buck does it at 100% and doesn't.

11Inductor current ripple and peak current

You know the average inductor current, IL = V/R. Switches and inductors must be rated for the peak, which sits ΔiL/2 above the average, where ΔiL is the peak-to-peak ripple.

Why exactly ΔiL/2?

Each ramp of iL(t) is a straight line between Imin and Imax, so each ramp's average is their midpoint, however long the ramp lasts.

Worked derivation (lecture): peak-to-average ripple
IL = 1Ts [ DTs + (1 − D)Ts ] Imin + Imax2 = Imin + Imax2 both ramps average to the midpoint ΔiL = Imax − Imin definition of peak-to-peak ripple ⇒ Imax = IL + ΔiL2,   Imin = IL − ΔiL2 peak-to-average ripple is ΔiL/2, for any D
Worked derivation (lecture): solving for ΔiL and the peak
vL = L diLdt; in ① (0 to DTs), vL = Vg − V is constant ΔiL = 1L ∫0DTs vL dt = (Vg − V) DTsL the rise during ① is the whole peak-to-peak swing iL,peak = IL + ΔiL2

Handy equivalent forms (substitutions, not on the slides): using V = DVg, ΔiL = VgD(1 − D)Ts/L. Using interval ② instead, ΔiL = V(1 − D)Ts/L. All three agree in steady state.

Analogy · tides around mean sea level

If the tide rises and falls in straight lines between the same high and low marks, mean sea level sits exactly halfway between them, no matter whether it spends longer rising or falling. High tide is half the tidal range above the mean. Same for Imax.

Tricky bits
  • Peak = IL + ΔiL/2, not IL + ΔiL. ΔiL is peak-to-peak.
  • "Independent of D" means the ΔiL/2 relationship, not ΔiL itself. ΔiL does depend on D: for fixed Vg it's largest at D = 0.5 (see the curve in the calculator).
  • R changes IL but not ΔiL. The ripple is set by Vg, D, L and fs. A lighter load (bigger R) lowers IL while the ripple stays, so Imin heads toward zero: that's the edge of CCM.
  • Units. Ts = 1/fs. With µs and µH, volts × µs ÷ µH gives amps directly.
