ENEL 585 · Lecture 8 · Study guide

Power balance and the DC link: where the 120 Hz ripple goes

A single-phase inverter delivers power to the AC side in pulses, twice per line cycle. The DC side can't supply those pulses on its own, so a DC-link capacitor soaks them up. This lecture finds that pulsation, sizes the capacitor for it, and shows why a three-phase inverter doesn't have the problem at all.

Part 1 · Single-phase power balance

01The setup and its two assumptions

Start with the full-bridge inverter from Lecture 7. A DC source vd feeds the bridge with current id. The bridge makes the AC voltage vt and pushes AC current it out of node A.

The lecture makes two simplifying assumptions so the main effect is easy to see:

  1. Ideal AC-side filter. The output current is a pure sinusoid. No switching harmonics, only the fundamental.
  2. Unity power factor. vt and it are exactly in phase (same θ), so reactive power Q = 0. The converter only moves real power.
vt = V̂t,1 cos(ωt + θ),   it = Ît,1 cos(ωt + θ) same θ: unity pf P = ½ V̂t,1 Ît,1 average power, from peak values
Practice numbers used in this guide (from your AC Fundamentals notes)
  • V̂t,1 = V̂g = 340 V, Ît,1 ≈ 29.5 A, 60 Hz, vd = 425 V (so ma = 340/425 = 0.800).
  • P = ½ × 340 × 29.5 = 5015 W ≈ 5.02 kW, which matches the Pac ≈ 5.02 kW in those notes.
Analogy · the frictionless physics problem

In first-year physics you ignore friction so you can see the core idea (a ball rolling forever). These two assumptions do the same job. Real inverters have some harmonics and some reactive power, but those would only blur the one effect this lecture is about: AC power arrives in pulses.

Tricky bits
  • The ½ in P. With peak values, P = ½V̂Î. Forgetting the ½ doubles every answer that follows (ΔE, C, Id). With RMS values it's P = VrmsIrms, no ½.
  • The assumptions don't cause the ripple, and removing them won't remove it. The pulsation comes from multiplying two sinusoids. It's there with a perfect filter.
  • θ is common to v and i. It only slides everything in time. It doesn't change P or the size of the ripple.

02Instantaneous power: a steady P plus a 2ω pulsation

Multiply voltage by current at every instant and you get the power actually flowing at that moment. For two in-phase sinusoids, that product is always positive but it is not constant. It swings between 0 and 2P, at twice the line frequency.

p(t) = P + P cos(2(ωt + θ)) average + twice line-frequency range 0 to 2P · pulsation frequency 2f (120 Hz at 60 Hz) · pulsation amplitude P Id = P/Vd average DC-side current
Worked derivation (lecture slide 5)
p(t) = vt(t) · it(t)
     = V̂t,1 cos(ωt+θ) · Ît,1 cos(ωt+θ)
     = V̂t,1Ît,1 cos²(ωt+θ) same angle, so it's a square
     = ½V̂t,1Ît,1 (1 + cos(2(ωt+θ))) cos²x = ½(1 + cos 2x)
     = P + P cos(2(ωt+θ)) P = ½V̂t,1Ît,1

Practice numbers: P = 5.02 kW, so p(t) swings from 0 up to 2P = V̂Î = 340 × 29.5 = 10.03 kW and back, 120 times a second.

Try it: vt × it = pt
vtitptaverage P

The lecture's last point on this slide: the bridge only connects it to the DC side while a diagonal pair (S1S4 or S2S3) conducts. So the current the bridge pulls from the DC side, id,inv, is a pulse train. Averaged over each switching period it follows pt/Vd, which wiggles at 2ω around Id = P/Vd. Section 3 has a demo of it.

Analogy · pedaling a bike

Your pedal power isn't steady. It peaks when each foot pushes down and drops to almost nothing when the cranks are vertical. Two pushes per revolution, so the power pulses at twice the crank rate, even though your average power is steady. One line cycle is one revolution: the positive half and the negative half each deliver a push.

Tricky bits
  • 2f, not f. The pulsation is at 120 Hz for a 60 Hz system (100 Hz for 50 Hz). Power is v·i, and both flip sign together, so p repeats every half cycle.
  • The pulsation's amplitude is P itself, not ½P and not 2P. p goes from P − P = 0 to P + P = 2P.
  • At unity pf, p is never negative. v and i always have the same sign. Power flows one way; it just flows unevenly.
  • Two different DC currents. Id = P/Vd is the average. The bridge's local average pt/Vd swings from 0 to 2P/Vd.

Part 2 · Sizing the DC-link capacitor

03Why a DC-link capacitor?

Section 2 pretended vd was a stiff source: a perfect voltage that can supply any current, including a 120 Hz pulsation. Real DC sources aren't like that. The lecture shows four examples:

DC sideWhy it isn't stiff
PV panelIts voltage depends on the current you draw; pulsing the current pulls the panel off its operating point.
Battery pack (cell, Rb) through series LsRb and Ls drop voltage whenever the current changes, so a pulsing current means a bouncing vd.
Machine through a three-leg active rectifierThe rectifier regulates an average; it can't track a 120 Hz demand on its own.
Resistive load (power flowing AC → DC)Here id is negative: the converter works as a rectifier. A resistor wants a steady voltage, not pulses.

The fix is a DC-link capacitor C across the DC terminals. It is the intermediate energy store: it absorbs and releases the difference between DC-side and AC-side power. Two consequences:

  1. The ripple on vd stays small. This matters because the bridge passes any vd ripple straight on to the AC voltage (vt is built from ±vd).
  2. The source current id stays nearly DC. By KCL at the top node, the capacitor supplies the pulsating part of what the bridge draws.
id + ic = id,inv ic leaves the capacitor, id,inv enters the bridge If id = Id (constant), then ic = id,inv − Id: everything except the average.

Analogy · a water tower

A town's pumps push water in at a steady rate. Households draw it in bursts (morning showers, evening dishes). The water tower sits between them: it fills when demand is low and drains when demand is high, so the pumps never see the bursts. The capacitor is the tower, and vd is the water level, which rises and falls a little but stays near its setpoint.

Tricky bits
  • Direction of ic. In the lecture's model ic leaves the capacitor toward the bridge. ic > 0 means the capacitor is discharging.
  • The capacitor has two jobs: keep vd steady (for the AC voltage) and keep id steady (for the source). Same component, two benefits.
  • id negative isn't an error. With a resistive load on the DC side, power flows from AC to DC, and the sign convention makes id negative.

04Capacitor power: pc = pt − pd

Now use the equivalent circuit: a DC current source id, the capacitor C, and the bridge. Power balance at the DC link says the capacitor makes up the difference between what the bridge takes and what the source gives:

pc(t) = pt(t) − pd(t) pc = power delivered by the capacitor With pd = P:   pc = P cos(2(ωt + θ)) the whole 2ω term

The capacitor discharges while pt > P (it helps the source) and charges while pt < P (the source's spare power fills it). Over a full cycle it gives back exactly what it took, so its average power is zero.

Analogy · a savings account

You earn a steady salary P (the DC source). Your spending pt swings between 0 and 2P. When you spend more than you earn, the difference comes out of savings (the capacitor discharges). When you spend less, the surplus goes back in. Your balance (the stored energy, and so vd) wobbles, but on average it stays put.

Tricky bits
  • Sign convention. pc as defined here is power delivered by the capacitor. pc > 0 means discharging, and vd is falling.
  • vd peaks don't line up with pt peaks. vd is highest at the end of charging (when pt rises back through P) and lowest at the end of discharging (when pt falls back through P). Scrub the demo to see it.
  • Average pc = 0. A capacitor stores energy; it doesn't produce or consume it.

05The energy swing ΔE = P/ω

How much energy does the capacitor have to give up in one discharge stretch? It's the area under one positive lobe of the 2ω pulsation. The lecture writes the lobe as P sin(2ωt), which is positive from t = 0 to t = π/(2ω):

ΔE = ∫0π/2ω P sin(2ωt) dt = P2ω[1 − cos π] = Pω Lobe length π/(2ω) = T/4, a quarter of the line period (4.17 ms at 60 Hz)
Worked integral (lecture slide 9), step by step
ΔE = ∫₀^(π/2ω) P sin(2ωt) dt
    = P [ −cos(2ωt) / (2ω) ]₀^(π/2ω) antiderivative of sin(2ωt) is −cos(2ωt)/(2ω)
    = (P/2ω) [ −cos(π) + cos(0) ] 2ω · π/(2ω) = π
    = (P/2ω) [ 1 − cos π ] = (P/2ω)(1 + 1)
    = P/ω

Practice numbers: ω = 2π(60) = 377.0 rad/s, so ΔE = 5020/377.0 = 13.32 J.

Try it: the shaded area is ΔE
Analogy · the longest overspend

Back to the savings account. The size of the dip in your balance isn't set by how much you spend at the worst instant. It's set by the total overspend during one stretch of spending more than you earn: the area of one lobe.

Tricky bits
  • Use ω, not f and not 2ω. ΔE = P/ω with ω = 2πf in rad/s. The 2 from 2ω cancels against the "1 − cos π = 2". Using f gives an answer 2π times too big; using 2ω gives half.
  • Why only one lobe? Over a full ripple cycle the net energy is zero. The voltage swing depends on how much energy moves one way between the minimum and the maximum, which is one lobe.
  • Lower line frequency = more energy. At 50 Hz each lobe lasts longer, so ΔE is 60/50 = 1.2× bigger for the same P.

06Sizing the capacitor for a ripple spec

You're told the peak-to-peak ripple must be at most Δvd. The capacitor's stored energy is E = ½Cvd². Moving ΔE in or out swings the voltage from vd,min = vd − Δvd/2 to vd,max = vd + Δvd/2:

ΔE = ½C[(vd + Δvd/2)² − (vd − Δvd/2)²] = ½C[2vdΔvd] = C vd Δvd C = ΔEvd Δvd  ⇒  C ≥ P/ωvd Δvd the boxed result
Worked derivation (lecture slide 9), step by step
vd,max² − vd,min² = (vd + Δvd/2)² − (vd − Δvd/2)²
   = [vd² + vdΔvd + Δvd²/4] − [vd² − vdΔvd + Δvd²/4]
   = 2vdΔvd squares cancel exactly, no approximation
ΔE = ½C · 2vdΔvd = C vd Δvd
C = ΔE/(vdΔvd), and with ΔE = P/ω (§5): C ≥ (P/ω)/(vdΔvd)

Practice example (numbers from your AC Fundamentals notes, 5% ripple spec added)

P = 5020 W, vd = 425 V, f = 60 Hz, Δvd = 0.05 × 425 = 21.25 V
ω = 2π(60) = 377.0 rad/s
ΔE = P/ω = 5020/377.0 = 13.32 J
C ≥ 13.32/(425 × 21.25) = 13.32/9031 = 1.474 mF
check: E = ½(1.474 mF)(425)² = 133.2 J, and ΔE/E = 13.32/133.2 = 10% = 2Δvd/vd ✓

Analogy · a wide bathtub

Pour the same bucket of water (ΔE) into a narrow tub and the level jumps; pour it into a wide tub and the level barely moves. C is the width of the tub. And since energy goes as vd², a tub that's already "full" to a high vd takes the same bucket with an even smaller level change.

Tricky bits
  • Δvd is peak-to-peak (vd,max − vd,min). If a question gives "±10 V", that's Δvd = 20 V. Using the half-swing doubles C.
  • It's a minimum. "C ≥" means any bigger capacitor also meets the spec. Round up when picking a part.
  • Scaling. C ∝ P, ∝ 1/f, ∝ 1/(vdΔvd). Double vd at the same percentage ripple and Δvd doubles too, so C drops by 4×.
  • Handy check: ΔE/E = 2Δvd/vd. A 5% ripple means the capacitor swings 10% of its stored energy.
  • Units. Answers land in the mF range for kW inverters. If you get µF or farads, check ω and the ½.

Part 3 · Three-phase power balance

07Three phases: the 2ω terms cancel

Now a three-leg inverter feeds phases A, B and C, with a split DC-link capacitor (two 2C in series, midpoint N). Same assumptions: ideal filter, unity power factor. The three voltages and currents are 120° apart:

vtAN = V̂t,1 cos(ωt + θ),   itA = Ît,1 cos(ωt + θ) vtBN = V̂t,1 cos(ωt − 2π/3 + θ),   itB = Ît,1 cos(ωt − 2π/3 + θ) vtCN = V̂t,1 cos(ωt + 2π/3 + θ),   itC = Ît,1 cos(ωt + 2π/3 + θ)

Each phase on its own still has p = P + P cos(2(·)). But added together, the three pulsations wipe each other out:

Worked sum (lecture slide 11), step by step
p(t) = pA + pB + pC
    = 3P + P[cos(2(ωt+θ)) + cos(2(ωt−2π/3+θ)) + cos(2(ωt+2π/3+θ))]
let x = 2(ωt+θ). The other two angles are x − 4π/3 and x + 4π/3.
−4π/3 and +2π/3 point the same way (they differ by 2π); so do +4π/3 and −2π/3.
    = 3P + P[cos x + cos(x + 2π/3) + cos(x − 2π/3)]
    = 3P + P · 0 cos x + cos(x − 2π/3) + cos(x + 2π/3) = 0
    = 3P, constant

Quick check at x = 0: cos 0 + cos(−120°) + cos(120°) = 1 − 0.5 − 0.5 = 0. When phase A is at its peak 2P, phases B and C are each at P/2, and 2P + P/2 + P/2 = 3P.

Analogy · three riders on one chain

One cyclist's pedal power pulses. Put three riders on one drive chain, each with their cranks rotated a third of a turn from the next. Whenever one rider is at a dead spot, the other two are mid-push. The pulses fill each other's gaps and the chain sees steady power.

Tricky bits
  • Doubling the angle keeps the balance. The phases are at 0, −120°, +120°. At 2ω they sit at 0, −240°, +240°, which is the same set as 0, +120°, −120°. Still three equal arrows 120° apart, still summing to zero. Try the phase-spacing slider: the total is flat only at 120° (the lecture's case) and at 60°, where the doubled angles land on the same balanced set.
  • P is per phase. Total power is 3P. For the same total 5.02 kW, each phase carries 1.67 kW.
  • It needs balance. The cancellation relies on three equal amplitudes 120° apart. Unbalance one phase (slider, beyond the lecture) and some 2ω ripple returns.

08What that buys you: pd = pt

Since the total AC power is constant, the instantaneous power into the inverter equals the instantaneous power out: pd = pt. The lecture draws two conclusions:

  1. No 2ω energy storage. The DC capacitor doesn't have to supply any 2ω power, so the energy requirement from §5 and §6 disappears. The DC capacitance can be much smaller.
  2. A capacitor is still required for switching-ripple mitigation. id,inv is still a pulse train at the switching frequency, and something has to supply those fast pulses locally.

Demo numbers: the same 5.02 kW total from 425 V, split over three phases (1.67 kW each), m = 0.8, so each leg's phase voltage to the midpoint N is 0.8 × 425/2 = 170 V peak and Î = 19.7 A peak. The 2ω ripple is gone; the capacitor only carries the switching-frequency pulses.

Analogy · smooth road, small shocks

Single-phase is a road with a big dip every few metres: you need big suspension (a big C). Three-phase smooths out the dips completely, but the road still has small gravel (switching ripple). You still need shock absorbers, just much smaller ones.

Tricky bits
  • "No 2ω energy requirement" is not "no capacitor". Exam answers that say the three-phase inverter needs no DC capacitor are wrong.
  • Split capacitor. Two 2C capacitors in series give 2C·2C/(2C+2C) = C total. The midpoint N is the reference for the phase voltages vtAN, vtBN, vtCN.
  • Id is still total P over vd. 5.02 kW / 425 V = 11.8 A, the same average as single-phase at the same power. What changes is the 120 Hz part, which is now zero.

09The other extreme: pure reactive power (pf = 0)

At the other extreme, the three-phase inverter only delivers or absorbs reactive power (currents 90° from voltages). Each phase sloshes energy back and forth, but the three sloshes cancel, so:

pt(t) = 0 "as expected": no real power, and no 2ω either Id = 0 the DC source current has no DC component

The DC side still isn't quiet, though. The lecture's last slide traces it:

  1. The inverter's switching puts harmonic current into id,inv: pulses that average to zero.
  2. The DC-link capacitor carries those harmonics (ic ≈ id,inv), so vd doesn't see them.
  3. Hence there's very little harmonic content in the source current id.

See it in the §8 demo: . The pulses stay, their average drops to zero, and id goes flat at 0.

Analogy · passing buckets in a circle

Reactive power is energy carried out to the grid and brought right back, every half cycle. With three phases, one phase's returning bucket arrives exactly as another phase needs one to go out, so buckets pass hand to hand around the triangle and nothing ever needs to come from the DC well.

Tricky bits
  • Id = 0 doesn't mean id,inv = 0. The bridge still draws switching pulses; they just average to zero, and the capacitor supplies them.
  • beyond lectureSingle-phase is different. A single-phase inverter at pf = 0 has zero average power, but its p(t) still pulses at 2ω with amplitude ½V̂Î. It still needs the big 2ω capacitor. The "pt = 0" result is a three-phase result.
  • In between. For any pf, the balanced three-phase total is constant at 3 × ½V̂Î cos φ. Drag φ in the §8 demo: Id scales with cos φ, and the ripple stays gone.

The whole lecture in one picture

AC power in one phase arrives in pulses, like a single cyclist pedaling. A single-phase inverter needs a flywheel (the DC-link capacitor) big enough to ride out one pulse: that's ΔE = P/ω, and C follows from how much speed wobble (Δvd) you'll accept. Three phases are three riders offset by a third of a turn: the pulses fill each other in, and the flywheel only has to smooth out the tiny switching bumps.
