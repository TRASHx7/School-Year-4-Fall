Single-phase inverters: from DC to a controlled AC voltage

An inverter has no sine-wave generator inside. It only has four switches that connect the load to the top or bottom of a DC source. This whole lecture is about which switches to flip, and when, so the output's 60 Hz part has the size you want and the leftover junk (harmonics) is easy to filter.

Part 1 · Build the voltage

01The H-bridge: two legs, one output

The bridge has two legs. Leg A has S1 on top and S3 below. Leg B has S2 on top and S4 below. The bottom rail is called N and is the 0 V reference.

Each leg connects its middle point (A or B) to either the top rail (vd) or the bottom rail (0). The load only sees the difference:

vt = vAN − vBN,   where vAN, vBN ∈ {0, vd}
Analogy · two people, a stage and a floor

Each leg is a person who can stand either on the stage (vd) or on the floor (0). The load only cares about the height difference between the two people. A on stage and B on the floor: +vd. The reverse: −vd. Both on the stage or both on the floor: no difference, so 0.

Tricky bits
  • Four states, three levels. Two different states both give 0 (the "zero states"). That's not a mistake; it's why the bridge makes exactly +vd, 0 and −vd.
  • One switch per leg. In every commanded state, exactly one switch in each leg is on. The top and bottom switch of a leg are always opposites.
  • The bridge cannot make a sine wave directly. The smooth sine only appears as an average, after clever switching (modulation) and the inductor's smoothing.

02Safe switching: dead time and overlap time

Shoot-through happens when both switches in one leg are on (S1 with S3, or S2 with S4). That's a wire straight from + to −: it shorts the DC source, and the huge current destroys the switches.

The catch is that real switches take a little time to turn off. If you command "S1 off, S3 on" at the same instant, both conduct for a moment. So we add dead time:

  1. Turn S1 off.
  2. Wait (both off).
  3. Turn S3 on.
Analogy · an airlock vs a relay baton

Voltage source = airlock. You never open the inner and outer doors together, or all the air rushes out. Close one, wait, then open the other.

Current source = relay baton. A current source (a DC source behind a big inductor) insists its current keeps flowing. If every path is broken, the voltage spikes violently trying to push that current through. So both runners hold the baton for a moment: turn S3 on first, wait, then turn S1 off. That's overlap time.

Tricky bit · where does the load current go during dead time?

In a voltage-source bridge, the load (often an inductor) still carries current during the brief dead time. It flows through diodes that sit across each switch. You don't need the details for this lecture; just know that dead time is safe because of them.

Part 2 · Slow switching: one or two flips per cycle

03Square wave

Modulation is the recipe that decides when each switch turns on. The simplest recipe: S1 + S4 on for the first half of each cycle (+vd), then S2 + S3 on for the second half (−vd). Each gate is on for 0.5T1. Each switch flips only twice per 60 Hz cycle, so switching losses are tiny.

Every repeating wave is secretly a sum of sine waves. For the square wave:

V̂t,k = 4vdkπ,   k = 1, 3, 5, … peak; even harmonics are zero Fundamental = 4vd/π ≈ 1.27 vd · 3rd = ⅓ of it · 5th = ⅕ · voltage THD = 48.3%
Analogy · a flute and a buzzy synth

A flute and a buzzy synthesizer can play the same note. They share the same fundamental (the pitch), but the synth adds loud overtones (harmonics) that make it sound harsh. The square wave is the buzzy synth: right pitch, lots of junk close to it.

Tricky bits
  • Why is the fundamental (1.27vd) taller than the square wave (vd)? The square wave sits at full height for the whole half cycle, while a sine only touches its peak for an instant. To carry the same "area", the best-fit sine has to poke above vd.
  • Phase is the only control. Sliding the wave in time moves the fundamental with it, but its size stays locked at 4vd/π. To change the size, you'd have to change vd itself.
  • Why only odd harmonics? The negative half is an exact mirror of the positive half (half-wave symmetry). Even harmonics can't have that shape, so they cancel out.

04Phase shift: dial in some zeros

Instead of flipping both legs together, delay one leg relative to the other. Now there are moments when A and B sit on the same rail, so vt = 0. The output becomes three-level: +vd, 0, −vd, 0, … Each gate is still on for 0.5T1; each zero interval lasts αT1, with 0 ≤ α ≤ 0.5.

V̂t,k = 4vd |cos(kπα)|kπ  ⇒  V̂t,1 = 4vdπ cos(πα) α = 0: plain square wave (1.27 vd) · α = 0.5: zero output
Analogy · two dancers

Leg A and leg B are two dancers doing the same steps. vt is the difference between them. Moving exactly opposite gives the biggest difference (the square wave). Moving in perfect sync gives zero. Sliding one dancer's timing (α) lets you dial anything in between.

Bonus: delete one harmonic

Pick α so cos(kπα) = 0, i.e. kπα = 90°, so α = 1/(2k). α = 1/6 removes the 3rd; α = 0.1 removes the 5th. Try both above.

Tricky bits
  • α controls size, not timing. To move the phase, shift all four gate signals together.
  • You can null one harmonic, never all of them. The rest are still low-order (close to 60 Hz).
  • Still only two flips per switch per cycle, same as the square wave.

05Why harmonics matter: harmonic current

Picture the bridge feeding a clean grid through an inductor L. A clean grid has no harmonics (Vg,k = 0 for k > 1), so at every harmonic frequency the grid acts like a short circuit. The harmonic voltage drives only the inductor:

Ît,k = V̂t,kkω1L inductor impedance grows with k
Analogy · a heavy shopping cart

An inductor is a heavy shopping cart. Pushing it slowly back and forth is easy; shaking it rapidly barely moves it. Slow wiggles (low k) get through. Fast wiggles (high k) are crushed. For the square wave, high harmonics lose twice: their voltage falls as 1/k and the impedance grows as k, so the current falls as 1/k².

Calculator: harmonic currents for slow switching

Lesson: 15 A of useless 3rd-harmonic current is terrible. The fix is to move the harmonics to high frequency, where the inductor crushes them. That's exactly what PWM does.

Part 3 · Fast switching: sinusoidal PWM

06Bipolar PWM: the duty cycle sets the average

Switch much faster than 60 Hz (kHz) and vary how much of each short switching period Ts = 1/fsw is spent at +vd. Call that fraction d:

⟨vt⟩Ts = d(+vd) + (1 − d)(−vd) = (2d − 1)vd d = 0.5 gives 0 Choose dk = (1 + mi sin ω1tk)/2  ⇒  ⟨vt⟩ = mivd sin ω1t  ⇒  V̂t,1 = mivd

In hardware, a slow sine reference is compared with a fast triangle carrier: reference above the triangle → S1 + S4 on (+vd); below → S2 + S3 on (−vd). It's "bipolar" because vt only ever jumps between +vd and −vd.

KnobDefinitionWhat it controls
mi (modulation index)reference peak ÷ carrier peak, 0 to 1How big: V̂t,1 = mivd, and the harmonic sizes
mf (frequency ratio)fsw ÷ f1Where the harmonics sit: around mf, 2mf, 3mf, …
Analogy · newspaper photos

A printed photo is only black dots on white paper, yet from arm's length you see smooth greys: dense dots look dark, sparse dots look light. PWM does the same with voltage. Up close it's only ±vd; "from arm's length" (through the inductor) it's a smooth sine. The harmonics didn't vanish, they moved up to the switching frequency, where the inductor can't hear them.

Tricky bits
  • Voltage ceiling. In the linear range (mi ≤ 1) the fundamental can't exceed vd, which is less than the square wave's 1.27vd. That's the price of amplitude control.
  • Bigger mf = smoother tracking. More switching periods per cycle, so the staircase of averages hugs the sine more closely. Drag mf from 3 to 41 above.
  • Notation. The lecture uses mi; Table 8-1 labels the same thing ma.

07Reading Table 8-1 and computing harmonic current

The table gives ch = V̂t,h/vd, a normalized amplitude, so V̂t,h = chvd for any DC voltage. A row like "2mf ± 3" means two harmonics, h = 2mf − 3 and h = 2mf + 3, each with that amplitude. Harmonic h sits at frequency hf1.

Tricky bit · interpolating between columns

Your mi is almost never exactly 0.2, 0.4, … 1.0. Find the two columns it sits between, work out how far along you are, and go that far between the two table values:

ch = clow + (mi − mlow)/(mhigh − mlow) × (chigh − clow)

Watch the sign: some rows shrink as mi grows (like mf and 2mf ± 1), so chigh − clow is negative.

08Overmodulation (mi > 1): back toward the square wave

Make the reference taller than the triangle and, near its peaks, it stays above the carrier the whole time. No crossings means no switching: pulses merge together. The fundamental grows less than mivd, maxes out at 4vd/π, and low-order harmonics (3rd, 5th) come back.

Analogy · volume past max

Cranking a speaker past its maximum makes it a little louder, but the sound clips and distorts. Overmodulation trades waveform quality for at most 27% more fundamental.

Tricky bits
  • Table 8-1 only applies for mi ≤ 1 (the linear range). Don't use V̂t,1 = mivd above that.
  • Modern converters run mf ≫ 21, so the exact mf hardly matters. Low-mf rules are in Mohan §8-2-1.

09Unipolar PWM: double the effective frequency for free

Bipolar already pushed the harmonics up to mf. But its carrier harmonic is big: in the lecture example, 332.6 V at h = 45, giving 1.96 A. Can we push harmonics even higher without switching faster?

Yes: control each leg separately. Leg A compares +vref with the triangle; leg B compares −vref with the same triangle. The output now has three levels: +vd, 0, −vd.

Analogy · two drummers

Two drummers each hit once per bar, but offset by half a bar. The audience hears double the tempo, yet neither drummer works any harder. Each switch still flips once per carrier cycle, but the legs' edges interleave, so vt changes level twice as often: effective frequency 2fsw.

Bonus: the steps are smaller. Bipolar leaps the whole flight from +vd to −vd; unipolar walks down one stair at a time (between 0 and ±vd).

Result: the mf group cancels completely. The first surviving group sits at 2mf ± 1. Go back to the PWM demo and press Unipolar: the bars around mf vanish.

Worked example: same bridge, unipolar
  • mf = 2700/60 = 45 and mi = 0.9701 (both unchanged).
  • The mf rows cancel, so the first harmonic is h = 2mf − 1 = 89, at 5340 Hz.
  • Interpolate row 2mf ± 1: ch = 0.314 + 0.8505 × (0.181 − 0.314) = 0.2009.
  • V̂t,89 = 0.2009 × 525 = 105.5 V.
  • Ît,89 = 105.5 / [2π(89)(60)(0.010)] = 105.5/335.5 = 0.31 A, about six times less than bipolar's 1.96 A.

You're sketching a sine wave with only three crayons: +, 0 and −. The square wave uses two big strokes. Phase shift adds a gap.
