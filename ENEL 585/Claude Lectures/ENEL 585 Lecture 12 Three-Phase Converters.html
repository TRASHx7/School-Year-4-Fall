Three-Phase Converters Guide

A three-phase converter is three half-bridge legs, each driving a grid phase through its own inductor. Without a neutral wire, the three phase currents must add to zero, the grid's star point floats, and every phase equation picks up a term from the other two legs. This lecture untangles that: it splits any three-phase set into positive, negative and zero sequence parts, shows that the missing neutral removes only the zero sequence, and uses the Clarke transform to turn three coupled equations into two independent single-phase ones.

Start hereBig picture

Topic 11 controlled the current of one grid phase: a bridge makes a voltage, an inductor sits between it and the grid, and the inductor current follows the difference. This lecture asks what changes with three phases. The answer is: surprisingly little, once you look at the circuit the right way. Three legs into a three-phase grid with no neutral wire behave exactly like two independent single-phase circuits, so Topic 11's controller can be used twice.

Part 1 · Sequences

Section 1Why three phases

A single-phase inverter delivers power in pulses: the instantaneous power swings between zero and twice its average, twice every line cycle, and the dc link has to absorb that \(2\omega\) ripple (Topic 8). Three phases fix this. Each phase still pulses, but the three pulses are spaced so that they add up to a perfectly flat total. The dc link then sees steady power and needs no large ripple-buffering capacitor.

The condition is that both the phase voltages and the phase currents form a balanced set: equal peaks, one frequency, each phase \(120^\circ\) (that is, \(\frac{2\pi}{3}\) rad) from the other two.

\[ \begin{aligned} x_{A}&=\hat X\cos\omega t\\ x_{B}&=\hat X\cos\Bigl(\omega t-\frac{2\pi}{3}\Bigr)\\ x_{C}&=\hat X\cos\Bigl(\omega t+\frac{2\pi}{3}\Bigr) \end{aligned}\]
a balanced set (recalled from Topic 8)
\(x_A, x_B, x_C\) = the three phase quantities (a voltage in V or a current in A) · \(\hat X\) = their common peak, same unit · \(\omega\) = angular line frequency, in rad/s · \(t\) = time, in s
\[ p_{A}(t)=P+P\cos 2\omega t \qquad\qquad p_{t,1}(t)=p_A+p_B+p_C=3P \]
one phase pulses; three balanced phases at unity power factor give a constant total
\(p_A(t)\) = instantaneous power of phase \(A\), in W · \(P\) = average power per phase, in W · \(p_{t,1}(t)\) = total instantaneous power of the three phases (fundamental), in W
Example 1 (lecture): why the three \(2\omega\) terms cancel
Balanced phase voltages of peak \(\hat V\) and balanced phase currents of peak \(\hat I\), in phase with each other (unity power factor).

Phase \(A\) power is voltage times current. Use \(\cos^2\theta = \frac{1}{2}(1+\cos 2\theta)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} p_A &= \hat V\cos\omega t\cdot \hat I\cos\omega t\\ &= \frac{\hat V\hat I}{2}\bigl(1+\cos 2\omega t\bigr)\\ &= P + P\cos 2\omega t,\qquad P=\frac{\hat V\hat I}{2} \end{aligned}\]

Phases \(B\) and \(C\) are the same with \(\omega t\) shifted by \(\mp\frac{2\pi}{3}\), so their \(2\omega\) terms are shifted by twice that:

\[ \begin{aligned} p_B &= P + P\cos\Bigl(2\omega t-\frac{4\pi}{3}\Bigr)\\ p_C &= P + P\cos\Bigl(2\omega t+\frac{4\pi}{3}\Bigr) \end{aligned}\]

The three cosines are \(2\omega t\), \(2\omega t - 240^\circ\) and \(2\omega t + 240^\circ\): again three equal waves \(120^\circ\) apart, which sum to zero at every instant. What is left is

\[ p_{t,1}(t) = 3P \]
Total power is constant at \(3P\): no \(2\omega\) ripple on the dc link

Analogy

Three people pedal a tandem bicycle, each pushing hardest at a different third of the crank turn. Any one rider's push surges and fades, but the chain feels a smooth, steady pull. One rider alone (single phase) gives a jerky ride that a flywheel (the dc-link capacitor) has to smooth out. The analogy breaks down if one rider pushes harder than the others: real cancellation needs the three phases to be exactly balanced.

Tricky bits
  • Both sets must be balanced. Balanced voltages with unbalanced currents (or the reverse) leave some \(2\omega\) ripple. The demo below shows it.
  • Peak values carry a \(\frac{1}{2}\). \(P = \frac{\hat V\hat I}{2}\) per phase. With RMS values it is \(V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}\), no \(\frac{1}{2}\).
  • The total is \(3P\), not \(\sqrt{3}P\). \(\sqrt{3}\) appears when you write power with line-to-line voltage; the sum of three phase powers is simply three times one.
  • Each phase still pulses from \(0\) to \(2P\). Only the sum is flat.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{positive:}\quad & x_A=\hat X\cos\omega t,\ \ x_B=\hat X\cos(\omega t-120^\circ),\ \ x_C=\hat X\cos(\omega t+120^\circ)\\ \text{negative:}\quad & x_A=\hat X\cos\omega t,\ \ x_B=\hat X\cos(\omega t+120^\circ),\ \ x_C=\hat X\cos(\omega t-120^\circ)\\ \text{zero:}\quad & x_A=x_B=x_C=\hat X\cos\omega t \end{aligned}\]

Positive and negative sequence sets are both balanced: they sum to zero at every instant. A zero sequence set is not: its three phases add up to three times one phase.

Example 2 (lecture figure): reading the order of peaks

The lecture plots each set over one cycle. For the positive sequence, \(x_A\) peaks at \(\omega t = 0\), \(x_B\) at \(\omega t = 120^\circ\) and \(x_C\) at \(\omega t = 240^\circ\): order \(A, B, C\). For the negative sequence, \(x_B = \hat X\cos(\omega t + 120^\circ)\) peaks when \(\omega t = -120^\circ\), which is \(240^\circ\), and \(x_C\) peaks at \(120^\circ\): order \(A, C, B\). For the zero sequence, all three peak together at \(\omega t = 0\).

Analogy

Three dancers in a circle each raise an arm once per beat. In the positive sequence the wave goes round clockwise, in the negative sequence it goes round the other way, and in the zero sequence everyone raises their arm at the same moment. Same dancers, same beat; only the direction (or absence) of the wave changes. Real phase sets are not people, of course: a measured set is usually a mix of all three patterns at once, which is the next section.

Tricky bits
  • Look at the sign on \(x_B\). \(\cos(\omega t - 120^\circ)\) on phase \(B\) means positive sequence; \(\cos(\omega t + 120^\circ)\) on phase \(B\) means negative.
  • Negative sequence is just \(B\) and \(C\) swapped. Swapping two supply wires on a motor reverses its direction for exactly this reason.
  • "Balanced" means summing to zero. A zero sequence set has equal peaks but is not balanced.

Section 3Any three-phase set is a sum of sequences

Fortescue's theorem: any three phasors at one frequency, however unbalanced, can be written as the sum of one positive sequence set, one negative sequence set and one zero sequence set. Name each set by its phase \(A\) phasor: \(\underline{V}_{+}\), \(\underline{V}_{-}\), \(\underline{V}_{0}\). Then each phase is built from those three, rotated as its sequence requires.

\[ \begin{aligned} \underline{V}_{A}&=\underline{V}_{+}+\underline{V}_{-}+\underline{V}_{0}\\ \underline{V}_{B}&=\underline{V}_{+}\,1\angle{-120^\circ}+\underline{V}_{-}\,1\angle 120^\circ+\underline{V}_{0}\\ \underline{V}_{C}&=\underline{V}_{+}\,1\angle 120^\circ+\underline{V}_{-}\,1\angle{-120^\circ}+\underline{V}_{0} \end{aligned}\]
Fortescue: phases from sequences
\(\underline{V}_A, \underline{V}_B, \underline{V}_C\) = phase voltage phasors, in V (peak) · \(\underline{V}_{+}, \underline{V}_{-}, \underline{V}_{0}\) = phase \(A\) phasor of the positive, negative and zero sequence sets, in V · \(1\angle\theta\) = unit rotation by angle \(\theta\) (no unit)
\[ \underline{V}_{0}=\frac{1}{3}\bigl(\underline{V}_{A}+\underline{V}_{B}+\underline{V}_{C}\bigr) \]
add the three lines: the positive and negative sets each sum to zero
Example 3 (lecture): build an unbalanced set from 100 V, 40 V and 25 V
\(\underline{V}_{+}=100\ \text{V}\), \(\underline{V}_{-}=40\ \text{V}\), \(\underline{V}_{0}=25\ \text{V}\), all with their phase \(A\) peaks at \(0^\circ\).

Phase \(A\) simply adds the three:

\[ \underline{V}_{A}=100+40+25=165\angle 0^\circ\ \text{V} \]

Phase \(B\) rotates the positive set by \(-120^\circ\) and the negative set by \(+120^\circ\). In rectangular form, \(1\angle{-120^\circ} = -0.5 - j0.866\) and \(1\angle 120^\circ = -0.5 + j0.866\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \underline{V}_{B}&=100\angle{-120^\circ}+40\angle 120^\circ+25\\ &=(-50-j86.60)+(-20+j34.64)+25\\ &=-45-j51.96\ \text{V}\\ &=68.7\angle{-130.9^\circ}\ \text{V} \end{aligned}\]

Phase \(C\) swaps the two rotations, which gives the mirror image:

\[ \begin{aligned} \underline{V}_{C}&=100\angle 120^\circ+40\angle{-120^\circ}+25\\ &=-45+j51.96\ \text{V}=68.7\angle 130.9^\circ\ \text{V} \end{aligned}\]

Check by recovering \(\underline{V}_0\). The imaginary parts of \(\underline{V}_B\) and \(\underline{V}_C\) cancel, so \(\underline{V}_B+\underline{V}_C = -90\ \text{V}\):

\[ \underline{V}_{0}=\frac{1}{3}\bigl(165-90\bigr)=25\ \text{V} \]
\(\underline{V}_A=165\angle 0^\circ\ \text{V}\),
\(\underline{V}_B=68.7\angle{-130.9^\circ}\ \text{V}\),
\(\underline{V}_C=68.7\angle 130.9^\circ\ \text{V}\)

Section 4Each sequence can be solved on its own

The lecture's test circuit: three star-connected sources (star point \(n\)) feed three equal resistors \(R\) (star point \(n'\)), and a neutral wire of resistance \(R_n\) joins \(n'\) to \(n\). The circuit is linear and every phase has the same \(R\), so superposition works: split each source into its \(+\), \(-\) and \(0\) parts, solve the circuit for each part alone, and add the currents.

  • Positive sequence alone. The circuit looks the same from every phase, so the currents are a positive sequence set too. They sum to zero, so \(i_n = 0\), no voltage appears across \(R_n\), and \(n'\) sits at \(n\). Each phase is just its source across \(R\).
  • Negative sequence alone. The same with \(B\) and \(C\) swapped.
  • Zero sequence alone. All three sources are the same, so all three currents are the same \(\underline{I}_0\), and the neutral carries all three: \(\underline{I}_n = 3\underline{I}_0\).
\[ \underline{I}_{+}=\frac{\underline{V}_{+}}{R},\qquad \underline{I}_{-}=\frac{\underline{V}_{-}}{R},\qquad \underline{V}_{0}=(R+3R_{n})\,\underline{I}_{0},\qquad \underline{I}_{n}=3\underline{I}_{0} \]
three single-phase circuits, one per sequence; only the zero sequence sees \(R_n\)
\(\underline{I}_{+}, \underline{I}_{-}, \underline{I}_{0}\) = phase \(A\) current of each sequence, in A · \(R\) = resistance per phase, in Ω · \(R_n\) = neutral wire resistance, in Ω · \(\underline{I}_{n}\) = neutral wire current, in A
Example 4 (lecture): where the \(3R_n\) comes from
Zero sequence sources \(\underline{V}_0\) in all three phases, resistors \(R\), neutral resistance \(R_n\).

Go round phase \(A\): source, then the resistor \(R\), then back through the neutral wire, which carries all three equal phase currents:

\[ \begin{aligned} \underline{V}_0 &= R\,\underline{I}_0 + R_n\,\underline{I}_n\\ &= R\,\underline{I}_0 + R_n\,(3\underline{I}_0)\\ &= (R+3R_n)\,\underline{I}_0 \end{aligned}\]

With no neutral wire, \(R_n \to \infty\), so \(\underline{I}_0 = 0\). The positive and negative sequence circuits never see \(R_n\) at all.

Zero sequence equivalent: \(\underline{V}_0\) drives \(R + 3R_n\)

Analogy

Three houses share one drain pipe to the street. When the houses take turns using water (positive or negative sequence), the flows are staggered and the shared pipe barely notices. When all three flush at once (zero sequence), the shared pipe carries three times one house's flow, so its resistance counts three times over. Remove the shared pipe and nobody can flush at once at all. The picture breaks down for sinusoids: balanced currents do not take turns so much as cancel exactly, at every instant.

Tricky bits
  • It is \(R + 3R_n\), not \(R + R_n\). The neutral carries \(3\underline{I}_0\), so its drop seen per phase is \(3R_n\underline{I}_0\).
  • \(R_n\) affects only the zero sequence. Positive and negative currents sum to zero and never use the neutral wire.
  • Superposition needs a linear circuit with equal impedances in all phases. If the three resistors differed, the sequences would couple.

Section 5Zero sequence needs a return path

Remove the neutral wire. The sources are still star-connected at \(n\) and the load at \(n'\), but nothing joins the two. Now the lecture argues at each instant, not with phasors:

  1. KCL at \(n'\): \(i_A + i_B + i_C = 0\) at every instant.
  2. No zero sequence current: a zero sequence current is the same \(i_0\) in all three phases, so KCL says \(3i_0 = 0\). None flows.
  3. A zero sequence voltage can still exist. Added equally to all three sources, it just lifts \(n'\) with respect to \(n\) and drives no current.

These steps use only KCL and KVL at each instant, so they hold for any waveforms, switched PWM voltages included, not only sinusoids. At each instant, the zero sequence part of any three signals is their average:

\[ x_{0}=\frac{1}{3}\bigl(x_{A}+x_{B}+x_{C}\bigr) \]
instantaneous zero sequence component
\(x_0\) = zero sequence part at one instant, same unit as \(x_A, x_B, x_C\) (V or A)
Example 5 (lecture): what the missing neutral removes
Three sources star-connected at \(n\), three equal resistors star-connected at \(n'\), no wire from \(n\) to \(n'\).

Write KVL around each phase from \(n\) to \(n'\), with \(v_{n'n}\) the voltage of \(n'\) above \(n\):

\[ \begin{aligned} v_A &= R\,i_A + v_{n'n}\\ v_B &= R\,i_B + v_{n'n}\\ v_C &= R\,i_C + v_{n'n} \end{aligned}\]

Add the three lines and use KCL, \(i_A + i_B + i_C = 0\):

\[ v_A+v_B+v_C = 3\,v_{n'n}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_{n'n}=\frac{v_A+v_B+v_C}{3}=v_0 \]

So the load star point sits exactly at the zero sequence voltage, and each resistor sees \(v_A - v_0\): only the positive and negative parts.

No neutral: \(i_0 = 0\), and \(n'\) moves by \(v_0\) with respect to \(n\)
Filled in

The slide says a zero sequence voltage "moves \(n'\) with respect to \(n\)". The three KVL lines above are added to show it moves by exactly \(v_0\). This is the same algebra the lecture uses later for the converter (Section 8).

Analogy

Three people hold the corners of a triangular trampoline that is not tied to the ground. If all three lift their corners by the same amount, the whole trampoline rises but nothing stretches. Only uneven lifts stretch the fabric (drive current). The zero sequence is the "everyone lifts together" part, and without a tie to the ground (neutral wire) it does nothing but move the trampoline's centre.

Tricky bits
  • Zero sequence current is removed; zero sequence voltage is not. The voltage still exists; it appears between \(n\) and \(n'\) instead of across the load.
  • The argument is instantaneous. It does not need phasors, so it applies to the square-edged leg voltages of a converter.
  • \(x_0\) is an average, not a sum. Divide by 3.

Part 2 · The three-leg converter

Section 6Three legs into a grid

The converter is Topic 8's three-leg bridge with Topic 11's inductor and grid source in every phase. A dc link \(v_d\) has a midpoint \(N\) used as the voltage reference. Each leg (\(A\), \(B\), \(C\)) has a top and a bottom switch (\(S_1\)/\(S_2\) for leg \(A\), \(S_3\)/\(S_4\) for \(B\), \(S_5\)/\(S_6\) for \(C\)). Each leg midpoint drives an inductor \(L\) into one grid phase \(v_{gA}\), \(v_{gB}\), \(v_{gC}\). The grid is star-connected at \(n\), and there is no wire from \(n\) to \(N\).

After the lecture's figure. Currents \(i_{LA}, i_{LB}, i_{LC}\) flow from the legs toward the grid.

Why phasors stop here. A phasor describes only the steady state: a sinusoid of fixed amplitude and phase. To control the currents we need to know how they change in time, so from now on the circuit equations are written in time and averaged over one switching period \(T_s\), as in Topic 11.

Each leg connects its midpoint to the top or the bottom of the link, so with respect to \(N\) it can only make \(+\frac{v_d}{2}\) or \(-\frac{v_d}{2}\). Switching fast (\(f_s \gg f_1\)) and averaging over \(T_s\) gives any value in between:

\[ \langle v_{tAN}\rangle_{T_{s}}=m_{A}\,\frac{\langle v_{d}\rangle_{T_{s}}}{2},\qquad m_{A}=2d_{A}-1 \]
averaged leg voltage
\(v_{tAN}\) = voltage of leg \(A\)'s midpoint with respect to \(N\), in V · \(\langle\cdot\rangle_{T_s}\) = average over one switching period · \(v_d\) = dc link voltage, in V · \(d_A\) = fraction of \(T_s\) with \(S_1\) on (no unit, 0 to 1) · \(m_A\) = leg \(A\)'s modulation (no unit, \(-1\) with \(S_2\) always on, \(+1\) with \(S_1\) always on) · \(T_s\) = switching period, in s · \(f_s\), \(f_1\) = switching and line frequency, in Hz

Section 8KVL and the floating neutral

Go round phase \(A\): from \(N\) up to leg midpoint \(A\) (\(v_{tAN}\)), through the inductor, through the grid source \(v_{gA}\) to the grid star point \(n\), and from \(n\) back to \(N\) (\(v_{nN}\)). The same loop works for \(B\) and \(C\):

\[ \begin{aligned} L\frac{di_{LA}}{dt}&=v_{tAN}-v_{nN}-v_{gA}\\ L\frac{di_{LB}}{dt}&=v_{tBN}-v_{nN}-v_{gB}\\ L\frac{di_{LC}}{dt}&=v_{tCN}-v_{nN}-v_{gC} \end{aligned}\]
KVL, phase by phase
\(L\) = inductance per phase, in H · \(i_{LA}, i_{LB}, i_{LC}\) = inductor currents toward the grid, in A · \(v_{gA}, v_{gB}, v_{gC}\) = grid phase voltages (to \(n\)), in V · \(v_{nN}\) = voltage of grid star point \(n\) with respect to \(N\), in V

This is Topic 11's equation plus one term. We chose the leg voltages; \(v_{nN}\) we did not. To find it, add the three equations: with no wire from \(n\) and equal \(L\), the currents sum to zero, so the left side sums to zero; and a balanced grid has \(v_{gA}+v_{gB}+v_{gC}=0\).

\[ v_{nN}=\frac{v_{tAN}+v_{tBN}+v_{tCN}}{3} \]
the neutral sits at the average of the three leg voltages; it moves in steps of \(\frac{v_d}{3}\)
Example 8 (lecture): where the neutral sits

\[ \begin{aligned} L\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(i_{LA}+i_{LB}+i_{LC}\bigr) &= (v_{tAN}+v_{tBN}+v_{tCN}) - 3v_{nN} - (v_{gA}+v_{gB}+v_{gC})\\ 0 &= (v_{tAN}+v_{tBN}+v_{tCN}) - 3v_{nN} - 0\\ v_{nN} &= \frac{v_{tAN}+v_{tBN}+v_{tCN}}{3} \end{aligned}\]

Each leg is \(\pm\frac{v_d}{2}\), so the sum of the three is \(\pm\frac{3v_d}{2}\) or \(\pm\frac{v_d}{2}\), and \(v_{nN}\) can only be \(\pm\frac{v_d}{2}\) or \(\pm\frac{v_d}{6}\): steps of \(\frac{v_d}{3}\).

because balanced references sum to zero.

Section 9Three equations, two independent

Put \(v_{nN}\) back into the phase \(A\) equation. Phase \(A\)'s inductor now depends on all three leg voltages: the phases are coupled. A controller that sets \(m_A\) to fix \(i_{LA}\) would also disturb \(i_{LB}\) and \(i_{LC}\).

\[ L\frac{di_{LA}}{dt}=\frac{2}{3}v_{tAN}-\frac{1}{3}v_{tBN}-\frac{1}{3}v_{tCN}-v_{gA} \]
phase \(A\) after substituting \(v_{nN}\) (phases \(B\) and \(C\) follow the same pattern)
\[ i_{LA}+i_{LB}+i_{LC}=0 \]
only two of the three currents are independent: two of them give the third

So what we really want is two new variables, each with its own voltage and its own equation, and no \(v_{nN}\) anywhere. Part 3 builds them.

Section 10A balanced set is one rotating arrow

Three phase values at one instant are three numbers. Draw each one along its own phase axis in a plane: phase \(A\) along \(0^\circ\), phase \(B\) along \(120^\circ\), phase \(C\) along \(-120^\circ\). Scale each by \(\frac{2}{3}\) and add them head to tail. The sum is a single arrow (the red arrow in the lecture's figure).

\[ \vec x = \frac{2}{3}\Bigl(x_{A}+x_{B}\,1\angle 120^\circ+x_{C}\,1\angle{-120^\circ}\Bigr) \]
the three phases combined into one arrow (a complex number)
\(\vec x\) = the combined arrow, a complex number in the same unit as the phases (V or A) · \(1\angle 120^\circ\), \(1\angle{-120^\circ}\) = the directions of the \(B\) and \(C\) axes (no unit)

For a balanced positive sequence set, this arrow has length \(\hat X\) at every instant and turns at \(\omega\). Three oscillating numbers have become one arrow of constant length turning steadily.

\[ \vec x = \hat X\,e^{j\omega t}\qquad\text{(balanced positive sequence)} \]
length \(\hat X\), angle \(\omega t\)
Example 10 (lecture): why the arrow keeps its length
\(x_{A}=\hat X\cos\omega t\), \(x_{B}=\hat X\cos(\omega t-120^\circ)\), \(x_{C}=\hat X\cos(\omega t+120^\circ)\).

Write each cosine with Euler's formula, \(\cos\phi=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(e^{j\phi}+e^{-j\phi}\bigr)\). For phase \(B\), multiplying by its axis \(1\angle 120^\circ = e^{j120^\circ}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} x_B\,e^{j120^\circ} &= \frac{\hat X}{2}\Bigl(e^{j(\omega t-120^\circ)}+e^{-j(\omega t-120^\circ)}\Bigr)e^{j120^\circ}\\ &= \frac{\hat X}{2}\Bigl(e^{j\omega t}+e^{-j\omega t}\,e^{j240^\circ}\Bigr) \end{aligned}\]

Phase \(C\) is the same with the signs of the angles flipped. Collect the \(e^{j\omega t}\) and \(e^{-j\omega t}\) terms of all three:

\[ \begin{aligned} \vec x &= \frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{\hat X}{2}\Big[e^{j\omega t}\bigl(1+1+1\bigr)\\ &\qquad\qquad +e^{-j\omega t}\bigl(1+1\angle 240^\circ+1\angle{-240^\circ}\bigr)\Big] \end{aligned}\]

The second bracket holds three unit arrows \(120^\circ\) apart, which sum to zero. The first is 3, and \(\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot 3 = 1\):

\[ \vec x = \hat X\,e^{j\omega t} \]

Section 11The Clarke transform

Instead of a complex arrow, take its two components. The \(\alpha\) axis lies along phase \(A\); the \(\beta\) axis is \(90^\circ\) ahead. Each phase axis is \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) at \(\theta = 0^\circ, 120^\circ, -120^\circ\), and those become the columns of a matrix:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}x_{\alpha}\\x_{\beta}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{2}{3}\begin{bmatrix}1&-\frac12&-\frac12\\0&\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{A}\\x_{B}\\x_{C}\end{bmatrix} \]
the arrow's two components
\(x_\alpha\) = component along the \(\alpha\) axis (phase \(A\)'s direction) · \(x_\beta\) = component along the \(\beta\) axis, \(90^\circ\) ahead of \(\alpha\); both in the same unit as the phases (V or A)

Two rows cannot hold three numbers, so a third row, \(x_0\), keeps all the information. The matrix becomes square and invertible:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}x_{\alpha}\\x_{\beta}\\x_{0}\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\frac{2}{3}\begin{bmatrix}1&-\frac12&-\frac12\\0&\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\\frac12&\frac12&\frac12\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{T}}\begin{bmatrix}x_{A}\\x_{B}\\x_{C}\end{bmatrix} \]
the Clarke transform \(\mathbf{T}\); the third row gives \(x_0=\frac{1}{3}(x_A+x_B+x_C)\)
\(\mathbf{T}\) = Clarke matrix (no unit) · \(x_0\) = zero sequence component, same unit as the phases

It holds at each instant, for any three signals: switched leg voltages too, not only sinusoids. And each sequence lands somewhere simple:

PhasesAfter Clarke
Positive sequence\(x_\alpha = \hat X\cos\omega t\), \(x_\beta = \hat X\sin\omega t\): \(x_\beta\) lags \(x_\alpha\) by \(90^\circ\); \(x_0 = 0\)
Negative sequence\(x_\alpha = \hat X\cos\omega t\), \(x_\beta = -\hat X\sin\omega t\): \(x_\beta\) leads by \(90^\circ\); \(x_0 = 0\)
Zero sequenceonly \(x_0 = \hat X\cos\omega t\); \(x_\alpha = x_\beta = 0\)

So a missing neutral, which removes only the zero sequence, costs nothing in \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\).

Example 11 (lecture): the positive sequence after Clarke
Positive sequence set of peak \(\hat X\).

\(x_\alpha\) and \(x_\beta\) are the real and imaginary parts of the arrow \(\vec x = \hat Xe^{j\omega t}\) from Section 10:

\[ \begin{aligned} x_\alpha &= \frac{2}{3}\Bigl(x_A - \frac{1}{2}x_B - \frac{1}{2}x_C\Bigr) = \hat X\cos\omega t\\ x_\beta &= \frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\,(x_B - x_C) = \hat X\sin\omega t = \hat X\cos(\omega t - 90^\circ)\\ x_0 &= \frac{1}{3}(x_A+x_B+x_C) = 0 \end{aligned}\]
\(x_\beta\) lags \(x_\alpha\) by \(90^\circ\); \(x_0 = 0\)

Analogy

Giving a location as "3 blocks east, 2 blocks north" instead of using three streets that meet at \(120^\circ\). Tricky bits
  • The \(\frac{2}{3}\) multiplies every row, so the zero row is \(\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{3}\) of the sum: \(x_0\) is the average, not the sum.
  • \(\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\): \(x_\beta = \frac{x_B - x_C}{\sqrt3}\). Getting \(B - C\) the wrong way round flips the sign.
  • Positive: \(x_\beta\) lags. Negative: \(x_\beta\) leads. Easy to swap.
  • When \(x_A+x_B+x_C=0\), \(x_\alpha = x_A\). A handy check.

Section 12Back to phases

The controller will work in \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), but the legs need \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\). The inverse transform takes us back:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}x_{A}\\x_{B}\\x_{C}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\\-\frac12&\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&1\\-\frac12&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{\alpha}\\x_{\beta}\\x_{0}\end{bmatrix} \]
inverse Clarke, \(\mathbf{T}^{-1}\)
Same symbols as Section 11. Each row is that phase's axis \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\), then a 1.

Read it back: each phase is the arrow's component along that phase's axis, plus \(x_0\). There is no \(\frac{2}{3}\) here; it lives only in the forward transform.

Example 12 (lecture): check the inverse on phase A

Row \(A\) says \(x_A = x_\alpha + x_0\). Substitute the forward transform:

\[ \begin{aligned} x_\alpha + x_0 &= \frac{2}{3}\Bigl(x_A - \frac{1}{2}x_B - \frac{1}{2}x_C\Bigr) + \frac{1}{3}\bigl(x_A + x_B + x_C\bigr)\\ &= \Bigl(\frac{2}{3}+\frac{1}{3}\Bigr)x_A + \Bigl(-\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{3}\Bigr)x_B + \Bigl(-\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{3}\Bigr)x_C\\ &= x_A \end{aligned}\]
The inverse returns \(x_A\) exactly; rows \(B\) and \(C\) check the same way

Section 13Clarke applied to the KVL

Now use the transform on the converter. Start from the three KVL equations of Section 8 in vector form, under the lecture's three conditions: equal \(L\) in the three phases, no neutral wire, balanced grid.

\[ \begin{aligned} L\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}i_{LA}\\i_{LB}\\i_{LC}\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}v_{tAN}\\v_{tBN}\\v_{tCN}\end{bmatrix}-v_{nN}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}v_{gA}\\v_{gB}\\v_{gC}\end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}\]

Multiply both sides by \(\mathbf{T}\). Since \(\mathbf{T}\) is constant and \(L\) is the same in every phase, \(L\frac{d}{dt}\) comes outside, and every vector simply becomes its \(\alpha\beta 0\) version. The neutral term needs \(\mathbf{T}\) times \((1,1,1)\), which is \((0,0,1)\): it vanishes from the \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) rows.

\[ L\frac{di_{L\alpha}}{dt}=v_{t\alpha}-v_{g\alpha},\qquad L\frac{di_{L\beta}}{dt}=v_{t\beta}-v_{g\beta} \]
two independent single-phase circuits, with no \(v_{nN}\)
\(i_{L\alpha}, i_{L\beta}\) = Clarke components of the inductor currents, in A · \(v_{t\alpha}, v_{t\beta}\) = Clarke components of the leg voltages \(v_{tAN}, v_{tBN}, v_{tCN}\), in V · \(v_{g\alpha}, v_{g\beta}\) = Clarke components of the grid voltages, in V
\[ 0 = v_{t0} - v_{nN}\qquad\bigl(i_{L0}=0,\ v_{g0}=0\bigr) \]
the zero row just gives the neutral result again
\(i_{L0}\) = zero sequence inductor current, in A (zero with no neutral wire) · \(v_{g0}\) = zero sequence grid voltage, in V (zero for a balanced grid) · \(v_{t0}\) = average of the three leg voltages, in V
Example 13 (lecture): one change per step

Step 1. Multiply by \(\mathbf{T}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} L\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}i_{L\alpha}\\i_{L\beta}\\i_{L0}\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}v_{t\alpha}\\v_{t\beta}\\v_{t0}\end{bmatrix}-v_{nN}\,\mathbf{T}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}v_{g\alpha}\\v_{g\beta}\\v_{g0}\end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}\]

Step 2. Evaluate \(\mathbf{T}(1,1,1)\) row by row:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{row } \alpha:&\quad \frac{2}{3}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr) = 0\\ \text{row } \beta:&\quad \frac{2}{3}\Bigl(0+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\Bigr) = 0\\ \text{row } 0:&\quad \frac{2}{3}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Bigr) = 1 \end{aligned}\]

Step 3. So \(v_{nN}\) drops out of the \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) rows, which leaves the two independent equations above. The zero row, with \(i_{L0}=0\) and \(v_{g0}=0\), gives \(0 = v_{t0} - v_{nN}\), so \(v_{nN} = v_{t0} = \frac{1}{3}(v_{tAN}+v_{tBN}+v_{tCN})\): the neutral result of Section 8 again.

\(L\frac{di_{L\alpha}}{dt}=v_{t\alpha}-v_{g\alpha}\) and \(L\frac{di_{L\beta}}{dt}=v_{t\beta}-v_{g\beta}\): two copies of Topic 11's single-phase equation
Filled in

Analogy

Back to the seesaw from Section 9: describing it by three heights tangled everything together. Describe it instead by its two tilt angles (\(\alpha\), \(\beta\)) and its overall height (\(0\)). The two tilts move independently, each with its own push, and the overall height is fixed by the pivot (no neutral wire). Two separate steering problems replace one tangled one.

Tricky bits
  • "\(L\frac{d}{dt}\) comes outside" needs equal \(L\) in all three phases. Unequal inductors would mix \(\alpha\), \(\beta\) and \(0\).
  • \(v_{nN}\) does not vanish; it moves. It is all in the zero row, where it equals \(v_{t0}\).
  • \(v_{t\alpha}\) uses the leg voltages to \(N\). Using them is fine precisely because the \(N\) to \(n\) offset is zero sequence.
  • With no neutral, \(i_{L\alpha} = i_{LA}\) (the currents sum to zero), so the \(\alpha\) equation is phase \(A\) without the coupling.
